# Korean Broadcast Television Industry

The Korean broadcast television system represents one of the country's major media pillars and historical shapers of mass culture, distinct from both the film industry and the newer streaming platforms. The three major national broadcasters—**KBS** (Korean Broadcasting System), **MBC** (Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation), and **SBS** (Seoul Broadcasting System)—have dominated terrestrial television since the 1950s, shaping the nation's media landscape, regulatory environment, and cultural identity.

## Historical Development

### Early Era and State Control (1956–1987)

Korean television broadcasting began with **HLKZ-TV** on May 12, 1956, as the country's first television station, initially under KORCAD (Korean RCA Distributor). After a studio fire in 1959 and subsequent reorganizations, the channel was absorbed into state broadcasting infrastructure and eventually restructured as **KBS** in 1973 under the Park Chung-hee government's Broadcasting Act amendments. MBC (then called **Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation**) launched on December 2, 1961, with mixed ownership that included regional affiliates and government ties. SBS arrived much later—incorporated in 1990, it launched radio in March 1991 and began television broadcasting in **December 1991**, marking the formal end of the KBS–MBC duopoly.

During the authoritarian decades, broadcasters functioned as state propaganda instruments, heavily censoring content and serving regime messaging alongside entertainment programming. Park Chung-hee's 1972 Yushin Constitution further tightened media controls; MBC's regional affiliates were absorbed into a centralized network under government pressure. The live-broadcast infrastructure created by these networks became critical during national events, from the 1988 Seoul Olympics to election coverage. **KBS 9 O'Clock News**, which launched in 1973, became the most-watched news broadcast in the country and a key vehicle for government messaging.

### Democratization and the Three-Network Era (1987–2000s)

The 1987 June Democratic Uprising and subsequent **Broadcasting Act amendment of 1990** loosened state control, allowing more editorial independence and commercial competition. SBS's launch broke the duopoly and introduced a three-way competitive structure. Terrestrial advertising became the primary revenue model, with programs competing fiercely for viewership shares. This era saw the rise of weekly variety shows, investigative news programs, and the earliest serialized dramas that would evolve into the globally recognized K-drama template.

The period also saw intensely competitive primetime drama slots emerge. Early anchor dramas like MBC's *Jealousy* (1992) and KBS's *Asphalt Man* demonstrated the market power of the evening slot. By the late 1990s, KBS and MBC were producing dramas that averaged 30–50% household viewership, numbers rarely matched by any programming genre since.

### Cable, Digital, and Streaming Transition (2000s–Present)

Cable and satellite television gained regulatory footing from the mid-1990s onward. The launch of key cable channels—**tvN** (a CJ ENM channel) in **2006** and **JTBC** in **December 2011**—introduced serious non-terrestrial competition for prestige drama content. The **2009 Media Act** further liberalized broadcasting ownership, allowing print media companies (including Chosun Ilbo, JoongAng Ilbo, Dong-A Ilbo) to hold broadcast licenses, which led to the establishment of a new crop of comprehensive programming channels.

Korea completed its **transition from analog to digital terrestrial broadcasting on December 31, 2012**, earlier than many comparable countries. The digital transition expanded channel capacity, enabled HD programming, and set the stage for OTT competition.

Simultaneously, **Netflix's Korea launch in January 2016** began pulling premium drama investment away from terrestrial exclusivity. Netflix's 2019 commission of *Kingdom* (a historical zombie thriller) marked a watershed moment: the platform began funding original Korean content at budgets and production values matching or exceeding terrestrial networks. The runaway success of **Squid Game** (September 2021), which became Netflix's most-watched non-English series in history with 1.65 billion viewing hours in its first 28 days, validated the global appetite for Korean content and intensified the streaming wars domestically.

## Ownership and Governance

### KBS (Korean Broadcasting System)

KBS is the only fully state-owned broadcaster, funded by a combination of the **Television and Radio Receiving Tax** (currently **2,500 won per month**, collected by KEPCO alongside electricity bills) and advertising revenue. It operates two primary terrestrial services:

- **KBS 1**: Free terrestrial programming with a public-service orientation—news, documentaries, educational content, and prestige historical dramas.
- **KBS 2**: Commercial terrestrial programming with entertainment-heavy scheduling—primetime dramas, variety shows, and music programs.

KBS also operates KBS World, an international satellite and cable channel broadcasting Korean programming to diaspora and international audiences in over 100 countries. This hybrid model creates tension between public-service obligations and commercial competitive pressures, particularly during politically sensitive periods when government editorial control is suspected. The chairman of KBS is appointed through a governmental process, making the position a recurring flashpoint during transitions of political power.

### MBC (Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation)

Originally structured with complex ownership involving regional affiliates, MBC is now a company whose controlling shares are held by the **Foundation for Broadcast Culture** (방송문화진흥회, Bangmunhoe), a public foundation, with a minority stake held by the Jeongsu Scholarship Foundation—an arrangement legally shielded from direct government ownership but historically influenced by executive appointments to the foundation's board. MBC's flagship terrestrial channel targets the broadest family audience. Its cable arm, **MBC Drama** and **MBC Every1**, carry specialized and repeat programming. MBC operates 17 regional affiliate stations nationwide, making it effectively the most geographically distributed network.

MBC has been the site of major labor conflicts: in **2012**, MBC workers conducted a **170-day strike**, one of Korea's longest media labor actions, over accusations of politically motivated editorial manipulation by the then-management appointed under the Lee Myung-bak administration.

### SBS (Seoul Broadcasting System)

SBS operates as a more commercial, advertiser-dependent entity and is the flagship of the **SBS Media Holdings** group, which has interests in cable (SBS Plus, SBS FunE, SBS Golf, SBS CNBC, SBS Sports) and digital media. SBS's regional footprint differs from KBS and MBC: it is a Seoul-based broadcaster that maintains partnerships rather than full ownership of regional affiliates. Its commercial DNA makes it more responsive to audience trends but also more vulnerable to advertiser pressure and ratings swings.

## Programming and Production Model

### Terrestrial Drama as the Flagship Format

The most prestigious programming category on terrestrial networks remains the **evening drama slot** (typically 21:00–22:30), where networks invest tens of billions of won per series in high-profile productions. Korean dramas have traditionally followed a distinctive production model:

- **Single head-writer model**: One lead writer (sometimes called a "queen writer" for prominent figures like Kim Eun-sook or Noh Hee-kyung) drives narrative continuity across 16–20 episodes, unlike American writers-room approaches. This creates a distinctive authorial voice but also risks narrative collapse if the writer underperforms under deadline pressure.
- **Near-live shooting (생방 촬영, saengbang chwalyeong)**: Historically, much Korean drama was filmed just days before broadcast, creating intense production pressure but also allowing script adjustments based on real-time audience feedback and ratings. A mid-series storyline might be extended or a secondary character's arc expanded if audience response indicated popularity.
- **Star system**: A-list actors command premium salaries—by the 2010s, top stars commanded 30–50 million won per episode—and guarantee minimum viewership. The casting announcement of a A-list actor for a drama series is itself news that drives pre-premiere buzz.
- **Episode count standardization**: The 16-episode "miniseries" (미니시리즈) became the dominant prime drama format from the 1990s onward; longer 50–100 episode "daily dramas" air in morning/afternoon slots targeting homemakers.

#### Landmark Dramas and Ratings Records

Korean terrestrial dramas have produced some of the most-watched television in any language:

| Drama | Network | Year | Peak Rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Dae Jang Geum* (Jewel in the Palace) | MBC | 2003–2004 | 57.8% (national household) |
| *What Is Love?* | MBC | 1991–1992 | 64.9% |
| *Winter Sonata* | KBS 2 | 2002 | 22–23% (drove Korean Wave in Japan) |
| *Descendants of the Sun* | KBS 2 | 2016 | 38.8% |
| *Reply 1988* | tvN | 2015–2016 | 19.6% (cable record) |
| *Crash Landing on You* | tvN | 2019–2020 | 21.7% (cable record) |

*Dae Jang Geum* (2003–2004) became Korea's most internationally exported drama up to that point, airing in over 90 countries and triggering the "Korean Wave" (hallyu) in Asia. *Winter Sonata*, starring Bae Yong-joon and Choi Ji-woo, provoked a mania in Japan—the "Yon-sama" fandom among Japanese women—that generated enormous tourism and merchandising revenue.

### News and Current Affairs

The main evening news broadcasts—**KBS 9 O'Clock News**, **MBC News Desk**, and **SBS 8 News**—compete fiercely for viewership and editorial prestige. Launched in 1973, KBS 9 O'Clock News has been the most-watched single news broadcast in Korea for most of its history. News serves as a legitimacy anchor for public broadcasters and a major advertising draw. Coverage of political developments, economic crises, and social issues directly shapes national discourse. During major events (the Sewol ferry disaster in April 2014, the MERS outbreak in 2015, and the COVID-19 pandemic beginning in 2020), the three networks provided extended 24-hour coverage that still commands a centrality no single streaming platform has replicated.

### Entertainment, Variety, and K-Pop Music Shows

Weekly entertainment and variety shows are cultural anchors. **Infinite Challenge** (MBC, 2005–2018) became one of the longest-running and most influential variety shows, launching comedian Yoo Jae-suk to national icon status. **Running Man** (SBS, launched 2010) became one of Korea's most-exported variety formats, spawning local versions in China, Vietnam, and Thailand.

Music broadcast programs are globally significant platforms for K-pop:

- **Inkigayo** (SBS) — SBS's flagship Sunday music program (under its current name since 1998); a weekly chart-based competition where K-pop artists compete for the number one position (a "triple crown" is three consecutive first-place finishes).
- **Music Bank** (KBS 2) — Weekly music program featuring live performances, chart rankings, and award segments.
- **Show! Music Core** (MBC) — Similar live-music competition format.

These Friday-to-Saturday shows serve as essential promotional platforms; a K-pop group's promotional cycle is typically timed around live performance appearances across all three networks. Performance footage from these shows generates hundreds of millions of YouTube views. **Inkigayo** alone had over 2.7 million subscribers on YouTube as of 2024.

## Soft Power and Cultural Impact

The dominance of terrestrial broadcasters in Korean media created the "broadcast TV effect" on culture:

- **Hallyu acceleration**: The earliest hits driving the Korean Wave—*Dae Jang Geum* (MBC, 2003), *Winter Sonata* (KBS, 2002), *Descendants of the Sun* (KBS, 2016)—aired on terrestrial networks, establishing the K-drama brand globally before Netflix amplified it further.
- **Language standardization**: Broadcast Korean became the de facto standard accent and vocabulary. The Seoul-dialect standard promoted by KBS (known as "KBS Korean") has influenced education, public speaking, and the speech patterns of younger Koreans nationwide.
- **Event broadcasting**: Major broadcasts of national events (presidential inaugurations, World Cup matches, Olympics) create shared viewing moments central to national identity. The 2002 FIFA World Cup, during which Korea reached the semi-finals, produced unprecedented street gatherings of fans watching terrestrial broadcasts on massive outdoor screens.
- **Cultural export infrastructure**: The production ecosystem built around terrestrial drama—writers, directors, cinematographers, production designers—became the talent base for Netflix originals, theatrical films (including Oscar-winner *Parasite*), and global co-productions.

## Regulatory Framework

### Korea Communications Commission (KCC) and KCSC

Broadcast regulation is divided between the **Korea Communications Commission** (방송통신위원회, KCC), which oversees ownership, licensing, and structural regulation, and the **Korea Communications Standards Commission** (방송통신심의위원회, KCSC), which adjudicates content standards including decency, political balance, advertising rules, and accuracy. The KCSC issues sanctions (formal cautions, warning orders, broadcast suspension) for violations.

Debates over product placement (**PPL**, 간접광고), celebrity endorsements, and the blurring of entertainment and advertising have led to periodic regulatory updates. The Broadcasting Act permits PPL in entertainment and drama programming but prohibits it in news and documentary content. A 2010 revision of the Broadcasting Act explicitly legalized indirect advertising after years of informal practice, creating a regulated framework.

### Political Control and Editorial Independence

Broadcast networks have been persistent flashpoints in South Korea's political struggles. The appointment of KBS presidents and key editors has become politicized; each change in government tends to accompany pressure for editorial shifts. Notable events:

- **2008–2009**: KBS management under the Lee Myung-bak government was accused of directing news coverage to favor the administration; union members organized the 2008 "KBS bias" protests.
- **2012**: MBC workers' 170-day strike against management accused of political manipulation; the strike ended without full victory but raised public awareness.
- **2016–2017**: The Park Geun-hye corruption crisis and subsequent impeachment led networks to cover the story with varying degrees of alacrity; JTBC's "tablet PC" scoop revealing Park's adviser Choi Soon-sil's role became a model of cable-channel investigative journalism rivaling terrestrial news.

## Economic Significance

Broadcast television generates substantial revenue within a concentrated industry:

- **Advertising**: Primetime commercial slots during evening dramas command premium rates. In the 2010s, a 30-second spot during a top-rated drama on KBS 2 or MBC could cost 20–40 million won. Combined terrestrial broadcast advertising revenue was approximately **3–4 trillion won annually** in the late 2010s, declining from the mid-2010s peak as digital media captured share.
- **Drama export revenue**: The overseas sale of drama broadcast rights and streaming licenses grew from a minor line item to a major revenue stream. Korean drama exports reached approximately $700 million in 2021 and over $1 billion annually by 2022–2023, driven by Netflix and other global platforms' hunger for Korean content.
- **Ancillary revenues**: Product placement fees (often 500 million to 2 billion won per drama series), merchandise licensing, format sales, and celebrity-driven promotions supplement core broadcast revenue.
- **Employment**: Writers, directors, actors, technical crews, post-production specialists, and production companies depend on terrestrial network funding. The "drama production company" (드라마 제작사) ecosystem includes hundreds of specialized firms.

The industry is intertwined with the broader entertainment complex. Production companies bidding for drama slots often also produce for cinema and streaming. Major production houses like Studio Dragon (a tvN/CJ ENM subsidiary) and JTBC Studios produce for both cable/streaming and terrestrial slots. Actors and directors move fluidly between terrestrial drama, film, and OTT platforms.

## Contemporary State (2020s)

Terrestrial broadcasters remain cultural institutions but in managed decline relative to their peaks:

- Household viewership shares for top-rated evening dramas have stabilized around 15–25% (down from 50–60% peaks in the 1990s–2000s), reflecting fragmentation across cable, streaming, and social media.
- Network news maintains prestige and reach among older demographics (50+) but struggles with audiences under 35, who increasingly source news from YouTube, Naver, and social media.
- **Streaming deals provide new revenue**: International licensing of dramas to Netflix, Disney+, Apple TV+, and other platforms has become a major growth area. Netflix paid approximately 2 billion won per episode for some premium Korean dramas by the early 2020s, figures comparable to major American streaming productions.
- The three terrestrial networks jointly own **wavve** (a joint venture of KBS, MBC, and SBS with SK Telecom, formed in 2019 from the merger of the broadcasters' POOQ service and SKT's oksusu) to compete in the domestic streaming market against Netflix and domestic rival **Tving** (controlled by CJ ENM, the same company behind tvN).

The "Korean broadcast television system"—the three-network structure, evening-drama prestige, and state/commercial hybrid model—remains internationally distinctive, studied by media scholars as a unique model distinct from American network TV or European public broadcasting. Networks continue to invest in flagship dramas, though competition with cable networks (tvN's *Crash Landing on You*, JTBC's *My Mister*) and streaming hits (*Squid Game*, *Extraordinary Attorney Woo*) has pushed excellence to multiple platforms rather than concentrating it on terrestrial slots alone.
