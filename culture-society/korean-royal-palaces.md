# Korean Royal Palaces and Heritage Sites

**Category**: Culture & Society
**Key figures**: King Taejo, King Taejong, King Sejong, King Jeongjo, King Gojong

## Overview

Korea's royal palaces are the primary architectural legacy of the [Joseon dynasty](../history/joseon-dynasty.md) (1392–1910), representing more than five centuries of courtly power, Confucian statecraft, and aesthetic refinement. The five principal palaces in Seoul — Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung, and Gyeonghuigung — were each built for distinct political and ceremonial purposes, and together define the historic urban core of the Korean capital. A sixth major complex, Hwaseong Fortress in Suwon, extended royal authority beyond the capital. Collectively, these sites embody the political geography, ritual life, and architectural philosophy of Korea's last and longest-ruling dynasty.

## The Five Grand Palaces of Seoul

### Gyeongbokgung Palace (경복궁)

Gyeongbokgung, meaning "Palace Greatly Blessed by Heaven," was founded in 1395 by King Taejo, the founder of the Joseon dynasty, as the primary royal seat of government. Located at the northern end of Seoul's central axis, it was designed according to geomantic principles (pungsu-jiri), positioned with Bugaksan mountain to its rear and the Cheonggyecheon stream to its south. At its peak under King Sejong (r. 1418–1450), the palace encompassed approximately 330 buildings across a 43-hectare site.

The palace was almost entirely destroyed during the Japanese invasions (Imjin War, 1592–1598), when invading forces burned it to the ground; it remained in ruins for nearly 270 years. Regent Heungseon Daewongun undertook a massive reconstruction between 1865 and 1868, restoring approximately 330 structures at a cost that strained the royal treasury. The Gwanghwamun Gate — the palace's southern main gate and one of the most iconic structures in Seoul — was moved by Japanese colonial authorities in 1926 and restored to its original position in 2010.

The National Folk Museum of Korea and the National Palace Museum of Korea are located within the palace grounds. The daily changing-of-the-guard ceremony at Gwanghwamun Gate, performed in traditional dress, attracts hundreds of thousands of visitors annually.

### Changdeokgung Palace (창덕궁)

Built in 1405 by King Taejong as a secondary palace, Changdeokgung became the primary royal residence after Gyeongbokgung's destruction in the Imjin War and remained the main seat of Joseon government from 1609 to 1867 — a period of 258 years. The palace is considered the finest surviving example of Korean palace architecture, praised for its harmonious adaptation to the natural terrain of its site rather than imposing rigid symmetry.

The palace's Huwon (후원), known in English as the Secret Garden or Rear Garden, covers approximately 78 acres (32 hectares) and contains 44 pavilions, ponds, and garden terraces developed incrementally over three centuries. The Buyongji Pond and Buyongjeong Pavilion, developed during the reign of King Jeongjo (r. 1776–1800), represent the pinnacle of Korean landscape architecture, with their geometrically-configured ponds, lotus plantings, and surrounding forested hillsides.

In 1997, Changdeokgung Palace Complex, including the Secret Garden, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. UNESCO recognized it for its outstanding universal value as an example of Far Eastern palace architecture and garden design that harmoniously blends human constructions with the natural landscape. It was the first of Korea's palaces to receive this designation.

### Changgyeonggung Palace (창경궁)

Originally constructed in 1483 by King Seongjong as a residence for his father's widowed consorts, Changgyeonggung is connected to Changdeokgung through shared gardens and walls, forming a single extended palace complex. Beginning in 1909, during the late Korean Empire under Japanese pressure, the authorities converted the palace grounds into a public zoo and botanical garden and renamed the site Changgyeongwon — a deliberate act of cultural desecration that, extended through the colonial period (1910–1945), stripped it of its royal character. The zoo was removed after independence, and substantial restoration work was completed in the 1980s and 1990s.

### Deoksugung Palace (덕수궁)

Deoksugung evolved from a private aristocratic residence into an emergency royal retreat following the Imjin War, temporarily housing King Seonjo after Gyeongbokgung's destruction. It assumed greatest historical significance during the Korean Empire period (1897–1910), when Emperor Gojong established it as his primary residence after returning from political asylum at the Russian legation in 1897. Gojong commissioned several Western-style stone buildings within the palace compound — including Jeonggwanheon Hall (1900) and Seokjojeon Hall (1910, completed the year of annexation) — creating a unique architectural coexistence of traditional Korean wooden structures and European neoclassical architecture.

The Deoksugung Stonewall Road (Doldam-gil), which runs alongside the palace wall, became one of Seoul's most beloved urban promenades. The palace is closely associated with the final years of the Korean Empire and the diplomatic coercion that culminated in the 1910 annexation, and with the independence movement that emerged in the colonial period. See also: [Korean Independence Movement](../history/korean-independence-movement.md).

### Gyeonghuigung Palace (경희궁)

Built between 1616 and 1623 during the reign of King Gwanghaegun, Gyeonghuigung served as the West Palace (Seogwol), a secondary residence used when the main palaces were occupied or inauspicious. At its peak it contained over 100 buildings. Japanese colonial authorities systematically demolished it between 1915 and 1926 to build Keijō Middle School (now Seoul High School); subsequent construction of the Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education further reduced what remained. Partial restoration began in 1985 and continues, though only about 10% of the original complex has been reconstructed.

## Hwaseong Fortress (화성)

Built between 1794 and 1796 in Suwon under the direction of King Jeongjo, Hwaseong Fortress represents a distinct category: a fortified palace city rather than a standalone palace. King Jeongjo constructed it partly to relocate the tomb of his father, Crown Prince Sado, and partly as a planned center of royal power to rival Seoul. The fortress was designed by scholar Jeong Yakyong (Dasan) using advanced military engineering principles, incorporating Chinese and Japanese fortification innovations. Its 5.7-kilometer circuit wall integrates 48 defensive structures including watchtowers, gun emplacements, and gates. Hwaseong Fortress was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997, the same year as Changdeokgung, recognized for its innovative synthesis of military architecture and urban planning. See also: [Suwon](../geography/suwon.md).

## Architectural Principles and Design

### Geomancy and Site Selection

Joseon palace siting followed pungsu-jiri (Korean geomancy), demanding a mountain to the north, a river to the south, and hills framing the east and west — a configuration known as baesanimsu (backed by a mountain, facing water). Gyeongbokgung's position, with Bugaksan behind it and Hangang River accessible to the south, exemplifies this principle. The cardinal orientation of major halls reflected cosmological significance, with the main throne hall (geunjeongjeon at Gyeongbokgung) facing south along a central axis.

### Confucian Spatial Hierarchy

Palace layouts encoded Confucian social order in physical space. The outer court (oejeon) housed administrative and ceremonial functions; the inner court (naejeon) served as the private royal household. Throne halls were elevated on multi-tiered stone platforms, their scale expressing royal authority. The royal apartments, gardens, and ancestral shrines were progressively more secluded, reflecting the concentric privacy of Confucian family organization.

### Construction Materials and Techniques

Korean palace architecture relied predominantly on local timber — principally pine — combined with fired clay roof tiles (giwa) in characteristic grey-green hues. Dancheong (단청), the elaborate multicolored painting applied to wooden structural elements, served both decorative and wood-preservation functions, using mineral pigments in five cardinal colors (blue, red, yellow, white, black) derived from Taoist cosmology. Palace halls were constructed without nails, using interlocking bracket systems (gongpo) to distribute roof loads. This [traditional visual arts](traditional-visual-arts.md) tradition continues in palace restoration work.

## Japanese Colonial Period and Damage

The Japanese Government-General of Korea (1910–1945) deliberately altered the palaces to diminish their symbolic power. The Government-General Building, an enormous Western-style administrative structure, was constructed directly in front of Gyeongbokgung's Gwanghwamun Gate (1926), blocking the palace's central axis. Changdeokgung was partially opened as a public exhibition space. Colonial road-widening projects cut through palace perimeters. The removal of the Government-General Building was one of Korea's most significant postcolonial restoration decisions, completed in 1995 to mark the 50th anniversary of liberation. See also: [Japanese Colonial Period](../history/japanese-colonial-period.md).

## Postwar Restoration and Preservation

The [Korean War](../history/korean-war.md) (1950–1953) caused additional damage to palace structures already reduced by colonial demolition. Systematic postwar restoration began in earnest in the 1960s and accelerated through the 1980s and 1990s as South Korea's economic development generated resources for heritage investment. The Cultural Heritage Administration (now National Heritage Service) has overseen ongoing restoration, using traditional materials and techniques documented in surviving historical drawings and records from the Joseon Veritable Records (Josillok).

By 2010, Gyeongbokgung had been approximately 40% restored relative to its 19th-century extent, with continued work ongoing. The restoration of Gwanghwamun Gate to its pre-colonial position, completed in 2010 after a seven-year project, was one of the most visible milestones. A master plan targets eventual restoration of approximately 89 major structures within Gyeongbokgung.

## Tourism and Living Heritage

The palaces collectively attract over 10 million visitors annually. Gyeongbokgung alone received approximately 4.5 million visitors in recent pre-pandemic years, making it South Korea's most visited cultural heritage site. Visitors may rent hanbok (traditional Korean dress) from vendors near palace gates, receiving complimentary admission when wearing it — a policy that dramatically increased cultural engagement, particularly among younger generations and international tourists.

The palaces host seasonal programs including royal guard-changing ceremonies, traditional music performances, night tours (Gyeongbokgung's night opens, limited in number, are typically sold out within minutes), and cultural education programs for schoolchildren. The institutions operating within palace grounds — the National Palace Museum, National Folk Museum, and National Museum of Korean Contemporary History — add curatorial depth to palace visits.

The palaces feature prominently in Korean historical drama productions (sageuk), with Changdeokgung and Gyeongbokgung serving as filming locations for numerous major productions. This connection between physical heritage and the [Korean drama industry](korean-drama-industry.md) has extended global awareness of the palaces and contributed to heritage tourism among international visitors drawn to Korea through the [Hallyu wave](hallyu.md).
