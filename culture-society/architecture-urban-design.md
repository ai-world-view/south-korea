# Architecture and Urban Design in South Korea

**Category**: Culture & Society
**Key figures**: Seung H-Sang, Kim Swoo-geun, Zaha Hadid, Rem Koolhaas, Dominique Perrault, Mario Botta, Jean Nouvel

## Summary

Contemporary South Korean architecture represents a synthesis of modernist internationalism, traditional Korean aesthetics, and rapid technological innovation shaped by the country's compressed industrialization. From the 1960s onward, as South Korea pursued rapid economic development under the developmental state, architecture became a tool for nation-building and economic competitiveness. Early modernist works emphasized functional efficiency (apartment complexes, government buildings, industrial facilities), while later decades saw the emergence of more distinctive Korean architectural voices engaging with traditional forms and materials. Seoul's skyline transformation — from a low-rise traditional city to a vertical metropolis — became a global emblem of South Korea's economic ascent.

## Traditional Korean Architecture

Korean architectural tradition predating Japanese colonization (1910–1945) rested on several distinctive elements: the *hanok* (traditional wooden house), palace complexes (*gung*), Buddhist temple ensembles (*saji*), and Confucian ceremonial structures. Hanok construction relied on post-and-beam timber framing with clay tile or thatched roofs, raised floors for ondol (underfloor heating), and courtyard-centered spatial organization that mediated indoor-outdoor relationships. Major surviving palace complexes in Seoul include Gyeongbokgung (경복궁, originally built 1395, destroyed during Japanese invasion in the 1590s, partially restored in the late Joseon period, and undergoing ongoing restoration from the 1990s to present) and Changdeokgung (창덕궁), which along with its rear garden Biwon was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997.

Buddhist temple architecture achieved distinctive regional forms including the three-gate layout (*ilju-mun*, *cheonwang-mun*, *buru*) and pagoda designs derived from Chinese models but refined over centuries. The Bulguksa temple in Gyeongju, built during the Unified Silla period (751 CE) and designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995, exemplifies the pinnacle of Silla Buddhist architecture.

## Colonial and Early Modern Period (1910–1960)

Japanese colonization fundamentally disrupted Korean architectural continuity. Colonial authorities introduced Western neoclassical and eclectic architectural styles for government buildings — the former Government-General building (completed 1926, demolished in 1995 on the 50th anniversary of liberation) stood directly in front of Gyeongbokgung palace as a deliberate assertion of colonial authority. Colonial urban planning transformed Seoul (then Gyeongseong) with grid street plans, modern drainage, and new commercial districts.

After liberation in 1945 and the Korean War (1950–1953), much of the built environment lay devastated. Reconstruction from the early 1950s through the 1960s emphasized utilitarian construction with limited design ambition, reflecting severe material scarcity and priority given to basic shelter and infrastructure.

## Developmental State Architecture (1960s–1980s)

Under Park Chung-hee's government (1961–1979), architecture was subordinated to developmental goals. The Han River Development Project (1967–1982) transformed floodplain areas south of the river into Gangnam, one of the most significant urban development projects in Korean history. Streets on a grand scale, high-rise apartment blocks, and civic infrastructure defined this era.

Kim Swoo-geun (김수근, 1931–1986) was the preeminent architect of this period, often called the "father of Korean modern architecture." His notable works include the Seoul Olympic Main Stadium (1988, though he died before the Games), the Freedom Center (1963), the Korean Broadcasting System headquarters (1976), and the Spaces building (1977). Kim engaged with Brutalist and metabolist influences while exploring Korean spatial sensibilities, particularly the *madang* (courtyard) as an organizing principle.

The **aparteu** (아파트, apartment complex) emerged during this period as South Korea's dominant housing form. Massive standardized complexes — such as the Mapo apartment complex (1960s) and later the enormous Jamsil development (1970s) — housed hundreds of thousands of Koreans. By the 2020 census, approximately 77% of Korean households lived in apartments or multi-family buildings, one of the highest rates of apartment living in the world. The standardized 25-pyeong (approximately 82 m²) apartment layout became a template replicated across the country.

The Five New Towns project (1989–1990s) — including Bundang, Ilsan, Pyeongchon, Sanbon, and Jungdong — aimed to relieve Seoul's housing pressure. Bundang (in Seongnam) was the flagship development, covering 19.6 km² and housing approximately 390,000 people when completed.

## Democratic Opening and the Architectural Renaissance (1990s–2000s)

Democratization after 1987 opened space for more diverse architectural expression. International architects were invited to South Korea for prestige projects, and Korean architects began winning international recognition. The 1988 Seoul Olympics (despite being formally under Chun Doo-hwan's legacy) produced significant infrastructure and opened Korea to global design culture.

The **Leeum Samsung Museum of Art** (리움미술관), opened in 2004 in Itaewon, Seoul, exemplifies the new international ambition. Three galleries were designed separately by Mario Botta (Museum 1, housing traditional Korean art), Jean Nouvel (Museum 2, housing contemporary art), and Rem Koolhaas (Samsung Child Education and Culture Center). The project signaled that Korean cultural institutions could commission world-class architects at the highest level.

The **National Museum of Korea** (국립중앙박물관), opened 2005 on the Yongsan site formerly occupied by the US military command's golf course, covers approximately 137,000 m² of total floor area and ranks among the six largest museums in the world. Its design, selected through an international competition, incorporates traditional Korean elements (layered eaves, stone platforms) within a modern institutional framework.

The **Cheonggyecheon Stream Restoration Project** (2003–2005), championed by then-Seoul Mayor Lee Myung-bak, demolished an elevated highway and restored a 5.8 km urban stream through central Seoul. The project became an internationally cited model for urban environmental restoration and public space creation, drawing landscape architects and urban planners worldwide as a case study.

Seung H-Sang (승효상, born 1952) emerged as perhaps the most intellectually influential Korean architect of his generation, articulating an "aesthetics of poverty" (*빈자의 미학*) that valued spatial emptiness, restrained materiality, and attentiveness to Korean everyday environments. His firm IROJE kmh Architects completed works including the Sujoldang house (1992) and various memorial and civic projects.

## Contemporary Landmarks (2000s–Present)

The **Dongdaemun Design Plaza** (DDP, 동대문디자인플라자), designed by Zaha Hadid Architects and opened in March 2014, is perhaps the most internationally prominent recent Korean architectural work. The futuristic structure, clad in 45,133 aluminum panels, covers 85,320 m² and incorporates a museum, exhibition halls, design market, and public plaza. The site incorporates archaeological excavations of Joseon-era structures revealed during construction.

**Lotte World Tower** (롯데월드타워), completed in April 2017, stands 555 meters tall with 123 floors, making it the fifth-tallest building in the world at completion and the tallest in South Korea. Its design by KPF Architects draws on traditional Korean ceramic forms. The tower anchors the Lotte World Mall complex in Jamsil, Seoul.

**Incheon International Airport** (인천국제공항), opened March 2001 with Terminal 2 added in January 2018, is consistently rated among the world's best airports. The terminal, designed by an international consortium, is a vast curvilinear structure housing traditional Korean cultural performances, garden atriums, and art installations, integrating cultural identity into transportation infrastructure.

The **Songdo International Business District** (송도국제도시), developed from 2003 on 6 km² of reclaimed land in Incheon, is one of the world's largest planned city projects. Designed as a "smart city" from inception, Songdo embedded sensors, pneumatic waste collection, and integrated ICT infrastructure throughout its urban fabric. As of 2020, roughly half of its planned 65,000 housing units were occupied, with continued development ongoing.

## Hanok Preservation and Heritage Architecture

Traditional hanok preservation became increasingly urgent as modernization threatened historic districts. **Jeonju Hanok Village** (전주한옥마을) retained approximately 800 traditional structures and became one of South Korea's leading cultural tourism destinations, attracting over 11 million visitors annually before the COVID-19 pandemic. Seoul's **Bukchon Hanok Village** (북촌한옥마을) preserved approximately 900 hanok in the area between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung, creating a popular historic district managed through ongoing regulatory designations since the 1970s.

The Korean government has designated over 13,000 buildings as national or local cultural properties under the Cultural Heritage Protection Act (1962, revised multiple times). Tension between heritage preservation and real-estate development remains acute in Seoul's historic districts, where hanok replacement by dense residential or commercial buildings has been an ongoing battle for decades.

## Urban Planning Challenges

Seoul's administrative area houses approximately 9.7 million people, with the broader Seoul Metropolitan Area containing over 25 million — nearly half of South Korea's population. This extreme urban concentration has generated persistent planning challenges: housing affordability, traffic congestion, green space scarcity, and preservation vs. development conflicts.

Major urban regeneration initiatives include the **Seoullo 7017** elevated walkway (opened 2017), transforming an unused overpass into a landscaped pedestrian garden, and the **Gwanghwamun Square** (광화문광장) redesign (completed 2022), expanding the central plaza in front of Gyeongbokgung while reducing traffic lanes. Both projects reflect global trends toward car-reduction and public space investment.

## Global Integration

Korean architects increasingly operate internationally. The International Architecture Biennale in Venice and major design fairs feature Korean practitioners. The emergence of Korean architectural schools — particularly programs at Seoul National University, KAIST, and Yonsei University — as internationally networked institutions reflects Korea's integration into global architectural discourse. Design competitions for public buildings routinely attract international entries, and major commissions involve international firms working alongside Korean partners.

## Significance

South Korean architecture and urban design demonstrate how rapid development and modernization can generate distinct architectural identities that blend international modernism with local particularities. The field illustrates Korea's transformation from a colonial and war-devastated society to a high-tech urbanized nation. Contemporary Korean architects operate on the global stage, winning international competitions and contributing to projects worldwide. The ongoing negotiation between traditional preservation and modern development reflects broader tensions in Korean society between tradition and progress, local identity and globalization.

Architecturally, South Korea has made distinctive contributions to global practice in large-scale urban design and infrastructure. The systematic approach to mass housing through standardized apartment complexes influenced housing policy across Asia. The emphasis on design excellence in public commissions reflects Korea's "soft power" strategy — using cultural and design achievements as tools for national prestige and economic competitiveness.
