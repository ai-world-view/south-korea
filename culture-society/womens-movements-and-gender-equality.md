# Women's Movements and Gender Equality in South Korea

South Korea's **women's movement** and ongoing struggle for gender equality represent a complex historical arc from Confucian patriarchalism through anti-colonial feminism, postwar subordination under developmental authoritarianism, to contemporary feminism reshaped by digital culture, economic precarity, and generational conflict. Women's activism has been integral to South Korean democratization, labor organizing, and cultural change, yet contemporary surveys consistently rank South Korea among the lowest in gender-equality measures among affluent democracies (the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index has ranked South Korea in the 90s–110s among 146 countries in recent years, at 94th in 2024). The tension between women's legal rights achievements and persistent structural inequality defines the contemporary women's movement in the 2010s–2020s.

## Historical Foundations: Colonial Period to Militarized Developmentalism

### Pre-Modern and Early Modern Periods

Traditional Confucian Korea prescribed strict gender hierarchies organized around filial piety (*hyo*) and the domestic ideal of the *hyeonmo yangcheo* (현모양처, "wise mother, good wife"). Women were expected to obey father in youth, husband in marriage, and son in widowhood — the doctrine of the "three obediences." **Remarriage of widows was prohibited** under Joseon law, and property rights were severely restricted. However, historical records show that elite women, particularly in merchant and yangban households, exercised significant cultural agency: women's poetry (*sijo*, female authorship), calligraphy, and household economic management were recognized domains.

The **Korean Independence Movement** (1876–1945) included women activists, though often in subordinate organizational roles. **Yu Gwan-sun** (유관순, 1902–1920), a 17-year-old student, became a nationalist martyr after her leadership in the **March 1st Movement** (1919) and subsequent death under Japanese torture in prison—she became an iconic figure in Korean national consciousness and feminist histories.

### Japanese Colonial Period (1910–1945)

The colonial occupation disrupted traditional gender arrangements while introducing modern education for women. Colonial authorities sponsored girls' schooling (within limits), creating a cohort of educated women. Simultaneously, the Japanese mobilization of Korean women as industrial and agricultural laborers, and the coercive recruitment of so-called "comfort women" (tens of thousands of women, by widely varying estimates ranging up to roughly 200,000 across occupied territories, a large share of them Korean, coerced into sexual slavery for Japanese military personnel), created both victimhood and, later, a basis for postwar feminist reckonings with colonial violence and gender exploitation.

Some Korean women participated in the **independence movement** alongside men, and postwar Korean histories recovered figures like **Park In-duk** (박인덕), an early advocate for women's education and Korean independence.

### Post-Liberation and Korean War Era (1945–1960s)

The liberation of Korea from Japanese rule (1945) created initial euphoria around democratization and women's rights. The **1948 Constitution of the Republic of Korea** guaranteed gender equality in principle through its equality-before-the-law clause, and women received the franchise. However, the **Korean War** (1950–1953) devastated the peninsula, and the postwar order prioritized military security, economic reconstruction, and national development over social reform. Traditional patriarchal structures largely persisted despite constitutional protections.

The postwar period saw the emergence of early women's organizations focused on welfare and mothers' support, but these were often state-controlled or narrowly focused on family stabilization rather than systemic gender equality.

### The "Comfort Women" Issue and the 2015 Agreement Controversy

The **"comfort women"** issue — the sexual enslavement of Korean women by the Japanese military — became a sustained feminist and diplomatic flashpoint in postwar Korea. Surviving victims (known as *haalmoni*, grandmothers) organized under the **Korean Council for Justice and Remembrance for the Issues of Military Sexual Slavery by Japan** (정의기억연대, formerly the Korean Council for the Women Drafted for Military Sexual Slavery by Japan, established 1990). From **January 8, 1992**, the Council organized a weekly **Wednesday Demonstration** (수요집회) outside the Japanese embassy in Seoul — one of the world's longest-running protest demonstrations. The demonstrations demanded formal Japanese state apology, acknowledgment of responsibility, and reparations.

In **December 2015**, the governments of South Korea and Japan announced a bilateral agreement between Foreign Ministers Yun Byung-se and Fumio Kishida that they declared "final and irreversible": Japan offered an official apology and ¥1 billion (approximately $8.3 million) for a fund to support survivors. The agreement was negotiated without consulting surviving comfort women or the civic organizations representing them. The Korean Council and most survivors **rejected the agreement** as inadequate, made without their consent, and lacking the formal admission of state responsibility they demanded. The Moon Jae-in government (2017–2022) declined to enforce the agreement and ultimately dissolved the special fund in 2019, worsening Japan-Korea diplomatic relations but satisfying feminist advocates. The unresolved comfort women question has remained a persistent intersection of feminist activism, historical memory, and international relations.

## Developmental State and Women's Subordination (1960s–1980s)

### Park Chung-hee's Developmentalism and Gender Policy

Under **Park Chung-hee's developmental dictatorship** (1961–1979), South Korea prioritized rapid industrialization and military security. The state's gender ideology emphasized women's roles as **"mothers of the nation"** (민족의 어머니), prioritizing women's reproductive and domestic contributions to national development and military strength. Confucian family values were invoked to justify women's subordination.

However, paradoxically, **Park's industrial expansion created demand for female factory labor**. By the 1970s, **approximately 50% of South Korea's manufacturing workforce was female**, concentrated in textile, electronics, and garment production. These women workers—often rural migrants in their late teens and twenties—labored in exploitative conditions: low wages, long hours (12–16 hours per day was common), unsafe working conditions, and sexual harassment by male supervisors.

### Labor Activism and the "Discipline of Women"

Female factory workers pioneered South Korea's labor movement from the 1970s onward, despite systematic efforts to suppress unionization. The **Dongil Textile Company struggle** (동일방직, culminating in a 1978 incident in which company-aligned men assaulted striking female unionists with human excrement) and the **YH Trading Company incident** (1979, during which the young worker Kim Kyung-sook died) became iconic moments in Korean labor history. The workers' demands for basic dignity and fair wages intersected with anti-dictatorship activism, as independent labor organizing was suppressed under Park's regime.

Women labor activists, while fighting for worker rights alongside male colleagues, often faced distinct gender-based subordination: dismissal for pregnancy, forced "retirement" upon marriage, sexual coercion, and marginalization from union leadership. These early women unionists became enduring symbols of working-class and feminist resistance.

The **Great Workers' Struggle** of 1987, which erupted during the democratic transition, included significant female participation, though accounts often centered male factory workers and union leadership while marginalizing women's contributions.

## Democratization and 1990s Feminism (1987–2000s)

### Civil Society Emergence

The **democratization movement** (1987) created space for civil-society organizations, including women's groups. The **Women's Movement** (여성운동) coalesced into multiple organizational forms:

- **Trade union women's committees** and labor-based women's organizations
- **Civic women's organizations** focused on gender equality, anti-discrimination, and policy advocacy
- **Violence-against-women organizations** addressing domestic violence, sexual assault, and workplace harassment
- **Reunification and peace-oriented women's groups** addressing North Korea policy and militarism's impact on gender

Key institutional organizations included the **Korean Women's Associations United** (한국여성단체연합, KWAU, founded 1987), an umbrella body coordinating over 20 member organizations that became the primary feminist advocacy voice in government; the **Korean Women Workers Association** (한국여성노동자회, founded 1987), focused on female labor rights; and the **Korean Women's Development Institute** (한국여성정책연구원), a government-funded research body. These institutions shaped feminist policy advocacy through the 1990s–2000s.

These organizations articulated demands for:
- **Equal pay for equal work**
- **Protection against sexual harassment and domestic violence**
- **Reproductive rights and family-law reform** (particularly divorce and inheritance rights, which remained heavily patriarchal)
- **Childcare and maternal support policies**

### Legislative Achievements (1990s–2000s)

The democratic period enabled legal victories:

- **Gender Equality in Employment Act** (1987, revised 1999): Prohibited sex discrimination in hiring, wages, and working conditions, though enforcement remained weak
- **Domestic Violence Prevention Act** (1998): Criminalized domestic violence and enabled protective orders, marking the first formal legal acknowledgment that domestic violence was a crime (previously treated as private family matter)
- **Sexual Harassment Prevention Act** (1999): Required workplaces to establish sexual harassment policies
- **Family Law reforms**: a 1990 revision (effective 1991) granted daughters equal inheritance rights with sons, reversing centuries of male-preferential practice, and a 2005 reform abolished the **hoju** (호주제) household-head system that had legally subordinated women within the family registry
- **Reproductive rights**: The Constitutional Court upheld limitations on abortion but gradually permissioned it under narrower circumstances; full legalization remained contested

### Mid-2000s: Second-Wave Institutional Expansion

By the mid-2000s, South Korea had established governmental machinery for gender equality (the **Ministry of Gender Equality and Family**, established 2001) and anti-discrimination frameworks. However, women's movement activists complained that institutional gains had not translated into cultural or workplace transformation. The gender wage gap remained one of the largest among OECD countries; women's labor-force participation, while rising, remained concentrated in part-time, precarious sectors; and senior leadership positions in government, corporations, and academia remained male-dominated.

## 2000s–2010s: Neoliberalism and Feminist Backlash

### Precarity and the "Flexible" Workforce

South Korea's 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and subsequent restructuring created a **dualized labor market** with stable, male-dominated full-time employment alongside precarious part-time and contract work, disproportionately done by women. The rhetoric of workforce "flexibility" masked the systematic channeling of women into low-wage, non-unionized sectors.

This restructuring coincided with neoliberal approaches to family policy: reduced welfare, emphasis on individual (not collective) responsibility, and market-based solutions to childcare and elder care. The burden of unpaid domestic labor, caregiving, and reproduction fell primarily on women, intensifying what scholars termed the "double bind" of career and family.

### Military Service and the "Woman Question"

South Korea's **mandatory male military service** (approximately 18–21 months) created what feminist scholars identified as a systematic gender divide: men's time was conscripted for national defense, but women were excluded from this citizenship obligation while also excluded from the state protection and lifetime veteran benefits that military service conferred. This generated ongoing feminist debate about gender and national security, though proposals for female military service or exemption remained politically contentious.

### Misogyny, Digital Culture, and Generational Backlash (2010s)

Beginning in the 2010s, South Korean feminism underwent significant generational shifts. Younger feminists (particularly those born in the 1990s–2000s) experienced different economic and cultural contexts:

- **Digital feminist activism**: Online platforms (Twitter, university communities, anonymous forums) enabled more grassroots, decentralized feminist organizing than the institutional women's movement. Hashtag campaigns (#위드유, #미투) mobilized rapidly around sexual violence and harassment
- **Economic precarity and education**: Despite women's higher university graduation rates than men (by 2010s), women faced systematized employment discrimination. The "Specs War" (스펙 전쟁) of competition among credentials-obsessed youth was experienced as gendered: women faced additional appearance standards and marriage-penalty expectations
- **Reproduction and choice**: Younger women increasingly rejected the imperative of marriage and childbearing, challenging postwar ideology. Birth rates declined precipitously, reaching among the world's lowest (approximately 0.72 per woman by 2023), sparking national policy panic and generational conflict over women's reproductive autonomy

The 2010s also saw emergence of explicit **misogynist backlash**: men's rights movements, online harassment campaigns against feminists, and political manipulation of gender tensions (e.g., male politicians attributing unemployment to "women taking men's jobs" in education and public service). The phenomenon of "digital misogyny" (온라인 성차별) became a significant social problem, with women activists reporting coordinated harassment. The **Ilbe Storehouse** (일베저장소) and similar online communities became incubators of misogynist content, gaming slang, and targeted harassment against women, including activists and female politicians.

Also in 2018, the **탈코르셋 (escape the corset) movement** emerged as a form of radical bodily autonomy activism: women publicly discarded cosmetics, cut their hair short, and refused conventional beauty routines and shaving, challenging the Korean "corset" of normative femininity. Activists posted before-and-after photographs online, building a community of women explicitly rejecting the economic and time burdens imposed by beauty standards — standards that surveys estimated cost Korean women an average of 30–40 minutes per day in preparation time and billions of won annually in cosmetics and beauty industry spending. The movement generated intense backlash from the cosmetics industry and conservative media, as well as from some feminist organizations who viewed it as essentializing gender norms.

## #MeToo Movement and Contemporary Feminism (2016–2020s)

### Global #MeToo and Korean Implementation

The **#MeToo movement**, which erupted globally in 2017 following Harvey Weinstein's exposure, reached South Korea with particular cultural intensity in January 2018, when prosecutor **Seo Ji-hyun** went on national television to describe sexual abuse by a senior colleague. High-profile allegations soon followed against figures in theater, film, literature, religion, and politics, mobilizing national discourse around sexual harassment and assault.

Notable cases included:

- **An Hee-jung**, governor of South Chungcheong Province: Resigned in March 2018 after his secretary **Kim Ji-eun** publicly accused him of repeated sexual assault; convicted on appeal and sentenced to three and a half years in prison
- **Lee Yoon-taek**, a celebrated theater director: Convicted of sexual assault of members of his troupe
- **Ko Un**, an internationally known poet: Faced allegations of sexual misconduct and lost a defamation suit he brought against an accuser
- **Cho Jae-hyun**, a prominent actor: Acknowledged past misconduct, apologized, and withdrew from public life

The movement prompted:
- **Institutional reforms**: Universities, government agencies, and corporations established anti-harassment policies and reporting mechanisms
- **Legal changes**: Enhanced criminal penalties for sexual harassment and assault (2020 reforms)
- **Cultural shifts**: Increased media coverage of gender-based violence and feminist perspectives

However, critics noted that prosecutions remained inconsistent, victim support systems remained inadequate, and cultural attitudes shifted more slowly than institutional policies.

### The Molka (Hidden Camera) Crisis and Hyehwa Station Protests

A distinct but closely related feminist mobilization concerned the **molka crisis** (몰카, a contraction of 몰래카메라 *mollae kamera*, "hidden camera" — covert cameras used to film women in public restrooms, motels, gyms, and workplaces without consent). Evidence suggested hundreds of thousands of Korean women had been victimized by non-consensual intimate photography, which was widely distributed on illegal pornography platforms. Critically, feminist activists identified a systematic disparity in enforcement: lenient sentences for male perpetrators while a female artist prosecuted for photographing male nude models received heavier sanctions.

From **May 2018**, women organized a series of large Saturday demonstrations in the **Hyehwa** area of Seoul, drawing tens of thousands of participants per rally and growing to an estimated 60,000–70,000 at their peak — among the largest all-female-organized protests in Korean history. The protesters' demands: equivalent criminal enforcement regardless of perpetrator gender, proactive government removal of illegal content from online platforms, and dedicated police units to investigate spycam crimes. The scale of the demonstrations — sustained over multiple weeks — was unprecedented for gender-specific feminist organizing and forced policy acknowledgment; the government subsequently created special investigations units focused on non-consensual photography and increased platform liability.

### Generational and Class Divides

Contemporary South Korean feminism displays complex internal divisions:

- **Generational divides**: Younger feminists (Gen Z, post-1995) emphasize reproductive autonomy and reject marriage imperatives, while older feminists emphasize workplace and family-law equality
- **Class and institutional position**: Educated, middle-class women in white-collar professions have benefited most from legal equality; working-class and precarious-sector women face compounded marginalization
- **Conservative backlash**: Conservative religious groups and male-identified movements have mobilized against feminist gains, particularly around LGBTQ+ rights and reproductive autonomy
- **4B movement**: The **4B movement** — from the Korean prefix *bi* (비, "no"), naming four refusals: 비연애 (no dating), 비성관계 (no sexual relationships), 비혼 (no marriage), and 비출산 (no childbirth) — crystallized a generation of younger feminists' explicit rejection of heterosexual relationship imperatives. Growing through the late 2010s online, the 4B movement gained global media attention as South Korea's total fertility rate fell to approximately **0.72 per woman in 2023** — the world's lowest — linking reproductive autonomy directly to feminist politics. The government's "population crisis" framing — which implicitly blamed women's choices for demographic decline — intensified feminist resistance to pro-natalist policies

## Contemporary Status and Challenges (2020s)

### Structural Inequalities Persist

Despite legal equality, South Korea faces persistent gender-based inequalities:

- **Wage gap**: South Korea's **gender pay gap** of approximately 31–36% is the largest among all OECD member states — a position it has held consistently since OECD began comparative reporting. The gap is driven by occupational segregation (women concentrated in care, education, and service sectors), mandatory career interruptions for childbearing, discrimination in promotion, and the marriage penalty (women's wages drop markedly after marriage relative to men's). The **World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Report 2024** ranked South Korea **94th out of 146 countries**, placing it below many developing economies despite its status as an OECD member with high female educational attainment
- **Corporate leadership**: Women hold approximately 6–8% of board seats and executive positions in major Korean corporations (the 300 KOSPI-listed firms); leadership pipelines remain male-dominated despite 2020 legislation requiring listed companies to have at least one female board member
- **Political representation**: Women hold approximately **20% of National Assembly seats** as of 2024 (60 of 300), a significant improvement from approximately 5% in the early 1990s but still below the global average for democracies; only one woman, **Han Myeong-sook** (2006–2007), has served as prime minister, and women remain rare in the major economic cabinet portfolios
- **Unpaid care work**: Women perform approximately 80% of household labor and childcare, a disparity that has narrowed only marginally despite women's increased workforce participation; the gender gap in time spent on unpaid work is among the widest in the OECD
- **Sexual violence**: Reported rates of sexual harassment and assault remain high; victim support and prosecution inconsistently applied; conviction rates in rape cases have historically been below 30%

### Reproductive Rights and Abortion

South Korea's abortion law has remained one of the most restrictive among democracies. While criminalized by statute since 1953, abortion was tolerated de facto through the 1980s–2010s in limited circumstances (incest, rape, fetal abnormality, maternal health threat). In 2019, the Constitutional Court ruled that the abortion ban was unconstitutional in light of women's fundamental rights, mandating legislative reform. However, subsequent legislative negotiations between feminist organizations, religious conservatives, medical professionals, and politicians remained deadlocked through 2026, with incomplete decriminalization and restricted access persisting.

### LGBTQ+ Rights and the Intersection with Feminism

South Korea's LGBTQ+ rights movement, while growing, faces organized conservative opposition. Feminist movements have been divided: some feminist organizations have allied with LGBTQ+ activism around gender justice and reproductive autonomy, while conservative women's organizations have opposed same-sex marriage and LGBTQ+ rights as conflicts with "traditional family values." This division reflects broader South Korean cultural polarization.

## Cultural and Institutional Legacies

The women's movement in South Korea has profoundly shaped contemporary culture and institutions:

- **Language and consciousness**: The Korean term *yeoseong* (여성, "woman") entered everyday discourse as both a demographic category and a site of political identity; feminist vocabulary and concepts permeated media and education
- **Educational access**: Women's university enrollment exceeded men's by the 2000s (approximately 52% female by 2010), though occupational outcomes remained gendered
- **Family structures**: Delayed marriage and childbearing, rising divorce rates, and increasing single-person households reflected the partial success of women's reproductive autonomy, though accompanied by national anxiety over population decline

## Legacy and Historical Significance

South Korea's women's movement occupies a distinctive position in both global feminism and Korean history:

- **Anti-colonial and labor roots**: Unlike some democracies where women's movements emerged primarily from middle-class organizing, South Korean feminism emerged from anti-colonial nationalism and working-class labor struggle, creating distinctive political orientations
- **Democratization nexus**: Women's activism was integral to the Korean **democratization struggle** (1987), though often underhistoricized in favor of male-centered narratives of labor and student movements
- **Persistence of Confucian patriarchalism**: Despite legal victories, Confucian cultural values around family hierarchy and gender roles have proven remarkably persistent, creating a gap between formal equality and lived experience
- **Generational and digital transformation**: The 2010s–2020s saw a generational rupture in women's organizing: younger feminists mobilized digitally and rejected older feminist priorities, reflecting both progress and new challenges

The women's movement remains central to South Korea's ongoing struggle to reconcile economic development and democratic governance with substantive gender justice. President **[Lee Jae-myung](../people/lee-jae-myung.md)**, elected in June 2025 following the constitutional crisis triggered by Yoon Suk-yeol's martial law declaration, pledged to reverse Yoon's push to abolish the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family — which Yoon had campaigned to eliminate as a symbol of what he framed as anti-male discrimination — and to advance LGBTQ+ civil-rights protections, signaling that gender-equality policy would remain a front-line political battleground into the late 2020s.
