# Korean Shamanism and Muism

Korean shamanism, known as **Muism** (무교, *mugyo*), is an indigenous spiritual tradition of Korea that predates Buddhism and Confucianism and continues as a living religious practice and cultural force in contemporary South Korea. Muism centers on the worship of spirits (*sin*), the performance of ritual ceremonies (*gut*), and the role of shamans (*mudang*) as mediators between the human and spirit worlds. The tradition encompasses animistic beliefs, ancestor veneration, and the propitiation of place spirits, household deities, and cosmic forces, and has shaped Korean culture across millennia despite periods of suppression under Confucian orthodoxy and during the colonial and modern eras.

## Historical Foundations and Pre-Buddhist Origins

Shamanism was the dominant spiritual system of the Korean peninsula long before the introduction of Buddhism in the 4th century CE and the adoption of Neo-Confucianism during the Joseon dynasty. Archaeological and textual evidence suggests that shamanic practices were widespread in the ancient Three Kingdoms period (Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla), where shamans held roles as healers, diviners, and ritual specialists in court and village settings. The *samguk sagi* (Historical Records of the Three Kingdoms) records shamanic ceremonies, spirit possession, and divination practices among early Korean peoples. During the Unified Silla (668–935) and Goryeo (918–1392) periods, shamanism coexisted with Buddhism; royal courts sometimes patronized shamanic rituals while Buddhist temples flourished, and the two traditions intermingled, with shamans often incorporating Buddhist elements into their practice and Buddhist monks attending shamanic ceremonies.

The Joseon dynasty (1392–1910), founded on Neo-Confucian principles, marked a shift in shamanism's institutional status. Confucian scholars viewed shamanism as superstition and heterodoxy, and official policies restricted shamanic activity in court and elite circles. Shamanism was progressively pushed to the margins of respectable society, relegated to rural villages, markets, and lower-class communities. However, shamanism never disappeared; it persisted as the spiritual practice of commoners, especially women, and retained deep cultural roots in Korean villages, where shamans continued to perform life-cycle rituals, heal illnesses, and mediate relationships with household and place spirits.

## Core Spiritual Beliefs and Cosmology

Muism's cosmological framework centers on a pluralistic spirit world. At the apex of the hierarchy sits the Sky God (*Hananim* or *Cheonsang*), a supreme divine being who governs cosmic order, though the Sky God is typically distant from human affairs and not directly worshipped. Below this are hierarchies of spirits: household deities (*gasin*), among them the house-frame guardian *Seongju*, who protect families and dwellings; village guardian spirits (*seonangsin* or *dangsin*) who oversee localities; mountain spirits (*sansin*); water and sea spirits; and the spirits of deceased ancestors and historical figures. Agricultural deities related to harvests, childbirth goddesses (*Samsin Halmoni*, the three-spirit grandmother presiding over birth and childhood), and disease-causing malevolent spirits all populate the Muist spirit world. The *Chilseong* (Seven Stars, the Big Dipper deity) governs fate and longevity. Spirits are not universally benevolent; some cause illness, misfortune, or death, and shamanic ritual aims to appease, negotiate with, or banish such spirits through offerings and ceremonial performance.

Central to Muism is the concept of *kut*, the elaborate public ritual ceremony that serves multiple functions: to honor spirits, cure illnesses attributed to spiritual causes, pacify the spirits of the dead (*ssikkimgut* or cleansing rite), celebrate life transitions, and restore communal harmony. A *kut* can last from several hours to multiple days and involves music, dancing, spirit possession, the recitation of mythological narratives (*musok sinhwa*), offerings of food and drink, and the ecstatic participation of shamans and community members. The mudang, the shaman (historically and predominantly female), undergoes rigorous initiation, often involving a spiritual crisis or calling, and develops the ability to enter trance states and commune with spirits. The mudang is both healer and performer, diagnostician and medium, playing a crucial role in village spiritual life.

## Initiation and the Mudang's Calling

The path to becoming a mudang is typically involuntary and marked by *shin-byeong* (신병, divine sickness): a period of severe illness, psychological crisis, or unusual experiences interpreted as a calling by spirits. *Shin-byeong* may manifest as physical ailment, visions, auditory phenomena, depression, or social withdrawal; the condition does not resolve until the afflicted individual accepts the spiritual calling and undergoes an initiation ritual (*naerim-gut*, 내림굿). The initiation ceremony formally establishes the new mudang's relationship with her spirit guardians and marks her social transition into the role of shaman.

After initiation, the new mudang trains under an experienced teacher (*sineomeoni*, spirit mother), learning ritual procedures, mythological narratives, drum and instrument technique, and the specific repertoire of her tradition. The relationship between spirit mother and spiritual daughter creates lineages of shamanic transmission that preserve regional styles and ritual knowledge. This ecstatic, possession-based path — the *gangsinmu* (강신무), the shaman who receives a deity — predominates in the central and northern regions, including Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Hwanghae. It stands in contrast to the hereditary *seseummu* (세습무) of the southern regions — Jeolla, Gyeongsang, and the East Coast — who inherit the office and its ritual repertoire through family lineage rather than through a possession experience.

## Regional Traditions and Styles

Korean shamanism exhibits distinct regional styles reflecting geographical and historical diversity. The **Seoul and Gyeonggi province** tradition (*Seoul Gut*, sometimes called *Gyeonggi Gut*) is characterized by elaborate, formally structured ceremonies with multiple sequential segments (*geori*), each addressed to a different deity. Seoul mudang wear distinctive bright robes with headgear specific to each spirit and typically perform with a team of musicians playing *janggu*, *buk*, *kkwaenggwari*, *haegeum*, and wind instruments. This tradition was heavily influenced by royal court and capital culture.

The **Hwanghae province** tradition (from the coastal western region, now North Korea) is known for its particularly dramatic and athletic performance style, with mudang leaping onto blades and performing acrobatic feats as evidence of spiritual empowerment. Many Hwanghae mudang fled to South Korea during the Korean War (1950–1953) and continued their tradition in Seoul and surrounding areas; the Hwanghae tradition has been designated National Intangible Cultural Property No. 82.

The **Jeolla province** tradition (southwestern Korea) features *dang gut* and is known for its deep integration with pansori vocal aesthetics and its emotionally intense, melodic ritual singing. The **East Sea coastal** (*Donghae an*) tradition emphasizes communal fishing-village ceremonies (*byeolsingut*) and is distinctive for its local maritime spirit worship. The **Jeju Island** tradition (*Jeju Musok*) is particularly archaic, preserving elements distinct from mainland Korean shamanism, and the **Jeju Chilmeori-dang Yeongdeung-gut** — a ritual honoring wind gods for fishing community protection — was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity list in 2009.

## Shamanism During Colonial Period and Modernization

Under Japanese colonial rule (1910–1945), shamanism faced renewed suppression as colonial authorities viewed shamanic practices as backward superstition incompatible with modernity. Japanese colonial policies discouraged or banned public shamanic ceremonies in some regions, though grassroots practice continued. The post-1945 era and rapid modernization of South Korea posed new challenges to shamanism. Urbanization, the spread of Christianity and Buddhism, improved healthcare, and state secularism relegated shamanic practice further to the periphery. By the mid-20th century, shamanism had largely disappeared from Seoul and major urban centers, surviving mainly in rural areas and among marginalized communities.

Yet shamanism did not disappear; it transformed. Urban shamans adapted their practice to city contexts, offering divination and healing services from small shrines or storefronts. Shamanism became associated with popular religion, folk belief, and entertainment, a cultural form that urban-educated Koreans often dismissed as superstition while simultaneously remaining culturally curious. The Korean government, influenced by modernization ideology, long neglected or discouraged shamanism, though it did not actively persecute it as severely as other Asian nations have. The National Folk Museum and scholars began documenting shamanic practices as living folklore and cultural heritage.

## Contemporary Shamanism and Cultural Revitalization

Since the 1980s and 1990s, shamanism has undergone a modest revival and reclaiming as cultural heritage. South Korea's domestic designation of specific shamanic traditions as Intangible Cultural Properties provided institutional recognition; **Gyeonggi Dodang-gut** (the shamanic ritual of the Gyeonggi region) was designated Important Intangible Cultural Property No. 98 in 1990, and west-coast fishing-community rites carried by Hwanghae-tradition shamans were recognized under No. 82. At the international level, the **Gangneung Danoje Festival** — which incorporates shamanic ritual (the *Danogut*) alongside the *Gwanno Gamyeongeuk* mask dance and folk performance — was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage Representative List in 2005, and the **Jeju Chilmeori-dang Yeongdeung-gut** in 2009. These UNESCO recognitions elevated shamanism's status as a recognized cultural tradition. Korean anthropologists, folklorists, and cultural activists have documented and studied shamanism, framing it not merely as superstition but as a complex spiritual and performance system with historical depth. Urban intellectuals and artists have shown renewed interest in shamanic aesthetics, mythology, and performance, incorporating shamanic elements into contemporary theater, film, and visual art.

Contemporary shamanism in South Korea encompasses diverse practitioners and contexts. Traditional rural shamans continue ceremonies in villages, particularly in Jeolla Province and other regions with stronger shamanic heritage. Urban shamans operate in Seoul and other cities, serving clients who seek divination, healing for psychological or spiritual distress, or blessings for life events. Shamanic shrines and fellowship groups exist in urban neighborhoods, attracting devotees from across social classes. Some Christian churches have incorporated shamanic elements or adopted shamanic healing frameworks into their ministry, creating syncretic forms of religiosity. The number of practicing shamans in South Korea is difficult to determine given the informal and unregulated nature of the practice; estimates range widely, and shaman associations have claimed membership in the hundreds of thousands.

## Shamanism and Other Korean Religions

Shamanism coexists and intermeshes with Buddhism, Christianity, and Confucianism in contemporary Korean religion, a context often described as Korea's "religious pluralism" or "syncretic religion." Many Koreans identify with multiple religious traditions simultaneously; a person may attend Buddhist temples, participate in Shamanic rituals, and respect Confucian filial piety. Buddhism and shamanism have a long history of coexistence, with Buddhist-influenced shamanic practice incorporating Buddhist cosmology and deities (*bodhisattvas* appearing as spirits). Christian churches, becoming the dominant religion among the urban educated after 1960, have sometimes competed with shamanism, framing shamanic practice as idolatry or superstition, yet some Christians consult shamans for healing, and a phenomenon of "Christian shamanism" emerged in some contexts.

Confucian orthodoxy, while officially suppressive of shamanism, never fully eradicated it; ancestor veneration, a Confucian practice, shares spiritual concerns with shamanic ritual. The enduring presence of household shrines, the propitiation of ancestors, and the maintenance of lineage rituals reflect overlapping spiritual concerns. In rural Korea especially, shamanic *gut* ceremonies often occur in the context of Confucian family rituals, without ideological contradiction at the community level.

## Shamanism in Art, Performance, and Popular Culture

Shamanic mythology, aesthetics, and performance have profoundly influenced Korean arts. Shamanic narrative traditions — including the *bon-puri* (본풀이), the sung origin myths that recount how each deity came to be, and epic narratives of heroic deities — circulate in folklore and have inspired literary and theatrical works. The ecstatic performance style of shamanic *gut*, with its driving percussion, dance, and trance, has influenced Korean performing arts, from pansori narrative singing to contemporary experimental theater. Artists and dancers have drawn on shamanic aesthetics for creative inspiration, and shamanic performance has been staged and reinterpreted in concert and festival contexts.

Contemporary Korean cinema and television have portrayed shamanism in varied ways, from respectful ethnographic documentaries to horror films depicting malevolent spirits and shamanic encounters. Na Hong-jin's *The Wailing* (곡성, 2016) is among the most discussed Korean films engaging with shamanism: it depicts a rural village afflicted by mysterious deaths and features a shaman, Il-gwang (played by Hwang Jung-min), performing an elaborate and unsettling *gut* ceremony at the film's climax. The South Korean–Thai co-production *The Medium* (랑종, 2021, directed by Banjong Pisanthanakun and produced by Na Hong-jin) further explores shamanic possession and inherited spiritual calling. Various Korean television dramas have included mudang characters in both dramatic and comedic roles. These cultural representations have both popularized shamanism among younger urban Koreans and sometimes perpetuated reductive stereotypes of mudang as fraudulent fortune-tellers or objects of horror.

## Challenges and Contemporary Debates

Shamanism in modern South Korea faces several challenges. The predominance of Christianity among the educated urban middle class has reduced shamanism's cultural cachet; it is often dismissed by progressive intellectuals as superstition or backward folk belief. The professionalization and commercialization of shamanic services has raised ethical concerns within the community itself about charlatanism and exploitation, particularly regarding finances (clients pay shamans substantial sums for ceremonies) and vulnerable individuals seeking healing. Gender dynamics in shamanism are complex; while mudang are predominantly women and shamanism has historically offered women spiritual authority and economic independence, shamanic women are also subject to social stigma and limited social mobility.

State support and cultural policy have been ambivalent. While some shamanic practices have been designated as Intangible Cultural Properties, the South Korean government has not systematically funded shamanic preservation as it has with other heritage forms. The association of shamanism with superstition and low-class practice has made policy support politically difficult. Some regions, particularly those with strong shamanic heritage like Jeolla, have begun documenting and preserving local shamanic traditions, and the Shamanism Studies Center at major universities conducts academic research.

## Legacy and Spiritual Significance

Despite modernization, urbanization, and religious competition, shamanism remains a living spiritual tradition in South Korea and an integral part of the country's religious and cultural landscape. For millions of Koreans, particularly in rural areas and among older generations, shamanic ritual addresses spiritual needs — healing, blessing, the honoring of ancestors — that other institutions do not serve. The mudang continues to serve as healer, counselor, and mediator of the sacred, preserving knowledge of ritual, myth, and spiritual practice transmitted across centuries. Shamanism's resilience testifies to deep roots in Korean culture and the persistence of spiritual concerns that urbanization and secularization have not displaced.

The recognition of shamanism as cultural heritage and the academic study of its spiritual and aesthetic dimensions have contributed to a modest cultural rehabilitation. Shamanism is increasingly understood not as a relic of the past but as a living tradition that continues to evolve while maintaining connections to ancient Korean spiritual foundations. For scholars of Korean religion, shamanism is indispensable to understanding Korea's pluralistic spiritual landscape and the ways that indigenous traditions persist, adapt, and coexist with world religions in the modern nation-state.
