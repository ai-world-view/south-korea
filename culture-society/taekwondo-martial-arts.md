# Taekwondo and Korean Martial Arts

Taekwondo (태권도, literally "the way of the foot and fist") is a Korean martial art characterized by high kicks, rapid footwork, dynamic movement, and striking techniques using the hands and feet. Developed in the 1950s and 1960s from older Korean martial traditions and external influences, taekwondo has evolved into the world's most widely practiced martial art with an estimated 80+ million practitioners globally and official recognition as an Olympic sport since 2000. It serves as both a domestic physical-education system and a major component of Korean soft power internationally. Korea also maintains traditional and modern martial-arts ecosystems including hapkido, tang soo do, ssireum (Korean wrestling), and other combat systems rooted in Korean and East Asian martial traditions.

## Origins and Historical Development

The exact genealogy of taekwondo is contested among historians and practitioners. Traditional narratives attribute its roots to three kingdoms-era martial systems and particularly to the Hwarang warrior society of Silla (6th–10th centuries), though direct lineage claims are not historically verified. The modern martial art emerged in the 1950s following Korean liberation (1945) from Japanese colonial rule. Korean martial artists, having absorbed Japanese karate techniques during the colonial period and post-war contact, synthesized indigenous striking traditions with external influences to create a distinctly Korean martial system. Key figures in the formalization included General Choi Hong-hi and other masters who trained soldiers in the newly formed Republic of Korea military. The Korea Taekwondo Association (KTA) was founded in 1959, establishing the framework for standardization and global promotion. An alternative genealogy attributes taekwondo development to the Tang Soo Do Association (also founded in the 1950s), which emphasized karate-influenced techniques; Tang Soo Do has remained a parallel and sometimes rival martial art, with practitioners claiming purer lineage and greater technical depth.

The term "taekwondo" itself was not universally adopted until the 1970s; the discipline was known under a variety of competing names in its early decades, including "tang soo do," "kong soo do," and "Korean karate," before "taekwondo" was settled on as the unifying term. The standardization of terminology and rules was driven largely by General Choi Hong-hi (1918–2002)'s efforts to promote taekwondo internationally and secure Olympic recognition. The World Taekwondo Federation (WTF, now World Taekwondo) was established in 1973, competing with the International Taekwon-Do Federation (ITF, founded 1966 by Choi) for international authority. Kukkiwon (국기원), the official World Taekwondo Headquarters and national training center, was established in 1972 in Seoul's Gangnam district; it functions as the sole issuing authority for international black-belt certification (단증, *danjeung*) and hosts the World Taekwondo Academy. The WTF's version ultimately achieved Olympic status and international dominance, particularly after the 1988 Seoul Olympics (demonstration sport), 1992 Barcelona Olympics (demonstration sport again), and the 2000 Sydney Olympics, where taekwondo was finally included as a full medal sport. The governing body was renamed from "World Taekwondo Federation" to simply "World Taekwondo" in 2017, to avoid the acronym WTF, which had acquired unintended internet slang connotations.

## Martial-Arts Philosophy and Technique

Taekwondo emphasizes power generation through hip rotation and lower-body mechanics, producing characteristic high kicks, spinning techniques, and explosive footwork. The martial art teaches five tenets: courtesy (예의, *ye-ui*), integrity (염치, *yeom-chi*), perseverance (인내, *in-nae*), self-control (극기, *geuk-gi*), and indomitable spirit (백절불굴, *baekjeol-bulgul*). These principles reflect Confucian values and serve as moral and psychological frameworks for practitioners. Training progression follows a colored belt system (typically white → yellow → green → blue → red → black), with strict hierarchies and protocols governing instructor-student relationships and sparring etiquette. The emphasis on respect and deference parallels broader Confucian social structures and Korean honor culture.

Technically, taekwondo strikes are classified into hand techniques (punching, blocking, striking) and foot techniques (kicks to various target zones). Distinctive techniques include the roundhouse kick (돌려차기, *dollyeo-chagi*), the spinning back kick (뒤차기, *dwi-chagi*), and jumping or flying kicks (뛰어차기, *ttwieo-chagi*). Traditional taekwondo (ITF style) emphasizes controlled power, self-defense applications, and board/brick breaking. Sport taekwondo (WTF/WT style) prioritizes Olympic-rule sparring with emphasis on speed, accuracy, and scoring within specific target zones (torso and head for official competition points). The sport version has evolved significantly since Olympic inclusion; rules have undergone frequent revision to enhance spectator appeal, prevent injuries, and standardize scoring, making WT taekwondo strategically more refined than traditional martial art applications.

## International Expansion and Soft Power

Taekwondo's global expansion accelerated from the 1970s onward, driven by Korean government policy, military training exchange programs, and private business interests. Korean taekwondo masters established dojang (dojos; training studios) worldwide, particularly in the United States, where the sport gained popularity among middle-class suburban communities seeking youth enrichment and self-defense. By the 1990s, taekwondo had become the default martial art for many non-Asian Western practitioners, surpassing karate in participation rates in some regions. The sport's Olympic trajectory—demonstrated at the 1988 Seoul Olympics, medaled at 1992 Barcelona, and made a permanent Olympic sport in 2000—elevated taekwondo's international prestige and commercial viability. Olympic inclusion led to standardization of rules, equipment, and competition formats, with governing bodies (particularly World Taekwondo, the WT) establishing transparent scoring systems, weight classifications, and anti-doping protocols.

Taekwondo serves as a vector for Korean cultural soft power (한류, *Hallyu*) similar to K-pop and K-drama. The sport carries Korea's international image as a technologically advanced, disciplined, and culturally vibrant nation. Korean athletes' dominance in Olympic taekwondo—winning multiple medals at every Olympics since inclusion and holding top world rankings—reinforces Korea's global visibility. Korean taekwondo federations have actively promoted the sport in developing nations, positioning taekwondo as an accessible and affordable martial art for youth development and character education. The International Taekwon-Do Federation (ITF, historically affiliated with North Korea through founder Choi's later-life residency there) has declined in global influence as the WTF (now World Taekwondo, renamed in 2017) asserted dominance, particularly after the WTF secured and retained Olympic status.

## Hapkido and Tang Soo Do

Hapkido (합기도, "the way of coordinated power") is a Korean martial art emphasizing circular motions, joint locks, and defensive techniques against multiple attackers. Developed in the 1950s–1960s, hapkido incorporates principles from Aikido (the Japanese martial art emphasizing harmony and redirection) with Korean striking and kicking traditions. The founder, Choi Yong-sool, trained in Daito-ryu Aikijujutsu in Japan and adapted those joint-manipulation techniques to Korean contexts. Hapkido emphasizes practical self-defense and includes hand strikes, kicks, grappling, joint locks (관절기, *gwanjeol-gi*), and throws. Hapkido practitioners learn to neutralize attackers through circular movements and efficiency rather than direct force, making it appealing to practitioners of varying physical sizes and ages. The martial art has remained more niche than taekwondo globally, practiced primarily among dedicated enthusiasts and self-defense-oriented students.

Tang Soo Do (당수도, "the way of the Chinese hand," from Chinese *Tang* dynasty) represents an alternative lineage emphasizing Japanese karate's technical foundations synthesized with Korean martial traditions. Tang Soo Do practitioners preserve kata (형, *hyeong*; choreographed movement patterns) and emphasize technical precision and power generation through precise body mechanics. Unlike taekwondo's relative standardization, Tang Soo Do maintains multiple competing organizations and less formal international governance structures, resulting in greater technical variability but less commercial standardization. Tang Soo Do has experienced modest international growth but remains far less prominent than taekwondo in media visibility or participation numbers. Some Tang Soo Do associations and practitioners claim truer preservation of pre-Olympic martial principles and deeper technical sophistication compared to sport taekwondo's rule-driven simplification.

## Taekkyeon: The Indigenous Kicking Art

Taekkyeon (택견, also romanized as taekkyon) is widely regarded as the indigenous Korean kicking art most directly ancestral to taekwondo, though the two are distinct systems. Unlike the formalized sport structure of modern taekwondo, taekkyeon emphasizes fluid, rhythmic footwork, dynamic leg motions, and whole-body balance rather than static stances or rigid technique sequences. Practitioners move with a characteristic bouncing rhythm (品자걸음, *pumjageoreum*, or "dance-like stepping") and seek to destabilize opponents through sweeping, hooking, and pushing leg techniques rather than powerful linear strikes. Taekkyeon was suppressed during the Japanese colonial period (1910–1945) and nearly disappeared; its survival is attributed in significant part to master Shin Han-seung (1928–1987), who maintained the tradition through the mid-20th century. UNESCO recognized taekkyeon as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2011, acknowledging both its cultural distinctiveness and its survival under adversity. Contemporary taekkyeon is practiced primarily for cultural preservation and sport demonstration rather than self-defense or competition, with several thousand practitioners registered with the Korea Taekkyeon Association.

## Ssireum: Korean Wrestling Tradition

Ssireum (씨름, also spelled *ssirum*) is Korea's indigenous wrestling tradition, documented in historical records from the Three Kingdoms period and prominently featured in Joseon dynastic records. Unlike Greco-Roman or freestyle wrestling emphasizing takedowns and pins, ssireum employs a cloth belt (satba, 샅바) worn around the waist and thigh, with wrestlers grasping opponents' belts for leverage and control. The sport emphasizes explosive motion, balance, and hip technique; the objective is to force an opponent's shoulders or hips to the ground. Ssireum tournaments (씨름대회, *ssirum daehoe*) are held during major festivals including Chuseok (harvest festival) and Seollal (lunar new year); the most prestigious is the Korean Ssireum League and national championship. In a notable act of inter-Korean cultural cooperation, ssireum was jointly nominated by South Korea and North Korea and inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2018—the only jointly submitted inscription by the two Koreas, reflecting ssireum's shared cultural significance across the peninsula. Despite deep cultural roots and historical prominence, ssireum has declined as a popular sport over the 20th–21st centuries, facing competition from international wrestling styles, modern sports culture, and demographic changes. Professional ssireum leagues operate with limited sponsorship and viewership compared to baseball, football, or esports. Revival efforts by the Korean Ssireum Association and government support for preservation have had modest impact on participation or international recognition.

## Martial Arts in Korean Education and Society

Taekwondo and other martial arts are integrated into Korean school physical-education curricula, particularly in elementary and middle schools. Many Korean elementary schools include mandatory or elective taekwondo instruction, with formal grading and belt-promotion systems incorporated into school athletics. The discipline is valued for its character-development emphasis, alignment with Confucian virtues, and contribution to national identity formation. Private taekwondo dojang throughout Korea serve as major after-school (hagwon-style) activity centers for youth, competing with academic tutoring, music lessons, and other enrichment activities for children's time and family resources. Adult participation in martial arts remains significant, with taekwondo and other systems serving fitness, self-defense, and stress-relief functions. Corporate and military taekwondo programs use the martial art for team-building and leadership development.

The gender dynamics of martial-arts participation in Korea have shifted significantly since the 1990s. Historically male-dominated, taekwondo has gradually attracted female practitioners; this trend accelerated following female athletes' Olympic successes (particularly South Korea's dominance in women's Olympic taekwondo competition). Women now comprise approximately 30–40% of casual practitioners in Korea and higher percentages internationally in some age groups. However, leadership and instructor positions remain disproportionately male, and female martial artists report persistent gender-based challenges including differential treatment, sexualization, and underrepresentation in advanced coaching. The Korean Taekwondo Association has initiated gender-equity programs, though progress has been gradual.

## Olympic Taekwondo and Competition Structure

Olympic taekwondo features sparring competition in weight-based categories (currently eight for men and eight for women, with specific kilogram divisions). Athletes score points by striking the torso (center of the chest and abdomen) with kicks or fists, with bonus points for head kicks. Modern Olympic rules (updated through 2024) emphasize scoring transparency through electronic headgear and body protectors that register valid strikes above certain force thresholds. Rules have evolved significantly since 2000; early iterations allowed excessive contact and head strikes, leading to safety concerns and rule modifications. Contemporary competitions feature time controls (match duration typically 3 × 2-minute rounds for adults) and automatic point deduction for rule violations (holding, fleeing the ring, excessive clinching).

Korean taekwondo athletes have achieved extraordinary Olympic success: South Korea ranks first or second globally in historical Olympic taekwondo medal counts. Korean athletes dominate women's competition particularly, with South Korean women winning multiple gold medals at each Olympic Games since 2000. This dominance reflects Korea's investment in systematic training infrastructure, government support for elite athletes, and cultural prestige surrounding the sport. Major Korean taekwondo organizations including the Korean Taekwondo Association and National Sports Organization run national team programs with dedicated coaching and sports-science support. International competition also includes the World Taekwondo Championships (held annually), World Cups, and continental championships, in which Korean athletes remain consistently highly ranked.

## Competitive Dynamics and Controversies

Competition between the World Taekwondo (formerly WTF) and International Taekwon-Do Federation has created historical fragmentation. The ITF, founded and long led by General Choi Hong-hi, maintained headquarters in Vienna and eventually developed close ties with North Korea (Choi's late-life interests); the ITF emphasizes traditional forms and self-defense applications alongside sport competition. The WTF, based in South Korea and later renamed World Taekwondo, achieved Olympic recognition and commercial dominance, making it the de facto international authority. Practitioners and organizations must choose allegiance, and federation differences affect international ranking recognition, competition participation, and commercial opportunities. Recent years have seen some reconciliation efforts and moves toward organizational transparency and governance reform in World Taekwondo.

Sport taekwondo has faced periodic criticisms regarding rule clarity and scoring consistency. Electronic scoring systems have enhanced objectivity but introduced technological dependency; periodic malfunctions or disputed decisions have generated controversy. Complaints about excessive head-contact injury risk led to rule changes (since 2019, headgear with electronic sensors and mandatory protective equipment); however, concerns about long-term neurological effects persist. Doping scandals, though less frequent than in many sports, have occurred among elite competitors. Coaching ethics and athlete welfare have also drawn scrutiny, particularly regarding intense training regimens, pressure on youth competitors, and the balance between competitive success and athlete well-being.

## Martial Arts and Masculinity

Korean martial arts, particularly taekwondo, have historically been associated with masculinity and martial virtue. Early promotion emphasized warrior identity, discipline, and physical toughness aligned with masculine ideals. The integration of martial arts into military training and the sport's association with national pride (through Olympic success) further reinforced masculine associations. Contemporary Korean martial arts culture continues to emphasize hierarchy, respect for authority, and disciplined training, values overlapping with traditional Korean masculinity concepts. However, the increasing participation of women and the sport's evolution toward technical sophistication and strategic gameplay rather than raw power has complicated gender associations. Scholarship on Korean masculinity notes that martial-arts participation can reflect diverse masculine identities, from aggressive competition to disciplined self-improvement to aesthetic appreciation of technique.

## Gumdo and Korean Sword Arts

Gumdo (검도, also rendered *kumdo* in older romanization) is the Korean equivalent of Japanese kendo, both deriving from a shared tradition of Japanese sword fencing and the samurai martial arts of the Edo period. Gumdo uses bamboo practice swords (죽도, *jukdo*) and full-body protective armor (호구, *hogu*) for sparring competitions governed by the Korean Sword Art Association (대한검도회, *Daehan Gumdo Hoe*). It is practiced widely across Korea and internationally, often taught alongside taekwondo in martial arts schools. Korean gumdo and Japanese kendo share overlapping technique sets and governance through related international federations (the International Kendo Federation governs both nationally affiliated organizations). Some Korean gumdo practitioners emphasize distinctly Korean historical origins; scholars note that the technical corpus is substantially shared with Japanese kendo, though modern Korean instructors have introduced variations and cultural framing. Haidong Gumdo (해동검도), founded in the 1980s, represents a newer Korean sword art system claiming distinct historical roots separate from kendo and featuring flowing sword-drawing (iai-style) techniques alongside forms and competition sparring.

## Traditional Korean Combat Systems and Archaeological Evidence

Korean historical records document martial traditions predating modern taekwondo. The Hwarang (화랑) of Silla kingdom (6th–10th centuries) were warrior-scholar elites trained in combat, riding, and philosophy; historical accounts describe unarmed fighting (수박, *subak*) and weaponry. The Muyedobotongji (무예도보통지, 1790), an illustrated compendium of Korean military arts commissioned during the Joseon dynasty, systematically documented 24 armed and unarmed combat disciplines, providing the most comprehensive historical record of pre-modern Korean martial arts; it remains an important reference for modern reconstructionists. Joseon dynasty sources document martial competitions and hand-to-hand combat training among soldiers and nobility. However, direct technical transmission from these historical systems to modern taekwondo is difficult to verify; much of the claimed lineage may be retrospective construction by 20th-century martial artists seeking historical legitimacy. Archaeological evidence for Korean martial techniques is limited; most historical documentation comes from literary accounts rather than visual or physical evidence of actual techniques.
