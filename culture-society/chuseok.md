# Chuseok

Chuseok (추석), also known as *Hangawi* (한가위), is Korea's most important traditional holiday and the cultural centerpiece of Korean family life. Celebrated on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, Chuseok marks the autumn harvest and is a time for families to gather, pay respects to ancestors, share food, and renew kinship bonds. The holiday spans three days officially (the day before, the day itself, and the day after), though its cultural observance extends before and after these dates. With comparable significance to Christmas in Western cultures or Chinese New Year in East Asian cultures, Chuseok is deeply embedded in Korean identity and society, reflected in literature, film, social practice, and national policy. In December 2013, UNESCO inscribed *Kimjang* (김장), the communal kimchi-making tradition closely associated with Chuseok preparations, on its Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The *Ganggangsullae* circle dance performed on Chuseok night was inscribed separately in 2009.

## Etymology and Historical Origins

The name "Chuseok" (추석) derives from *chu* (추, autumn) and *seok* (석, evening/night), literally referring to the evening of mid-autumn. An alternative name, *Hangawi* (한가위), is of uncertain etymology but may derive from Old Korean *Hangarim*, suggesting "great middle" or "great beginning," referring to the mid-point of autumn. Historical records indicate that harvest festivals have been celebrated on the Korean peninsula since antiquity, predating recorded history. Ancient Goguryeo is documented to have held autumn festivals involving music, dance, and communal feasting, and these practices were likely present in other early Korean kingdoms.

During the Three Kingdoms period (57 BCE–668 CE), seasonal festivals aligned with agricultural cycles held religious and social significance. The practice of honoring ancestors by visiting their graves and making offerings of new-harvest foods became systematized during the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910), when Neo-Confucian filial piety (*hyo*, 효) was elevated to the core of state ideology and personal ethics. Chuseok evolved into a formalized occasion for performing ancestral rites and reinforcing family hierarchy and kinship networks.

## The Chuseok Holiday Period

**Official Calendar Status**: Chuseok is a national public holiday in South Korea (and North Korea, though North Korean observance differs). In 2007, South Korea officially designated Chuseok as an important cultural day, and since then, the government has granted a three-day holiday period (one day before Chuseok, Chuseok itself, and one day after). In years when Chuseok falls on a weekend, substitute holidays are often added. This official recognition reflects Chuseok's status as central to national cultural identity.

**Lunar Calendar Observance**: Unlike holidays tied to the Gregorian calendar (such as New Year's Day or Christmas), Chuseok is observed according to the lunar calendar, placing it on September–October in Gregorian terms. The exact Gregorian date varies annually. Recent dates include: 17 September 2024, 6 October 2025, and 25 September 2026. This lunar observance reflects Korean cultural continuity with pre-modern East Asian timekeeping and agricultural cycles.

**Extended Observance Period**: While the official holiday is three days, the cultural observance of Chuseok extends for approximately two weeks. Many Koreans begin preparations a month or more in advance. The weeks immediately before Chuseok see heightened activity in food preparation, cleaning, and family arrangements. The weeks after include continued family visits, ancestor veneration, and leisure activities.

## Central Practices and Rituals

**Ancestral Rites (Jesa)**: The core ritual of Chuseok is *jesa* (제사), the ceremony of honoring deceased family members. Typically performed on the morning of Chuseok or the day before, jesa follows formalized procedures rooted in Confucian ritual practice. Family members (traditionally led by the eldest son or male family head) gather before a household altar or at an ancestor's grave. They arrange offerings of food and drink on a low table—typically including new-harvest rice and vegetables, meat dishes, fruits, and ritual beverages (wine or tea). Family members bow in a prescribed sequence (the formal *jeol*, 절, a kneeling bow), and the offerings are presented with verbal prayers or silent meditation honoring the ancestor. After the ritual, the family shares the food as a communal meal, symbolically including the ancestors in the family gathering.

In modern practice, jesa has become less rigidly formalized; many younger or urbanized families perform simplified versions. Some families also visit cemeteries to clean the grass around ancestral graves and place offerings, a practice called *beolcho* (벌초).

**Visits to Ancestral Homes and Family Gatherings**: Central to Chuseok is the return migration of Koreans to their hometowns and ancestral family homes. For many Koreans living in cities, Chuseok is the primary occasion for returning to rural villages or provincial towns where their families originated. This mass migration creates one of the world's largest periodic population movements; during Chuseok, roads and trains from Seoul to provincial areas become extraordinarily congested, and return migration causes temporary depopulation of cities. This practice reinforces family bonds, kinship networks, and connections to ancestral lands—even in an increasingly urbanized Korea where fewer people maintain permanent rural residences.

**Kimjang (Communal Kimchi-Making)**: Associated particularly with the weeks before Chuseok is *kimjang* (김장), the communal preparation of kimchi (fermented cabbage) in large quantities for winter storage. Traditionally, extended families and neighbors gathered to prepare dozens of jars of kimchi to sustain households through the cold months. This labor-intensive process involved purchasing and preparing vegetables, preparing the fermentation paste, and collectively assembling finished kimchi. Kimjang was both a practical food-preservation necessity and a social occasion for women (who traditionally led the effort) to gather, share knowledge, and strengthen community bonds. Even in modern urban Korea, where individual households can purchase commercial kimchi, the tradition persists in attenuated form; some families maintain small-scale kimjang gatherings, and the practice is celebrated culturally as emblematic of Korean food heritage. UNESCO's 2013 inscription of kimjang as Intangible Cultural Heritage has elevated its profile as a marker of Korean cultural identity.

**Food and Feasting**: Chuseok feasting is central to the holiday. Families prepare numerous dishes, many made from new-harvest ingredients. Traditional Chuseok foods include:

- **Songpyeon** (송편): A crescent-shaped rice cake filled with sesame seeds, chestnuts, or red bean paste, made freshly for Chuseok
- **Beef and vegetable dishes**: Grilled beef, vegetable side dishes (*namul*, 나물), and soups
- **New-harvest rice and grains**: The first rice from the autumn harvest is traditionally used for special dishes and ritual offerings
- **Fruits**: Fresh fruits from the season, such as persimmons, pears, grapes, and apples
- **Tteokguk** (떡국): A soup with sliced rice cakes, often eaten on New Year but also prepared for Chuseok
- **Alcoholic beverages**: Traditional rice wine (*makgeolli*, 막걸리) or distilled spirits (*soju*, 소주) accompany meals

The preparation of these foods involves labor-intensive cooking, often beginning days before Chuseok. In traditional practice, women of the household led food preparation, and the activity was both a family task and a social event involving neighbors and extended family helping one another. The Chuseok feast brings family members together around abundant food, reinforcing kinship and celebrating the harvest.

**Games and Entertainment**: Chuseok includes various recreational activities and games, particularly among children and youth. Traditional games include:

- **Ssireum** (씨름): Traditional Korean wrestling, often held as community competitions during Chuseok
- **Yutnori** (윷놀이): A board game played with four-sided sticks, involving luck and strategy, traditionally played by families during Chuseok
- **Ganggangsullae** (강강술래): A circle dance performed by women, involving rhythmic call-and-response singing and dancing traditionally done under the full moon on Chuseok night; inscribed by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2009
- **Kite flying**: Children fly hand-made kites (*yeon*, 연), such as the rectangular *bangpae-yeon* (방패연, "shield kite"), on open fields
- **Archery and martial games**: In some regions, traditional martial competitions are held

In modern times, these games have become less common in urban settings, though they are revived in cultural festivals and among those maintaining traditional practices.

## Chuseok in Modern South Korea

**Economic and Social Impact**: Chuseok represents the most significant domestic holiday in South Korea's calendar, with massive economic and social ramifications. The three-day holiday and surrounding period account for approximately 8–10% of Korea's annual retail sales, as families purchase gifts (ginseng, premium fruits, meat, imported goods), new clothes, and travel services. Ginseng-and-premium-fruit hampers commonly priced at 50,000–300,000 won (≈ US$40–230) are the most popular gift formats; department stores like Lotte and Hyundai launch pre-order campaigns six to eight weeks in advance. Korea Railroad (KORAIL) sells hundreds of thousands of reserved seats within minutes of the booking window opening. Road traffic between Seoul and provincial cities such as Busan and Gwangju can turn a two-hour drive into an eight-to-ten-hour journey. The return migration and family gathering activities generate surge demand for transportation (rail, highway, aviation), accommodations, and hospitality services. A significant portion of Korea's "gift culture" centers on Chuseok, with elaborate gift packages (*선물세트*, gift sets) exchanged among friends, colleagues, and family members.

The holiday also has psychological and social importance. For many Koreans, particularly migrant workers and those estranged from rural hometowns, Chuseok represents an emotional anchor to family identity and Korean cultural continuity. The obligation to return home for Chuseok remains strong in Korean society, with social expectation that adult children (even those living far away) make the effort to gather with family. Failure to return for Chuseok can carry social stigma or familial tension.

**Urban and Rural Dimensions**: The traditional rural character of Chuseok has shifted with urbanization. As fewer Koreans maintain permanent rural residences and ancestral lands, Chuseok observance has become more concentrated in cities. Urban families may return to ancestral villages for the holiday but maintain primary residences in cities. Some urban families perform jesa at home rather than at graves. Newer, younger Koreans (particularly those in their 20s–30s) may resist the heavy social expectations surrounding Chuseok, seeing the mandatory family gathering and expense as outdated or burdensome. This generational shift has led to declining participation in some traditional practices, though the holiday remains culturally significant.

**Commercial and State Promotion**: The South Korean government actively promotes Chuseok as a cultural marker of national identity. Tourism boards develop Chuseok-themed events, museums curate exhibitions on Chuseok traditions, and cultural organizations sponsor performances of traditional music and dance during the holiday period. This state promotion reflects Chuseok's role in national cultural strategy and soft power. Commercial enterprises capitalize on Chuseok as a major sales and marketing opportunity; department stores, supermarkets, and restaurants launch special Chuseok promotions and limited-edition products.

**Gender and Family Dynamics**: Chuseok's traditional structure places significant labor on women, particularly wives and daughters-in-law responsible for cooking, food preparation, and household management. Feminist scholars and social critics have noted that while Chuseok celebrates family bonds, it often results in women working intensively behind the scenes. Younger Korean women have increasingly questioned or negotiated the expected gender roles, leading to gradual changes in how Chuseok labor is distributed. Some families now share cooking responsibilities more equally or hire catering services rather than relying on unpaid domestic labor.

## Chuseok in North Korea and the Diaspora

North Korea observes a holiday called *Chusok* (추석) on the same lunar calendar date, though observance differs significantly from South Korea. The North Korean state has historically discouraged ancestor veneration as a feudal practice contrary to socialist ideology, though some private family observances persist. In recent decades, North Korean state media has occasionally highlighted Chuseok in nationalist terms, emphasizing Korean cultural continuity.

Korean diaspora communities worldwide maintain Chuseok observance, though practices vary by diaspora context. Korean communities in the United States, China, Japan, and Southeast Asia organize Chuseok festivals and family gatherings, adapting traditions to local circumstances. For diaspora Koreans, Chuseok often functions as a marker of cultural identity and connection to the homeland.

## Significance and Cultural Meaning

Chuseok represents several intersecting layers of Korean cultural meaning:

1. **Agricultural and Seasonal**: Chuseok marks the autumn harvest, connecting Korean society to agricultural cycles and natural rhythms. Even in modern urbanized Korea, the holiday maintains this seasonal significance.

2. **Filial Piety and Ancestor Veneration**: Rooted in Confucian values, Chuseok embodies the principle of *hyo* (filial piety), structuring family relationships and moral obligations across generations. The ritual honoring of ancestors reinforces kinship continuity and family hierarchy.

3. **Family and Kinship**: Chuseok is quintessentially a family holiday, emphasizing the bonds between family members across generations and geographic distances. In a rapidly urbanizing and increasingly mobile society, Chuseok serves as a periodic anchor for family unity.

4. **National Cultural Identity**: Chuseok functions as a symbol of Korean cultural distinctiveness and continuity. The holiday appears frequently in Korean literature, film, and art as emblematic of Korean identity. International promotion of Chuseok contributes to Korea's cultural soft power and global cultural recognition.

5. **Gender and Social Dynamics**: Chuseok reveals and reinforces gender roles, family structures, and social hierarchies. The holiday has been a site of feminist critique and negotiation around women's roles and family obligations.

6. **Modernity and Tradition**: Chuseok embodies the tension in modern Korea between traditional practices and contemporary life. Urban families navigate how to maintain traditional observances in settings far from ancestral homes and villages, leading to creative adaptations and selective adoption of practices.

## Chuseok and the Hallyu Wave

As Korean popular culture gained international traction through K-dramas, K-pop, and films, Chuseok traditions have traveled with it. K-drama episodes regularly build plot around Chuseok family reunions, in-law tensions, and the preparation of songpyeon, giving international audiences their first exposure to the holiday. Tourism South Korea (Korea Tourism Organization) actively markets Chuseok-period cultural programs to foreign visitors, who can observe ancestral rites re-enactments, learn to make songpyeon, and watch ganggangsullae performances at venues such as the National Folk Museum in Seoul and the Gyeongju Historic Areas. [Gyeongju](../geography/gyeongju.md), the ancient Silla capital, draws particularly large Chuseok crowds as Koreans visit ancestral and Buddhist heritage sites during the holiday.
