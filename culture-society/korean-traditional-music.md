# Korean Traditional Music and Performing Arts

Korean traditional music, known as **gugak** (국악, literally "national music"), encompasses the indigenous musical and performative arts of Korea that developed from ancient times through the Joseon dynasty and continue into the present. Gugak encompasses court and chamber music (*jeongak*), folk and village music (*minyo* and *pansori*), instrumental traditions played on traditional Korean instruments, and various performative art forms including dance, narrative recitation, and ritual music. Distinct from both Western classical music and contemporary K-pop, gugak represents Korea's living musical heritage, maintained through oral transmission, formal training institutions, and periodic government designation as Intangible Cultural Properties. Korean traditional music is characterized by pentatonic and hexatonic modal scales, complex rhythmic cycles, expressive ornamentation, and the deep integration of music with language, emotion, and narrative.

## Historical Foundations and Court Music

Korean musical traditions reach back to the ancient kingdoms. Archaeological evidence from tombs and historical records in the *samguk sagi* (Historical Records of the Three Kingdoms) document musical instruments, court orchestras, and ritual music in Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla. The Three Kingdoms played various wind and percussion instruments including *daegeum* (large bamboo flutes), *gayageum* (zither-like plucked strings), and *janggu* (hourglass drum), some of which developed through exchange with Central Asia and China. Silla's flourishing, particularly under the Unified Silla period (668–935), saw the development of court music and the adoption and adaptation of musical traditions from China and Central Asia.

During the Goryeo period (918–1392), court music (*jeongak*) flourished as an elite cultural practice. The royal court maintained large orchestras, and musical performance was integral to state ceremonies, banquets, and entertainment. Goryeo court music drew from multiple traditions — indigenous Korean, Chinese, and Central Asian — creating a cosmopolitan musical culture that reflected the kingdom's international reach. Goryeo also saw the development of new instruments and the refinement of musical theory.

The Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) institutionalized court music and elite musical training. The royal court maintained the National Institute for Music Training (*Jangakwon*), and Confucian scholars codified musical theory and repertoire. The Sejong the Great era (1418–1450) witnessed a flourishing of musical innovation; Sejong commissioned court musicians to compose new works and compile musical scores, and he personally contributed to musical theory, devising the *jeongganbo* staff notation system around 1447 — one of the world's earliest proportional rhythmic notation systems, distinct from Western staff notation. The *Akhak kwebom* (악학궤범, Guide to the Study of Music), compiled in 1493 under King Seongjong under the direction of the scholar-official Seong Hyeon, became one of the most comprehensive musical treatises of the East Asian world, documenting court music, instruments, theory, costumes, and performance practice across ten volumes. This systematization established court music as an elite, canonical tradition, distinct from and often elevated above folk music by Confucian scholars.

Joseon court music included orchestral genres for ritual (such as music for ancestral rites and royal ceremonies), entertainment music for banquets and court occasions, and chamber music for intimate settings. Instruments were classified by material (silk strings, bamboo, wood, metal) and by Confucian categorization. The role of court musicians was highly regulated; they were professional servants of the state, trained from childhood and expected to master complex repertoires. Court music was transmitted through lineages of professional musicians and through the rare written scores that have survived.

## Traditional Instruments and Instrumental Genres

Korean traditional music features a distinctive palette of instruments, many unique to Korea or significantly adapted from Chinese or Central Asian models. The **gayageum** (가야금), a 12-string plucked zither of ancient Korean origins, is one of the most revered traditional instruments. According to legend, it was created during the Gaya confederacy and was named after Gaya; historical evidence suggests it developed during the Three Kingdoms period. The gayageum produces warm, resonant tones and is capable of subtle pitch bending and expressive techniques; it is played both solo and in ensemble settings and is central to many chamber music and narrative genres.

The **daegeum** (대금), a large transverse bamboo flute, is played in court music, folk ensembles, and contemporary fusion contexts. The daegeum produces mellow, rich tones and is valued for its expressive range and ability to convey emotional nuance. Other important wind instruments include the **danso** (단소), a vertical bamboo flute with a warm, intimate tone, the **haegeum** (해금), a two-string bowed spike fiddle that produces vocal-like, expressive sounds, and the **taepyeongso** (태평소), a conical oboe-like double-reed instrument with a bright, penetrating sound traditionally used in folk ensembles and outdoor celebrations.

Percussion instruments are essential to Korean music. The **janggu** (장구), an hourglass-shaped double-headed drum played with two mallets of different materials, serves as the rhythmic foundation for many genres and is valued for the timbral contrast between its two heads. The **buk** (북), a cylindrical drum often struck with wooden mallets, provides deep bass support. The **sogo** (소고), a small hand drum, is featured prominently in percussion ensembles and folk performance. The **kkwaenggwari** (꽹과리), a small handheld gong, and the larger **jing** (징) gong provide the metallic timbres of outdoor percussion music. The **samulnori** (사물놀이) ensemble, a late-20th-century creation drawing on four traditional percussion instruments (*kkwaenggwari*, *jing*, *janggu*, and *buk*), has become iconic both domestically and internationally as a representation of Korean percussion virtuosity.

## Vocal and Narrative Traditions

Korean vocal music encompasses several distinct genres, each with specific aesthetic and performative characteristics. **Pansori** (판소리) is a narrative vocal form in which a solo singer accompanied by a single drummer recites an epic or dramatic story over the course of hours, employing vocal expression, bodily gesture, and rhythmic cycles (*jangdan*) to convey emotion and narrative development. Pansori emerged in the 17th or 18th century from folk traditions, likely performed by itinerant storytellers and entertainers, and developed into a formalized art form with a repertoire of five surviving canonical pieces (the *pansori daseot madang*, 판소리 다섯 마당): *Chunhyangga* (the romance of Chunhyang and Yi Mong-nyong), *Heungbuga* (the tale of the virtuous Heungbu), *Simcheongga* (the filial devotion of Simcheong), *Jeokbyeokga* (the Red Cliff battle), and *Sugungga* (the underwater kingdom of the Sea King). Each pansori piece requires mastery of complex vocal techniques, rhythmic precision, and emotional interpretation, and great pansori masters are revered as artists of the highest caliber.

**Minyo** (민요, folk songs) represent the vernacular songs sung by common people in villages and fields, transmitted orally across generations. Minyo express the sentiments of agricultural labor, love, sorrow, celebration, and daily life. Famous minyo traditions include **Arirang**, perhaps Korea's best-known folk melody, sung in many regional variations such as *Jeongseon Arirang* and *Jindo Arirang*, and regional song families such as the southwestern *namdo* minyo of the Jeolla region, known for emotional depth and ornamentation. Regional styles are broadly grouped by their characteristic modes and vocal timbre — the *gyeongto* songs of the Seoul–Gyeonggi area, the *seodo* songs of the northwest, and the *namdo* songs of the southwest.

**Sijo** (시조) refers to both a poetic form and a vocal music tradition. Sijo poetry, typically three lines of 10 or 11 syllables each, was an elite literary form during the Joseon dynasty. When sung, sijo is performed in a refined, ornamented style with rhythmic cycles, traditionally accompanied by instruments. Sijo singing requires precise understanding of both poetic meaning and musical phrasing, and the tradition has been maintained by specialists and lovers of classical poetry.

## Rhythmic Cycles and Musical Theory

A distinctive feature of Korean traditional music is the concept of **jangdan** (장단), rhythmic cycles or metric patterns that structure performance. Unlike Western metered music with regular beat divisions, jangdan patterns are complex, asymmetrical cycles that may stretch over many beats. The slowest and most expansive jangdan, **jinyangjo** (진양조), unfolds over a long cycle grouped in units of six beats and is used for the most contemplative passages. Progressively faster patterns include **jungmori** (중모리), **jungjungmori** (중중모리), **jajinmori** (자진모리), and the rapid **hwimori** (휘모리), while **gutgeori** (굿거리) and **semachi** (세마치) are lilting triple-metre patterns common in folk song and dance. Musicians internalize these rhythmic patterns and use them flexibly, allowing for rubato and expressive timing within an overarching rhythmic structure. The rhythmic sophistication of Korean music, particularly in ensemble contexts, requires intensive training and cultural familiarity.

Korean music traditionally employs pentatonic scales, creating a tonal palette distinct from Western major and minor tonality. Rather than fixed keys, performances are organized around modes (*jo*) — chiefly the bright, balanced *pyeongjo* and the plaintive, sorrowful *gyemyeonjo* — each defined by its characteristic pitches, ornaments, and emotional register. Microtonal inflections, pitch-bending, and the intense, wavering vibrato known as *nonghyeon* (on strings) or *yoseong* (in the voice) are central to expressive performance, particularly in vocal and bowed-string traditions.

## Ritual, Ceremonial, and Theatrical Contexts

Korean traditional music is integral to ritual and ceremonial life. **Confucian ritual music** accompanies state ancestral rites, most notably **Jongmyo jeryeak** (종묘제례악), the music and dance performed at the royal ancestral shrine of the Joseon kings, and the Confucian shrine music **Munmyo jeryeak** (문묘제례악). These employ specific instrumentation and repertoires associated with solemn, dignified occasions; Jongmyo jeryeak was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2001. The music for royal court ceremonies, documented in historical texts, reflects elaborate orchestrations designed to enhance the dignity and cosmic significance of state functions.

**Shamanic music** (*kut* music) accompanies shamanic rituals and ceremonies, employing percussion instruments and vocalization to support ritual action, spirit possession, and community participation. Shamanic drumming and music are often ecstatic and participatory, creating the sonic environment for spiritual engagement.

**Mask dance drama** (*tal-chum* or *talnori*), a theatrical tradition performing social satire and entertainment, employs live instrumental accompaniment. Performers wearing stylized masks enact comedic or dramatic scenes, often critiquing social hierarchy and elite pretension. The music for mask dance drama is typically folk-based and uses percussion-led ensembles.

## Renowned Practitioners and Living Treasures

The Intangible Cultural Properties system has elevated specific masters to national prominence. **Shin Jae-hyo** (1812–1884) is the towering historical figure of pansori, responsible for systematizing and refining the five canonical pansori pieces, codifying performance standards, and training the next generation. His influence shaped all subsequent pansori practice. Among modern pansori masters, **Park Dong-jin** (1916–2003), designated National Intangible Cultural Property No. 5, was revered for his mastery of the *Chunhyangga* and *Heungbuga* pieces. **Im Bang-ul** (1905–1961) is considered one of the most technically accomplished pansori voices of the 20th century.

In gayageum, **Hwang Byung-ki** (황병기, 1936–2018) was a preeminent figure who both preserved the classical repertoire and composed original contemporary works for the instrument, significantly broadening gayageum's expressive range and contemporary relevance. His compositions, including *Sup* (숲, The Forest, 1963) and *Chimhyangmu* (침향무, 1974), are now part of the modern Korean music canon. Composer-performers such as **Won Il** have likewise bridged traditional gugak and contemporary and fusion performance.

## Modern Preservation, Education, and Institutionalization

The Korean government's designation of traditional music forms as Intangible Cultural Properties (Intangible Cultural Assets) has been crucial to preservation. The 1962 Cultural Properties Protection Act established the Intangible Cultural Properties system; subsequent designations have recognized the greatest living masters (*boyuja*, holders) and traditions worth preserving. Pansori was designated National Intangible Cultural Property No. 5 in 1964, *gayageum sanjo* (solo gayageum) No. 23 in 1968, and numerous other traditions have received protection and funding. The system supports master performers, archives recordings, and funds training of younger generations; as of the 2020s, dozens of gugak traditions are designated.

Educational institutions have formalized the study of gugak. Universities offer degrees in traditional music, preserving and advancing the discipline. The **Seoul National University Department of Korean Music** (국악과), founded in 1959, is a leading institution. The **National Gugak Center** (국립국악원), established in 1951 and descended from the Joseon dynasty's Jangakwon, is the primary state institution for research, documentation, and performance of traditional music; it employs professional musicians, houses archives, and presents public concerts. The **Korean National University of Arts** (한국예술종합학교, K-Arts), founded in 1993, includes a School of Korean Traditional Arts offering rigorous conservatory-level training. Private academies and conservatories also teach gugak instrumentals and vocal traditions.

The Korean Broadcasting System (KBS) and other media have supported traditional music through documentation, broadcasts, and concert programming. Televised performances and festivals have brought traditional music to wider audiences, though the mediation of live performance through broadcast technology changes the listening experience.

## Contemporary Practice and Fusion

While traditional music repertoire and performance practice remain largely conserved, contemporary practitioners have also explored fusion and creative reinterpretation. The rise of **samulnori** in the late 1970s demonstrated how traditional instruments could be adapted for new aesthetic and performative contexts. Samulnori was created in 1978 by a quartet of percussionists led by Kim Duk-soo as a stage ensemble drawing on the *pungmul* (풍물) and *nongak* (농악) outdoor percussion traditions of farming communities. By removing the street-procession context and placing musicians in a seated concert format, samulnori transformed folk percussion into a concert art form. It became internationally successful in the 1980s and 1990s, touring globally and introducing Korean percussion virtuosity — particularly the contrasting timbres of the *kkwaenggwari* gong, *jing* gong, *janggu*, and *buk* — to world music audiences.

Contemporary composers and musicians have incorporated gugak elements into concert compositions, fusion ensembles, and experimental works. Musicians trained in both traditional and Western classical music sometimes create hybrid compositions. Film and theater composers draw on gugak instrumentation and aesthetic for soundtracks. Young musicians have formed fusion ensembles blending gugak with jazz, rock, and world music.

The dominance of K-pop, Western popular music, and global music culture has created a context in which gugak occupies a distinct but minority cultural niche in contemporary South Korea. Younger urban Koreans typically have less exposure to gugak than previous generations, though cultural education and heritage initiatives aim to sustain interest. Gugak remains vibrant in concert halls, academic settings, and among devoted practitioners and audiences, but is not the dominant musical culture for most young Koreans.

## International Recognition and Cultural Diplomacy

UNESCO's recognition of Korean traditional music forms as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity has elevated their international profile. **Pansori** was proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003 and formally incorporated into the Representative List in 2008. The **Gangneung Danoje** festival — which incorporates ritual music and performance — was inscribed in 2005. The **Namsadang Nori** (wandering performer troupe), which combines acrobatics, mask dance, percussion, and musical performance, followed in 2009, and **Gagok** (가곡, lyric song cycles for voice and chamber ensemble) in 2010; **Jongmyo jeryeak** had been among the first inscriptions in 2001. The **jeongganbo** notation system has been highlighted in international musicological scholarship. International tours by traditional music ensembles have introduced gugak to global audiences. Korean cultural organizations actively promote traditional music abroad as part of soft power and cultural diplomacy efforts.

Traditional music figures prominently in Korea's international cultural presentations; performances at Olympic ceremonies, world expos, and cultural festivals showcase gugak to global audiences. This internationalization has both benefits (funding, prestige, sustained interest) and challenges (potential decontextualization, commodification, and loss of the social and ritual contexts in which the music traditionally flourished).

## Legacy and Cultural Significance

Korean traditional music represents one of the world's sophisticated and historically deep musical traditions, with continuity from ancient kingdoms to the present. The preservation and continued practice of gugak by musicians, scholars, and government institutions ensures that this heritage remains alive. For Korean cultural identity, gugak embodies a sense of national heritage and artistic excellence distinct from both Western and Chinese musical traditions. The complex rhythmic systems, the integration of music with narrative and ritual, and the aesthetic emphasis on subtle emotional expression through ornamentation and phrasing represent distinctive Korean cultural values and artistic principles that continue to resonate with practitioners and audiences who encounter traditional music.
