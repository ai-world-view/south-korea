# Korean Drama Industry

**Category**: Culture & Society
**Key figures**: Kim Soo-hyun, Park Bo-gum, Lee Min-ho, Song Joong-ki, Ha Ji-won, Lee Byung-hun, Jeon Ji-hyun (Jun Ji-hyun), Bae Yong-joon, Writer Kim Eun-sook, Director Lee Eung-bok, Director Park Joon-woo

## Summary

The Korean drama industry (K-dramas) is a major component of South Korea's cultural and media export ecosystem, producing episodic television programming that has achieved extraordinary global reach since the early 2000s. Unlike K-pop, which emphasizes music performance and idol-system management, and Korean cinema, which develops feature films through separate production and distribution pipelines, K-dramas center on serialized narrative television—typically 12–24 episodes per series, broadcast weekly on a twice-weekly schedule (Wednesday/Thursday or Saturday/Sunday primetime slots). The industry generates revenue through domestic advertising, international licensing, streaming platform deals, merchandise, tourism, and spin-off consumer products.

### Structure of the Industry

The K-drama industry operates through three overlapping layers:

**Broadcasting Networks** — The "big three" public and semi-public networks—**Korean Broadcasting System (KBS)**, **Seoul Broadcasting System (SBS)**, and **Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation (MBC)**—have operated since the 1960s–1980s and historically dominated drama production and scheduling. Each network runs flagship drama timeslots: KBS's "Weekend Drama" and "Monday–Tuesday Drama" slots, SBS's "Friday–Saturday Drama," and MBC's "Monday–Tuesday Drama" are highly competitive placements that attract major talent. Public broadcasting networks are subject to Korea Communications Commission oversight and must meet content standards; this has occasionally shaped drama content toward family-friendly themes.

**Cable and Pay-TV Channels** — From the late 1990s onward, cable channels expanded the K-drama market by enabling content outside public broadcast constraints. **tvN** (owned by CJ ENM, launched 2006) became the most critically prestigious drama channel of the 2010s, producing acclaimed series such as *Signal* (2016), *My Mister* (2018), *Crash Landing on You* (2019–2020), and *Hometown Cha-Cha-Cha* (2021). **JTBC** (launched 2011) produced breakout series including *Sky Castle* (2018–2019), which achieved a cable ratings record of 23.8% nationwide audience share. **OCN** specialized in genre fare—thriller, sci-fi, horror—enabling content that would have been unlikely on public networks.

**Streaming Platforms** — Netflix launched in South Korea in January 2016 and rapidly transformed the K-drama production and distribution landscape. Netflix's early acquisition strategy (licensing rights to Korean dramas for global streaming) gave Korean content unprecedented simultaneous international exposure. By the late 2010s, Netflix shifted to co-production and original production deals: *Kingdom* (2019), a period zombie thriller, was Netflix Korea's first original Korean drama and demonstrated that genre-driven K-dramas could attract global subscribers. *Squid Game* (2021) eclipsed all prior benchmarks, becoming Netflix's most-watched series to that date within weeks of its September 17, 2021 premiere. Netflix estimated *Squid Game* generated approximately **$891 million in value** (measured by subscriber impact) in its first month, on a production budget of approximately **$21.4 million**. By late 2021, Netflix reported the show had been watched by **111 million households** globally within its first 28 days. Disney+ (Korea launch: November 2021), Apple TV+, and Amazon Prime Video have also entered the Korean content market, intensifying competition for talent and scripts.

### Production Model

K-drama production follows a model distinct from American television in several important respects:

**Live-shoot production** — The majority of K-dramas are produced using a "live-shoot" system (*생방송 드라마*, *saengbangsong drama*) in which episodes are written, shot, and edited on a rolling basis while the series is already airing. A 16-episode drama may begin broadcasting before more than 4–6 episodes have been completed. This system allows writers and producers to respond to audience feedback (measured through real-time ratings and social media), but creates punishing work conditions: actors and crew routinely work 15–20 hour days; writers revise scripts overnight for shoots scheduled the next morning. Industry labor advocates have documented widespread sleep deprivation, health crises, and occasional deaths related to overwork. Calls for pre-production models (completed before broadcast) have increased, and Netflix's international co-productions have largely moved to pre-production, but live-shoot remains common for domestic broadcast dramas.

**Star System and Casting** — Lead casting is one of the most consequential decisions in K-drama production. Top-tier actors ("Hallyu stars") command salaries of ₩100–200 million (approximately $75,000–$150,000) per episode, with full-series contracts for major productions reaching ₩2–4 billion ($1.5–3 million). The casting of a recognized star (especially one with established Japanese or Chinese fan bases) substantially affects international pre-sale licensing values. Agencies including **SM Entertainment**, **YG Entertainment**, **HYBE**, **KeyEast**, and numerous mid-size management companies negotiate talent deals with production companies.

**Screenwriter Power** — Unlike American television's writer's room model, Korean dramas are typically written by a single head writer (*메인 작가*), who may have one or more assistant writers. Head writers with strong track records—**Kim Eun-sook** (*Descendants of the Sun*, *Goblin*, *Mr. Sunshine*; *The Glory*), **Park Ji-eun** (*My Love from the Star*, *Crash Landing on You*), **Noh Hee-kyung** (*It's Okay, That's Love*; *Our Blues*), and **Kim Hee-won** among others—command very high fees and significant creative control. Scripts from established writers are acquired through competitive bidding by multiple broadcasters simultaneously.

### Historical Development

**1980s–1990s: Foundations** — Korean television drama production began in earnest in the 1960s with KBS and MBC dramas. The industry gained sophistication in the 1980s–1990s as production quality increased alongside South Korea's economic development. Politically, democratization after 1987 eased content restrictions. The 1997–1998 Asian Financial Crisis was a turning point: government policy under Kim Dae-jung's "cultural industries" initiative heavily subsidized and promoted the export of Korean cultural products (film, music, drama) as an economic recovery strategy.

**2000s: The First Hallyu Wave** — *Winter Sonata* (KBS2, 2002), a romance drama starring **Bae Yong-joon** and **Choi Ji-woo**, became a cultural phenomenon in Japan in 2003–2004 when broadcast on NHK. Bae Yong-joon's image became so iconic in Japan that he was nicknamed "Yon-sama"; his airport arrivals attracted crowds of thousands of Japanese fans, predominantly women in their 40s–60s. The Japanese "Korea Boom" (*Kankoku Boom*) it triggered generated an estimated ¥10–20 billion ($100–200 million) annually in related tourism, merchandise, and media sales. *Jewel in the Palace* (*Dae Jang Geum*, MBC, 2003–2004) extended pan-Asian K-drama appeal across China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Southeast Asia, reaching estimated viewership of 500 million across Asia.

**2010s: Global Expansion** — The 2010s saw K-drama reach beyond Asia. *Boys Over Flowers* (KBS2, 2009) became one of the first Korean dramas widely watched across Latin America and the Middle East via online streaming. *My Love from the Star* (SBS, 2013–2014) broke viewership records in China with over 2.5 billion video streams on Chinese platforms. *Descendants of the Sun* (KBS2, 2016), the first K-drama shot primarily in pre-production with international deals pre-negotiated, sold to 32 countries before broadcast and was watched by an estimated 800 million viewers across Asia. *Goblin* (tvN, 2016–2017), written by Kim Eun-sook and directed by Lee Eung-bok, delivered tvN's highest ratings at the time (20.5%) and generated massive international streaming.

**2020s: Netflix Era and Global Mainstream** — *Crash Landing on You* (tvN, 2019–2020) starring Hyun Bin and Son Ye-jin became a global sensation via Netflix, contributing to the couple's eventual real-world marriage (announced 2022) generating international media coverage. *D.P.* (Netflix Korea, 2021), an unflinching drama about military deserters, and *Hellbound* (Netflix Korea, 2021) demonstrated Korean drama's range into darker, socially critical territory. *Squid Game* (2021) and *All of Us Are Dead* (2022) established Korean horror and dystopian drama as globally competitive genres. By 2023, Korean content accounted for one of the largest shares of non-English content viewed on Netflix globally.

## Significance

### Soft Power and Cultural Diplomacy

K-dramas constitute a significant pillar of South Korea's soft power, operating alongside K-pop and Korean cinema to project Korean culture globally. The South Korean government's **Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA)** and the broader **Korean Wave (Hallyu)** policy framework have actively supported drama exports through subsidies, international promotion, and co-production treaties. The Korea-China FTA negotiations of 2014–2015 explicitly included cultural content (including drama broadcasting rights) as a trade issue—a sign of how economically significant Korean drama had become. When China informally restricted Korean cultural content imports in 2016–2017 in retaliation for South Korea's acceptance of the THAAD missile defense system, K-drama distributors estimated losses of several hundred million dollars in Chinese market revenues.

### Consumer Culture and Tourism

K-drama's influence extends deep into consumer behavior internationally. Filming locations become tourist destinations: the Jeju Island locations of *My Love from the Star*, the Nami Island location featured in *Winter Sonata*, and the Busan locations of *Reply 1997* and other dramas attract hundreds of thousands of visitors annually. Korean cosmetics (K-beauty), fashion, and food products are frequently embedded in drama product placement and generate consumer demand in markets ranging from Japan and Vietnam to Brazil and Nigeria. A 2020 Korea Foundation survey estimated that K-drama viewers were 2–3 times more likely than non-viewers to have purchased Korean food products.

### Genre and Narrative Innovation

K-dramas have developed distinctive narrative conventions that have influenced television globally. The "will-they-won't-they" romance arc stretched across 16 episodes, the emotionally heightened melodrama that cycles through betrayal and reconciliation, the integration of family conflict with romantic plots, and the intricate backstory reveals of the final episodes have all become recognized international genre conventions. Korean thriller and mystery dramas (*Signal*, *Tunnel*, *Voice*, *Stranger*) and legal/medical procedurals have developed audiences that overlap little with romance drama audiences, demonstrating the genre breadth of the industry. Platform competition has encouraged more experimental content—*My Mister*'s quiet emotional realism, *Hellbound*'s supernatural allegory, *D.P.*'s institutional critique—that might not have found broadcast slots a decade earlier.

### Labor Conditions and Industry Reform

The K-drama industry's live-shoot production model has attracted increasing scrutiny for labor exploitation. In 2016, the death of a drama production crew member after a traffic accident during an exhausting 24-hour shoot prompted industry-wide discussion. Industry labor groups have documented that crew members in below-the-line positions (lighting, camera operation, props) routinely work 18–20 hour days on compressed schedules. The Korea Producers' Association and related bodies have periodically negotiated improvements in minimum rest periods and overtime pay, but enforcement remains inconsistent, especially in the competitive mid-tier production sector. Netflix and other international platforms, whose contracts include standard provisions modeled partly on international norms, have driven incremental improvement among their direct production partners.
