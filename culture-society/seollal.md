# Seollal — Korea's Lunar New Year

**Category**: Culture & Society
**Key figures**: N/A (national folk observance)

## Overview

Seollal (설날), Korea's Lunar New Year, is the most significant traditional holiday in South Korea and one of the most widely observed in the Korean cultural sphere. Celebrated on the first day of the lunar calendar — a date that falls between January 21 and February 20 in the Gregorian calendar — Seollal marks the beginning of a new year according to the lunisolar system shared across East Asia. It is an official three-day public holiday in South Korea, though actual celebration periods commonly extend to five or more days as families travel and gather. The holiday anchors itself in the Confucian ethics of filial piety (*hyo*, 효), ancestor veneration, and multi-generational family unity that have structured Korean social life for centuries. Compare with [Chuseok](chuseok.md), Korea's autumn harvest festival, which shares Seollal's emphasis on family reunion and ancestral rites but is observed in the eighth lunar month.

## Historical Origins

Lunar New Year observances in Korea are attested from the Three Kingdoms period (57 BCE – 668 CE), when court records describe royal ceremonies marking the new lunar year. The [Goryeo dynasty](../history/goryeo-period.md) (918–1392) counted Seollal among its principal seasonal festivals, incorporating both Buddhist ritual elements and the ancestor veneration practices that would persist through the [Joseon dynasty](../history/joseon-dynasty.md). Under Joseon, Seollal became more explicitly Confucian in character, with the court performing elaborate rituals at royal ancestral shrines (Jongmyo) on the first day of the lunar year.

Japanese colonial authorities (1910–1945) attempted to suppress Seollal observance in favor of Japanese New Year (January 1), declaring solar January 1 the official new year and discouraging lunar-calendar celebrations as part of broader cultural assimilation policies. Despite this, Seollal observance persisted informally in households throughout the colonial period. After independence (1945), Seollal initially faced competition from the officially promoted solar new year; for several decades, South Korea recognized both solar and lunar New Year celebrations. Seollal was formally reinstated as an official national holiday with its current three-day structure in 1989, reflecting renewed emphasis on traditional Korean cultural identity following democratization.

## The Charye Ancestral Rite

The ceremonial heart of Seollal is *charye* (차례), the morning ancestral rite performed on the first day of the lunar new year. Distinguished from the more elaborate *jesa* memorial rites performed on death anniversaries, the Seollal charye is a collective thanksgiving ceremony directed to all recent ancestors (typically four generations back on the paternal line). Families arrange a table (jesang) with specific foods in prescribed positions: rice and soup at the front, fish and meat dishes in the center, vegetables and side dishes around them, and fruit at the rear. A full set of *sijeongban* ancestral offerings includes tteokguk (rice cake soup), various *jeon* (pan-fried dishes), steamed dishes, kimchi, and seasonal fruit.

The ceremony follows a formal sequence: incense is lit; the assembled family performs deep bows to the ancestors (traditionally two full bows, or *jaebae*); food and wine are offered; a period of silence allows the ancestors to partake; and a final bow closes the rite and sees the spirits off. The entire rite, in its traditional form, requires hours of preparation by family members — predominantly women — and approximately 30 minutes of ceremonial performance.

## Foods of Seollal

### Tteokguk (Rice Cake Soup)

The definitive food of Seollal is *tteokguk* (떡국), a soup made with sliced oval rice cakes (tteok) simmered in a clear beef or pheasant broth, garnished with egg strips, seaweed, and sometimes dumplings. The tradition of eating tteokguk on New Year's Day is tied to the belief that consuming it adds one year to one's age and bestows good fortune. The rice cakes are cut into oval coin-shapes symbolizing wealth and prosperity. Children in particular are teased that they will not grow a year older without eating their tteokguk. Regional variations include tteok-mandu-guk (tteokguk with dumplings, common in Seoul and the northern regions) and coastal versions enriched with oysters or anchovy broth.

### Jeon (Pan-Fried Dishes)

*Jeon* (전) — a broad category of pan-fried foods coated in egg batter — represent one of the most labor-intensive elements of Seollal preparation. Common varieties include *jijim-gae* (green onion pancake), *hwajeon* (flower rice cake), and individual protein-based jeon made with fish, tofu, beef, or kimchi. The sound of jeon sizzling in oil is so associated with Seollal preparation that it appears in cultural references as an auditory trigger for holiday memory.

### Hangwa and Confections

Traditional Korean confections (*hangwa*, 한과) — including *yakgwa* (honey-soaked wheat cookies), *yugwa* (puffed rice confections), and *dasik* (pressed tea cookies) — are prepared in elaborate quantities for ancestral offerings and gift-giving. The production of hangwa for Seollal represents a significant domestic industry, with demand peaking sharply in the weeks before the holiday.

### Gift Foods

Seollal gift-giving has generated a substantial food gift market. Traditional gifts include sets of dried goods (mushrooms, seaweed, dried fish), premium fruit boxes (pears, apples), and packaged traditional foods. Modern Seollal gift culture extends to gourmet food sets, premium hanwoo (Korean beef) packages, health supplements, and luxury goods, with department stores and online retailers reporting major sales spikes in the two weeks before the holiday.

## Rituals and Customs

### Sebae (New Year's Bow)

*Sebae* (세배) is the formal deep bow performed by younger family members toward elders on the morning of Seollal, following the charye ceremony. The bow is performed by kneeling, placing hands on the floor, and lowering the head — the most formal gesture of respect in Korean etiquette. In return for sebae, elders bestow *deokdam* (blessings or good wishes) and *sebaetdon* (세뱃돈), small monetary gifts in white envelopes. The amounts are scaled to the recipient's age and the giver's financial position; for young children, amounts traditionally ranged from the equivalent of a few dollars, though contemporary practice often involves much larger sums. Adults perform sebae to grandparents, parents, aunts and uncles, and older relatives; the ceremony reinforces generational hierarchy in experiential, embodied form.

### Hanbok

Seollal is one of the two primary occasions (alongside Chuseok) on which *hanbok* (한복), traditional Korean dress, is widely worn. Children typically wear brightly colored hanbok in greens, reds, and blues; adults may wear more subdued versions in natural dyes. The wearing of hanbok for multi-generational family photographs has remained consistent across generations despite overall declining use of traditional dress in daily life. Contemporary interest in modernized hanbok has created a fashion subculture that peaks around Seollal and Chuseok.

### Games and Activities

Traditional Seollal games include *yutnori* (윷놀이), a board game using four wooden sticks as dice, played in teams and popular for its combination of chance and strategy; *neolttwigi* (널뛰기), a seesaw game played by women and girls; *jegichagi* (제기차기), a shuttlecock-kicking game; *kite-flying* (연날리기); and *ddakji-chigi* (딱지치기), a paper-folding and flipping game. These games, once embedded in broader community celebrations at the village level, now primarily occur within nuclear family contexts or are practiced at cultural events organized by local governments and heritage organizations.

### Ancestral Grave Visits

Many families visit the graves of deceased relatives during Seollal, bringing food offerings, performing bows, and tidying grave sites. This practice — *seongmyo* (성묘) — mirrors the practice at Chuseok and reflects the dual function of Korean ancestral rites: honoring the dead and reinforcing the living family's sense of continuity.

## The Great Migration (Minsok Daijungi)

Seollal precipitates what South Koreans call the *minsok daijungi* (민속 대이동) — the Great Folk Migration — as tens of millions of people travel from major cities to ancestral hometowns. In recent years, South Korea's population of approximately 52 million sees 30–40 million individual journeys over the holiday period (as measured by transportation statistics). Highways become severely congested; a drive that might normally take 2–3 hours can extend to 8–10 hours. KTX high-speed rail tickets, sold two to four weeks in advance, sell out within minutes of release. The directional asymmetry of the migration — outward from Seoul, Busan, and major cities toward smaller towns and rural areas — temporarily reverses the usual urbanization gradient, flooding rural regions with returnees. See also: [Seoul](../geography/seoul.md), [Busan](../geography/busan.md).

## Gender, Labor, and Contemporary Critique

Seollal's domestic labor burden has historically fallen heavily on women, particularly daughters-in-law (며느리, *myeoneuri*), who are expected to carry out hours of cooking, cleaning, and ceremonial preparation while male family members conduct the ancestral rites and socialize. This gendered labor division has been the subject of sustained feminist critique in South Korea since at least the 1980s and has become increasingly visible in public discourse. Surveys consistently show that married women report higher stress levels around Seollal than their husbands or single family members. Contemporary negotiations include splitting preparation labor more equitably, purchasing pre-made foods, or simplifying the charye ceremony.

The holiday also generates tension around expectations placed on adult children regarding career success, marriage, and family formation. The semi-annual reunion format (Seollal and Chuseok) has come to be associated with intrusive questioning from older relatives, generating considerable online humor and social commentary among younger generations.

## Seollal in Contemporary Culture

The [Korean drama industry](korean-drama-industry.md) and [Korean cinema](film-cinema-industry.md) feature Seollal as a recurring narrative setting — a time when family tensions surface, class differences become visible, and protagonists face decisive moments. The holiday's mixture of warmth, obligation, and conflict has made it a reliable dramatic backdrop. Seollal also drives one of South Korea's largest film release windows; major productions time releases for the holiday period, competing for families seeking entertainment during multi-day gatherings.

The [K-pop industry](kpop-industry.md) and celebrity culture observe Seollal through performative family visits, public greetings, and special broadcast programming, integrating traditional holiday imagery into contemporary media.

## North Korea and the Korean Diaspora

Seollal is observed in North Korea as "Minsok Myeongjeol" (민속명절, Folk Holiday), a designation assigned in 1989 when Kim Il-sung formally restored Lunar New Year as an official state holiday after decades of suppression as a "feudal custom." North Korean observation is less elaborate than South Korean practice and subject to state direction, but family-centered food traditions and ancestral rites persist.

Among the [Korean diaspora](../geography/korean-diaspora-overseas-communities.md) worldwide — communities in the United States, China, Japan, Australia, and elsewhere — Seollal is observed with varying degrees of formality. Korean-American communities hold public Seollal festivals in cities with large Korean populations; Korean-Chinese communities (Joseonjok) observe it in alignment with the Chinese Spring Festival, which shares the same lunar date.
