# Banking and Finance Sector

**Category**: Economy  
**Key figures**: Park Chung-hee, Kim Woo-choong, Cho Soon, Lee Hyun-jae, Lee Kun-hee  
**Related sectors**: [Chaebol](../economy/chaebol.md), [Semiconductors](../economy/semiconductors.md), [Technology Sector](../economy/technology-sector.md)

## Summary

South Korea's banking and financial system is the product of deliberate state engineering in the 1960s, traumatic crisis in 1997, and ongoing adaptation to globalization and digital disruption. From its founding under [Park Chung-hee's](../people/park-chung-hee.md) developmental state to its contemporary configuration as a hybrid of state-influenced large banks and a surging fintech ecosystem, Korean finance reflects and amplifies the country's broader economic trajectory.

The sector's architecture was originally designed not to optimize capital allocation through market mechanisms but to concentrate resources behind the state's industrial strategy: heavy chemicals, shipbuilding, electronics, and eventually semiconductors. This directed credit system produced the Han River Miracle but created systemic fragility that the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis catastrophically exposed. Post-1997 restructuring partially liberalized the sector but preserved significant state influence, producing a system neither fully market-driven nor purely developmental. Today South Korea's financial sector manages assets equivalent to approximately 300% of GDP and operates alongside a booming fintech and digital payment industry that has made Korea one of the world's most cashless economies.

## Origins: The Developmental State and Directed Credit (1961–1980)

When [Park Chung-hee](../people/park-chung-hee.md) seized power in the military coup of May 1961, the South Korean financial system consisted of weak commercial banks, a primitive capital market, and pervasive informal money markets (curb markets, saechaesijang) that provided much of the credit actually reaching small businesses. Park's government moved decisively to bring the banks under state control: in 1961, the government nationalized the commercial banks (Bank of Seoul, Hanil Bank, Cho Hung Bank, Commercial Bank of Korea, and others), installing government-approved directors and subordinating lending decisions to industrial policy priorities.

The institutional architecture of developmental finance comprised several layers:

### Policy Banks
- **Korea Development Bank (KDB)**: Founded in 1954, KDB became the primary vehicle for long-term industrial credit, funded through government bonds and foreign borrowing. It channeled capital into heavy industries (steel, chemicals, shipbuilding) at below-market interest rates, absorbing the commercial risk that private banks could not.
- **Export-Import Bank of Korea (KEXIM)**: Established in 1976, KEXIM provided trade finance and export credit guarantees, enabling Korean firms to compete for international contracts by offering financing to foreign buyers of Korean goods.
- **Industrial Bank of Korea (IBK)**: Focused on lending to small and medium enterprises (SMEs), though in practice the large [chaebol](../economy/chaebol.md) absorbed the majority of credit.
- **Korea Housing Bank**: Specialized in mortgage finance for the rapidly urbanizing population; merged into Kookmin Bank in 2001.

### The Chaebol–Bank Nexus
The commercial banks under state control were effectively required to lend to chaebol that the government had designated as strategic. Interest rates were set below market clearing levels, which rationed credit through government discretion rather than price; access to this cheap credit was a major source of competitive advantage for the chosen chaebols. The debt-to-equity ratios of major chaebols regularly exceeded 300% and sometimes reached 500–600% in capital-intensive sectors, levels that would have been commercially unsustainable without the implicit government guarantee that the banks were lending on.

This created what economists later called the "too big to fail" problem at the heart of Korean capitalism: chaebols borrowed heavily, expanded aggressively, and were confident that the state would not allow major ones to fail—because their failure would bring down the banks and with them the entire credit system. The government periodically rescued failing firms and renegotiated debt terms rather than allowing market-driven restructuring.

### Curb Market and Financial Repression
Despite state control of formal banking, informal credit markets (curb markets) remained large throughout the 1960s–1980s, providing higher-interest lending to small businesses and households excluded from the formal banking system. The August 3, 1972 Presidential Emergency Decree froze all curb market debt, forcing a restructuring that converted informal loans to formal low-interest ones—a massive transfer from creditors to debtors that relieved chaebol financial pressure but destabilized informal lenders.

## The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis: Anatomy of a Collapse

The structural vulnerabilities built into Korea's developmental financial system collided catastrophically with changes in the global capital environment in 1997. The crisis unfolded in multiple stages:

### Background Fragilities (1993–1997)
The Kim Young-sam government (1993–1998) undertook partial financial liberalization: interest rates were partially deregulated, and restrictions on foreign borrowing by Korean financial institutions were relaxed. This enabled Korean banks and chaebols to borrow aggressively in international short-term capital markets, typically at lower rates than domestic borrowing. By 1997, Korean financial institutions had accumulated approximately **$120 billion in short-term foreign debt** with maturities under one year, against foreign exchange reserves of approximately $30 billion. The mismatch—short-term foreign liabilities, long-term domestic assets—created acute rollover risk.

### Trigger and Cascade (1997)
Following Thailand's baht devaluation in July 1997, international investors grew nervous about Asian emerging market exposure and began withdrawing short-term loans. In Korea, the crisis combined:

- **Chaebol failures**: Hanbo Steel (January 1997), Sammi Steel (March 1997), Jinro Group (April 1997), Kia Motors (July 1997), and several other major chaebols filed for bankruptcy or receivership during 1997, creating non-performing loans that destabilized the banks that had lent to them.
- **Currency collapse**: The won (KRW) fell from approximately 900 KRW/USD in early 1997 to nearly **1,964 KRW/USD** by December 1997—a depreciation of over 50%. Dollar-denominated debts became doubled in won terms overnight.
- **Liquidity crisis**: International banks, led by American institutions, refused to roll over short-term Korean debt in November 1997, triggering a liquidity emergency.
- **IMF intervention**: The Korean government formally requested IMF assistance on November 21, 1997. The IMF package totaled **$57 billion**—the largest bailout in IMF history to that date—drawn from the IMF, World Bank, Asian Development Bank, and bilateral creditors including the United States, Japan, Germany, France, UK, Australia, and Canada.

### IMF Conditionality and Social Cost
The IMF's conditions for the bailout required rapid macroeconomic adjustment: interest rate increases (benchmark rates rose to over 30% briefly), fiscal tightening, and structural reforms including financial sector restructuring. The social costs were severe:

- Unemployment rose from approximately 2% to over 7% in 1998; **approximately 1.3 million workers** lost jobs in a single year.
- GDP contracted by **5.5% in 1998**, the first Korean recession since the 1980 oil crisis contraction.
- The crisis disproportionately affected workers in mid-tier firms; chaebol affiliates often restructured by shedding subsidiaries and workers rather than collapsing.

The crisis became a defining national trauma, producing what Korean scholars call the "IMF generation" (IMF sedae)—young workers who entered the labor market in 1997–2001 and experienced permanent scarring in career trajectories, savings behavior, and economic psychology.

## Post-1997 Restructuring: Liberalization and Reconcentration

### Banking Sector Restructuring
The Kim Dae-jung government (1998–2003) directed a comprehensive financial sector restructuring:

- **Bank mergers and closures**: Five commercial banks were closed; others were merged. Hanil Bank and Commercial Bank of Korea merged to form Hanvit Bank (later Woori Bank); Kookmin Bank and H&CB Bank merged; Korea First Bank was sold to Newbridge Capital (a US private equity firm), the first foreign acquisition of a major Korean bank.
- **Bad loan resolution**: The Korea Asset Management Corporation (KAMCO) was empowered to purchase non-performing loans from banks at discounted prices, cleaning bank balance sheets. KAMCO acquired approximately 110 trillion won (about $100 billion) in bad assets between 1997 and 2002.
- **Recapitalization**: The government injected approximately 168 trillion won into the financial system through the Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation (KDIC) and other mechanisms to recapitalize surviving banks.
- **Corporate governance reforms**: Banks were required to implement independent board directors, stricter loan classification standards, and Basel I capital adequacy requirements (8% minimum capital ratio). Opaque cross-shareholding arrangements between banks and chaebol affiliates were restricted.

### The Big Deal and Corporate Restructuring
Simultaneously, the government orchestrated the "Big Deal" (1998–1999), requiring major chaebols to swap or consolidate overlapping business units to eliminate duplication—the memory-chip business was consolidated under Hyundai, which absorbed LG Semicon, and the aerospace firms were merged into a single venture—concentrating industrial structure while nominally rationalizing it. The "workout" program put distressed large firms under creditor-led restructuring rather than bankruptcy, enabling controlled wind-downs. Daewoo Group's collapse in 1999—with debts of over **84 trillion won** (approximately $70 billion)—was the largest corporate failure in Korean history and required a massive banking sector response.

## The 2003 Credit Card Crisis

Post-1997, policymakers sought to stimulate consumption and expand domestic demand. Credit card companies (card subsidiaries of banks plus independent issuers like LG Card and Samsung Card) were encouraged to expand aggressively, with card issuance subject to minimal credit checks. The number of credit cards in circulation rose from approximately 39 million in 1999 to over **100 million** by 2002 in a population of 47 million—more than two cards per adult.

Delinquency rates surged as consumers accumulated debt beyond their capacity. LG Card, the largest independent credit card company, required a government-orchestrated rescue in 2003–2004. Household credit delinquency affected approximately **3.7 million Koreans** (about 8% of the working-age population). The crisis forced regulatory tightening: minimum payment requirements, credit bureaus, and income-based credit limits were strengthened.

This episode reinforced the recurring Korean pattern: liberalization generates excessive risk-taking, crisis produces regulatory response, and the cycle repeats.

## Contemporary Banking Structure

South Korea's contemporary banking sector comprises several layers:

### Large Commercial Banks
Four large private-sector banks (with significant residual government ownership through KDIC and pension funds) dominate:
- **KB Kookmin Bank**: The largest Korean bank by assets; formed from the 2001 merger of Kookmin and Housing Bank. Assets exceed 450 trillion won (approximately $340 billion).
- **Shinhan Bank**: Formed through the merger of Shinhan and Chohung Bank; strong retail and SME franchise.
- **Hana Bank (KEB Hana Bank)**: Created from the merger of Hana Bank and Korea Exchange Bank (previously majority foreign-owned by Lone Star Funds).
- **Woori Bank**: Emerged from the post-crisis mergers; government-owned through KDIC for much of the post-crisis period; majority-privatized in 2017.

### State-owned and Policy Banks
- **Korea Development Bank (KDB)**: Remains a major industrial and corporate lender; periodically involved in rescue lending (including support for Korean shipping companies in 2016–2017).
- **Industrial Bank of Korea (IBK)**: Continues to focus on SME lending; government-owned.
- **Korea Export-Import Bank (KEXIM)**: Provides trade finance and overseas investment support.

### Foreign Banks
Citibank Korea, Standard Chartered Bank Korea, and branches of various international banks operate in Korea, primarily serving corporate and institutional clients. Their market share in retail banking remains small.

### Banking Supervision
The Financial Services Commission (FSC) and Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) regulate the banking sector, implementing Basel III capital requirements since 2013. South Korean banks generally maintained capital ratios well above the 10.5% fully-loaded Basel III minimum in the post-crisis decades.

## Fintech and Digital Banking Revolution

South Korea's combination of high smartphone penetration (approximately 95% in 2020), fast broadband infrastructure, and technologically sophisticated consumers made it fertile ground for fintech innovation.

### Mobile Payments
- **KakaoPay**: Launched in 2015 as part of the Kakao messaging ecosystem (see [Technology Sector](../economy/technology-sector.md)), KakaoPay processed approximately 1.4 billion transactions in its first full operational year and reached 36 million users by 2020.
- **Samsung Pay**: Using Near Field Communication (NFC) and Magnetic Secure Transmission (MST) technology, Samsung Pay allowed payment at virtually any card terminal; it leveraged Samsung's dominant position in the Korean smartphone market.
- **NaverPay**: Integrated into the Naver portal (Korea's dominant search engine), providing e-commerce payment services.

### Internet-Only Banks
Two internet-only banks launched in 2017:
- **KakaoBank**: Founded as a joint venture of Kakao Corp and Korea Investment Holdings; it opened more than a million accounts within days of its July 2017 launch and passed **5 million customers** within its first six months, the fastest new-account growth of any bank in Korean history, before becoming one of Korea's largest banks by customer count. Listed on the Korea Stock Exchange in 2021 with market capitalization exceeding 18 trillion won.
- **K-bank**: An internet-only bank associated with KT Corporation (telecom giant) and several financial partners; smaller than KakaoBank but expanding its crypto-related services through partnership with Upbit, Korea's largest cryptocurrency exchange.

A third internet-only bank, **Toss Bank** (2021), emerged from the fintech startup Viva Republica's Toss personal finance app.

### Cryptocurrency and Digital Assets
South Korea became one of the world's largest cryptocurrency trading markets by volume in 2017–2018. Korean "kimchi premium"—where Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies traded at 10–40% above global prices on Korean exchanges—reflected intense retail demand. The government responded with trading name verification requirements (real-name accounts mandatory from 2018), restrictions on initial coin offerings (ICOs), and periodic warnings. The collapse of Terraform Labs (founded by Korean entrepreneur Do Kwon), whose Luna/UST algorithmic stablecoin collapsed in May 2022 losing approximately $40 billion in market value, triggered renewed Korean regulatory attention and eventual arrest warrants for Do Kwon.

## Household Debt: The Persistent Structural Challenge

South Korea's household debt has been among the highest in the OECD as a percentage of disposable income. By 2022, household debt reached approximately **1,870 trillion won** (approximately $1.5 trillion)—equivalent to roughly 105% of GDP. The drivers include:

- **Housing prices**: Seoul apartment prices roughly doubled between 2017 and 2021 (before a subsequent correction), driving mortgage borrowing. The price of an average Seoul apartment reached on the order of 18 times median annual household income at the 2021 peak.
- **Education spending**: Expenditure on private tutoring (hagwon) constitutes a major household budget item (see [Education and Universities](../culture-society/education-and-universities.md)).
- **Cultural norms**: Korean social norms around homeownership and life-stage spending (weddings, education, housing) create concentrated debt loading at certain life stages.

The Bank of Korea and FSS have periodically implemented Loan-to-Value (LTV) and Debt Service Ratio (DSR) restrictions on mortgage lending to prevent crisis, with mixed success. Household debt management is considered the primary macroprudential risk facing Korean financial stability in the 2020s.
