# Energy and Resources Policy

**Category**: Economy  
**Key figures**: Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy (MOTIE); Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO); Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP); Korea National Oil Corporation (KNOC); Korea Gas Corporation (KOGAS)

## Summary

South Korea's energy and resources strategy reflects the country's resource-poor geography, energy-intensive economy, and geopolitical constraints. The nation imports roughly 93% of its primary energy — coal, crude oil, liquefied natural gas (LNG), and uranium — making supply diversification and strategic stockpiling core national interests. The sector is dominated by state-owned enterprises: KEPCO for electricity generation and distribution, KNOC for oil and gas exploration, and KOGAS for LNG importation. Nuclear power emerged as the cornerstone energy source beginning in the 1970s under Park Chung-hee's developmental state model; as of the mid-2020s, nuclear capacity represents roughly 25–30% of total installed capacity and provides approximately 30–35% of domestic electricity output. Coal supplies around 30–35% of generation, natural gas 25–30%, and renewables (solar, wind, hydro) have grown from under 5% in 2015 to approximately 9–11% by 2024.

## Historical Development

### Foundation Under the Developmental State (1950s–1970s)

South Korea entered the industrial era with virtually no domestic fossil fuel resources. The Korean War (1950–1953) destroyed what little infrastructure existed, and reconstruction depended on U.S. aid-funded coal and oil imports. President Park Chung-hee's Five-Year Plans from 1962 onward prioritized energy-intensive heavy and chemical industries (steel, petrochemicals, shipbuilding) without addressing the underlying import dependence. The 1973 and 1979 oil crises — when global crude prices quadrupled — devastated the Korean economy and forced a strategic reorientation toward nuclear power as the path to energy independence.

The first nuclear reactor, Kori-1 (고리 1호기) near Busan, came online in April 1978 with U.S. Westinghouse technology. Within a decade South Korea had added Kori-2 (1983), Wolsong-1 (a CANDU heavy-water reactor supplied by Canada, 1982), and begun the Yonggwang (later renamed Hanbit) and Ulchin (later renamed Hanul) complexes. The 1979 Strategic Petroleum Reserve program — modeled on the U.S. SPR established after the 1973 crisis — established storage facilities in Ulsan, Yeosu, and Seosan, eventually reaching approximately 146 million barrels of crude-oil reserves by the 2010s (sufficient for roughly 146 days of imports).

### Diversification and the LNG Boom (1980s–2000s)

The 1980s saw major diversification of both supply sources and fuel types. South Korea signed its first long-term LNG supply contract with Indonesia (Arun field) in 1986, inaugurating the Pyeongtaek LNG terminal; contracts with Malaysia's Petronas, Qatar's RasGas, Brunei, and Oman followed. KOGAS, established in 1983, became the world's largest single importer of LNG by the 2000s, receiving approximately 35–40 million tonnes per annum (MTPA). The heavy dependence on Middle Eastern crude oil was partially offset by deals with Central Asian producers (Kazakhstan via pipeline swap) and Australian coal for power generation.

Throughout the 1990s and 2000s, South Korea also pursued upstream resource investment abroad — KNOC took equity stakes in oil and gas fields in Kazakhstan, Vietnam, Canada, and Iraq — as part of an "energy security via ownership" strategy. These investments were partially unwound in the 2010s when global commodity prices fell and the assets underperformed.

### Nuclear Expansion and the Fukushima Inflection (2010s)

By 2010 South Korea operated 20 nuclear reactors at four sites (Kori, Hanbit, Hanul, and Wolsong) with total capacity of approximately 18.7 GW, making it the fifth-largest nuclear power nation by installed capacity. The March 2011 Fukushima Daiichi disaster in Japan triggered widespread anti-nuclear sentiment globally and domestically. A 2012 safety-scandal involving forged certificates for reactor components at Hanbit and Hanul — some 20,000 falsified quality-assurance records — shuttered several reactors for inspection and damaged public confidence in the safety regime.

President Lee Myung-bak's 2008 "Low Carbon, Green Growth" framework nominally embraced renewables but remained nuclear-centric. Under his administration, in December 2009 the Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) consortium won a $20.4 billion contract to build four APR-1400 reactors — a domestically developed third-generation pressurized-water design — at Barakah in the United Arab Emirates, South Korea's first major nuclear export. His successor Park Geun-hye continued the domestic nuclear-expansion program and promoted the APR-1400 abroad. The Barakah plant's Unit 1 achieved commercial operation in April 2021.

## Nuclear Phase-Out and Its Reversal (2017–2025)

President Moon Jae-in (2017–2022) declared a nuclear phase-out policy in June 2017, committing to no new reactor approvals, shutting down the oldest reactors upon license expiry, and reaching zero nuclear by the 2080s through a gradual renewable-led transition. The closure of Wolsong-1 in 2019 — ahead of its licensed end-of-life — became legally and politically contested; a 2020 Board of Audit and Inspection report accused the energy minister of manipulating the closure's economic analysis. Moon's "Renewable Energy 3020" plan aimed for 20% renewable electricity by 2030, leveraging offshore wind (Yellow Sea, South Sea) and utility-scale solar.

President Yoon Suk-yeol (2022–2025) reversed course dramatically after taking office in May 2022. His administration's "Energy Transition Initiative" (June 2022) reversed the phase-out: canceled nuclear closures (Hanul-1 and -2 lifetime extensions approved; Wolsong-1 reactivation debated), fast-tracked SMR (Small Modular Reactor) R&D funding at KHNP and Doosan Enerbility, and approved a new reactor pair (Shin Hanul-3 and -4, each 1,400 MW APR-1400) after a decade-long approvals freeze. The 2024 "K-Energy Mix" plan set targets of 30% nuclear, 21% renewables, and 22% LNG by 2030, explicitly rejecting Moon's phase-out trajectory.

## Fossil Fuels and Import Dependence

South Korea relies on coal-fired power for approximately 30–35% of annual electricity generation, with thermal coal imported predominantly from Australia (~45%), Russia (~15%, sharply reduced post-2022 Ukraine invasion), Indonesia (~15%), and the United States. Major coal power plants include the Dangjin (충남 당진, 6.4 GW), Taean (태안, 6.1 GW), and Boryeong (보령, 3.2 GW) complexes operated by Korea Southern Power and Korea Midland Power. A phase-down of pre-2007 coal plants was scheduled under Moon's policy but partly deferred under Yoon due to grid reliability concerns during peak-demand summers.

Crude oil imports, approximately 1.1–1.3 billion barrels per year, are sourced principally from Saudi Arabia (~30%), Kuwait (~15%), Iraq (~12%), UAE (~10%), and others. The Ulsan complex (operated by SK Energy, S-Oil, and Hyundai Oilbank) is one of Asia's largest refining clusters, with combined throughput of approximately 1.4 million barrels per day. South Korea is a net exporter of refined petroleum products (diesel, naphtha, jet fuel), generating significant foreign-exchange earnings.

LNG imports have grown substantially, reaching approximately 44–48 MTPA in the early 2020s. The 2022 Ukraine war disrupted Russian LNG supply from the Novatek-operated Yamal LNG facility, prompting emergency diversification contracts with U.S. Henry Hub-linked cargoes (Sabine Pass, Corpus Christi LNG) and spot-market purchases from Qatar and Australia. KOGAS operates six LNG receiving terminals with combined storage capacity exceeding 10 million cubic meters.

## Renewable Energy Development

South Korea's renewable energy sector has expanded rapidly since the 2008 Lee Myung-bak Green Growth initiative established the Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS), requiring utilities to source a rising share of electricity from renewables (rising to 25% by 2026 under subsequent amendments). Installed solar PV capacity grew from under 2 GW in 2015 to approximately 23 GW by 2024; offshore wind capacity remains under 2 GW but major projects are planned in the Yellow Sea (the Shinan-gun 8.2 GW offshore wind project, the world's largest planned offshore wind farm at its 2021 announcement) and on Jeju Island.

Key corporate players include Hanwha Solutions (solar modules — one of the world's top five manufacturers by volume), LG Energy Solution and Samsung SDI (EV and grid-storage batteries), and Doosan Enerbility (hydrogen turbines, SMR design). The hydrogen economy has been designated a strategic priority since 2019; the "Hydrogen Economy Roadmap" targets 6.2 million hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles by 2040, 1,200 hydrogen refueling stations, and 15 GW of hydrogen power generation.

Grid integration of intermittent renewables poses significant challenges. The 2022 summer blackout warnings — triggered by simultaneous nuclear maintenance shutdowns, peak air-conditioning demand, and low wind — highlighted the need for utility-scale battery storage. KEPCO's 2024–2028 grid-stability plan budgets approximately 12 trillion won ($9 billion) for Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) at substations and demand-response infrastructure.

## Key State Enterprises

**KEPCO** (Korea Electric Power Corporation, 한국전력공사), listed on the Korea Stock Exchange and NYSE ADR, holds a monopoly on electricity transmission and distribution and is the majority shareholder in six power-generation subsidiaries (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power, Korea South-East Power, Korea Midland Power, Korea Western Power, Korea Southern Power, Korea East-West Power). KEPCO's subsidized residential tariff structure has created recurring deficits — in FY2022 it reported a net loss of approximately 24 trillion won ($18 billion), the largest in its history, driven by soaring global LNG and coal prices that could not be fully passed through to consumers without government approval.

**KHNP** (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power, 한국수력원자력) operates all 26 commercial nuclear reactors as of 2024 (at Hanul, Hanbit, Wolsong, and Kori sites) and manages the domestic uranium fuel-fabrication plant in Daejeon. It spearheads the APR-1400 export program and SMR development (SMART — System-integrated Modular Advanced ReacTor — received standard design approval in 2012).

**KOGAS** (Korea Gas Corporation, 한국가스공사) is the world's second-largest LNG importer. It operates the cross-country natural gas pipeline network and has equity stakes in upstream LNG projects (Oman LNG, Rasgas Train 2 in Qatar, Prelude FLNG in Australia, and the troubled Mozambique LNG project).

## Significance

Energy policy shapes South Korea's geopolitical posture, fiscal sustainability, and climate commitments. The nation is a signatory to the Paris Agreement and has committed to achieving carbon neutrality by 2050; the 2030 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) targets a 40% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions from 2018 levels. Meeting these commitments while maintaining a stable, affordable grid in a resource-poor context requires technological progress in battery storage, grid management, and next-generation nuclear (SMRs — small modular reactors), where South Korean firms including KHNP, Doosan Enerbility, and Samsung C&T are competing globally.

Nuclear power remains politically contested. Anti-nuclear movements gained momentum post-Fukushima; waste disposal remains unresolved (the Gyeongju intermediate-level waste repository opened in 2015, but a permanent high-level waste repository site has not been designated). The post-2022 pivot toward energy independence — domestic LNG reserves expansion, accelerated renewable build, nuclear restart — represents a strategic rebalancing driven by the Ukraine-triggered energy crisis and geopolitical realignment away from Russian hydrocarbons.

The sector also reflects broader economic tradeoffs among industrial consumers (steel, petrochemicals, semiconductors demanding cheap reliable baseload), residential consumers (prioritizing affordability), and environmental advocates (pushing decarbonization). South Korea's export of nuclear and energy technologies — KEPCO/KHNP reactor sales (UAE, potential Poland), Hanwha solar modules, LG Energy Solution EV batteries now penetrating U.S. and European markets — further embeds energy policy within industrial and trade strategy.
