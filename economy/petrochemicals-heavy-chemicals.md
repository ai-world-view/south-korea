# Petrochemicals and Heavy Chemical Industry

South Korea's petrochemical and heavy chemical industry is a cornerstone of the country's export economy and industrial base. Despite being a resource-poor nation dependent on imported crude oil and natural gas, South Korea has built one of the world's largest and most competitive petrochemical sectors, producing plastic resins, synthetic fibers, fertilizers, pharmaceuticals, and specialty chemicals that feed downstream manufacturing industries ranging from textiles to automotive to electronics. The sector generates hundreds of billions of dollars in annual export revenue and employs hundreds of thousands of workers directly and indirectly.

## Origins and Development (1960s–1980s)

South Korea's petrochemical industry emerged as part of the government-led industrialization strategy of the 1960s and 1970s under President Park Chung-hee. Recognizing that upstream chemical production could provide feedstock for downstream manufacturing (textiles, plastics, synthetic fibers) while generating substantial export revenues, the Park government designated petrochemistry as a strategic industry and provided tax incentives, export-credit facilities, and infrastructure support to large conglomerates willing to build production capacity.

The country's first integrated petrochemical base, the **Ulsan Petrochemical Complex** (울산석유화학단지), came on stream in the early 1970s, with its first naphtha cracker—the cornerstone facility that breaks down naphtha into ethylene and other basic petrochemical building blocks—operating from 1972 on the southeastern coast. Ulsan and the adjoining **Onsan** (온산) industrial zone quickly became South Korea's primary petrochemical production region, with multiple cracking units, polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) plants, and downstream facilities operated by producers such as Honam Petrochemical (renamed Lotte Chemical in 2012) and others.

The 1973 and 1979 oil crises posed serious challenges—South Korea, with no domestic oil reserves, faced surging crude costs and periodic supply disruptions. However, the government's focus on importing crude as strategic feedstock and building efficient, large-scale production facilities kept the industry competitive. By the early 1980s, South Korea had become a significant exporter of petrochemicals, with capacity to compete against Middle Eastern producers and U.S. chemical majors.

South Korea's total ethylene nameplate capacity reached approximately **11–12 million tons per annum (tpa)** by the mid-2020s, placing it among the world's top six ethylene-producing nations. Annual petrochemical exports typically range between **$45–60 billion**, depending on commodity prices, with Northeast Asian markets (China, Japan, Southeast Asia) as primary destinations.

## Major Production Centers

### Yeosu (여수) — Yeosu National Industrial Complex

The **Yeosu National Industrial Complex** (여수국가산업단지), established in 1967 on the southern coast of South Jeolla Province, is South Korea's largest single petrochemical concentration by number of facilities. More than 400 companies operate on the 27 km² complex, producing approximately 25 million tons of petrochemical products annually. LG Chem's flagship petrochemical operations are centered here, with ethylene crackers, PVC plants, and ABS polymer lines. GS Caltex's Yeosu refinery—one of South Korea's largest, with a crude-processing capacity on the order of 800,000 barrels per day, part of a national refining base of roughly 3.3 million barrels per day—feeds aromatics directly into chemical production.

### Ulsan and Onsan (울산·온산)

The Ulsan region—including the Ulsan Petrochemical Complex and the adjoining Onsan industrial zone developed in the 1970s—is South Korea's second-largest petrochemical concentration and the most tightly integrated with domestic crude refining. Home to multiple ethylene cracking units, it produces the full spectrum of basic chemicals:

- **Olefins** (에틸렌, 프로필렌) — ethylene and propylene, the fundamental building blocks
- **Aromatics** (방향족 화합물) — benzene, toluene, xylene (BTX), used in plastics and fibers
- **Polyolefins** (폴리올레핀) — polyethylene and polypropylene resins, the world's most-produced plastics
- **Synthetic fibers** — polyester and nylon production for textiles

SK Geo Centric (formerly SK Innovation's chemical division, formally separated in 2021 to sharpen its business focus) and S-Oil (a Saudi Aramco affiliate) operate major facilities at Onsan. The complex is integrated with refining capacity, allowing cracking of crude oil directly and maximizing margins. S-Oil's Shaheen ("Falcon") project, announced in 2022 with a projected investment of roughly ₩9.25 trillion (about $6.9 billion at 2022 exchange rates), will add a steam cracker and derivative units at the Ulsan site, representing the largest single petrochemical investment in South Korea's history.

### Daesan (대산) — West Coast

**Daesan** (대산), located on the western coast near the Yellow Sea in South Chungcheong Province, hosts a major concentration of ethylene cracking, polyolefin production, and specialty chemicals plants. Key operators include Lotte Chemical, Hanwha Solutions, and Hyundai Chemical. The Daesan complex is notable for its methyl methacrylate (MMA), butanediol, and naphtha cracking capacity, and its proximity to Chinese export markets through Yellow Sea shipping lanes provides a logistical advantage.

### The Capital-Region Downstream Cluster

The Seoul capital region is not a site of large naphtha crackers—those are concentrated at Yeosu, Ulsan, and Daesan—but it hosts extensive downstream and specialty operations. In **Gyeonggi Province**, industrial complexes such as Banwol and Sihwa around **Ansan** (안산) and Siheung house thousands of small and mid-sized firms making plastics, additives, compounded resins, coatings, and intermediate chemicals for the region's manufacturing base. Nearby **Incheon**—a separate metropolitan city adjoining Gyeonggi—hosts the SK Incheon Petrochem refinery and aromatics complex, which processes crude into fuels and chemical feedstocks serving the greater Seoul metropolitan area.

## Products and Markets

### Basic Chemicals and Plastics

South Korea is a leading global producer of polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP), the two largest-volume plastics in the world. These materials are used in packaging, films, containers, pipes, automotive parts, and countless consumer and industrial products. South Korean resins are exported worldwide, and their quality and price competitiveness make them preferred inputs for downstream plastic converters.

### Synthetic Fibers

Polyester and nylon fibers produced in South Korea supply the global textile and apparel industry. While much of the downstream textile weaving and garment manufacturing has relocated to lower-cost regions, South Korea retains high-value synthetic fiber production, particularly specialty grades for technical textiles (automotive, industrial, medical, etc.).

### Specialty Chemicals

Fine chemicals, pharmaceutical intermediates, agricultural chemicals, and performance additives represent higher-margin product categories. South Korean chemical companies have expanded into specialties and have developed proprietary production processes and formulations for semiconductor manufacturing chemicals, coating materials, adhesives, and other specialized products.

### Petrochemical-Based Pharmaceuticals

South Korea's pharmaceutical industry, while distinct from bulk chemicals, relies on petrochemical feedstocks and manufacturing techniques. Large pharmaceutical producers like LG Chem Pharma Division and others use organic chemistry derived from petrochemical building blocks to produce active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and finished drugs.

## Market Position and Global Competition

South Korea is typically ranked among the world's top 5–7 petrochemical producers by volume and export value. Major competitors include the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iran), the United States (Gulf Coast complexes), Europe, China, and India. South Korea's competitive advantages include:

- **Integrated facilities** — co-location of refining and cracking, maximizing efficiency
- **Scale economies** — large, modern plants with lower per-unit production costs
- **Technology** — proprietary catalysts, process innovations, and quality control
- **Workforce** — skilled engineers and operators with deep industry expertise
- **Logistics** — proximity to Asian markets and efficient port infrastructure

Disadvantages include lack of domestic oil reserves (requiring imports at global prices) and exposure to crude-price volatility. South Korean producers hedge this through backward integration into oil refining and through long-term crude-supply contracts with Middle Eastern and Russian suppliers.

## Integration with Downstream Industries

Petrochemical production feeds critical downstream industries:

- **Textiles** — synthetic fibers become fabric and apparel
- **Automotive** — plastics and coatings for vehicles; synthetic rubber for tires
- **Electronics** — specialty resins and adhesives for semiconductor packaging and displays
- **Construction** — PVC pipes, insulation, coatings
- **Packaging** — films, containers, bags
- **Consumer goods** — appliances, furniture, toys

The chaebol structure (see Chaebol article) means that many South Korean conglomerates have integrated petrochemical, textile, automotive, and electronics divisions, allowing vertical integration and cross-selling of materials and end products. SK Group and Samsung, for instance, operate within ecosystems spanning from oil refining and basic chemicals through specialty materials to final-consumer products.

## Environmental and Health Considerations

The concentration of petrochemical production in coastal industrial zones has generated long-standing environmental and public-health concerns:

- **Air quality** — volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions, hydrogen sulfide, and other odorous or toxic gases from cracking and synthesis units affect air quality in surrounding areas. Major pollution incidents have occurred (e.g., chemical leaks affecting coastal communities).
- **Water pollution** — industrial wastewater discharge and cooling-water usage impact coastal waters and fishing communities. The Yellow Sea and East Sea have experienced eutrophication and ecosystem stress partly attributable to industrial discharge.
- **Soil contamination** — legacy pollution from older facilities and spills has left some industrial sites contaminated.
- **Climate impact** — petrochemical production is energy-intensive and carbon-intensive. Steam crackers and furnaces burn natural gas or fuel oil, contributing significantly to South Korea's industrial CO₂ emissions. Environmental regulations and carbon-neutrality commitments (2050 net-zero target) are prompting investment in cleaner processes and renewable energy.

Industrial complexes have increasingly implemented environmental monitoring, emission controls, and remediation programs. In recent years, regulations have tightened, and community opposition to new chemical plants or expansion projects has grown, particularly in areas with high population density and visibility (e.g., near Incheon and Songdo smart city).

## Workforce and Labor

The petrochemical industry employs hundreds of thousands directly (plant operators, engineers, technicians, logistics) and indirectly (downstream manufacturing, distribution). The work is typically high-wage but also hazardous, requiring strict safety protocols. Trade unions have a significant presence in petrochemical plants, and labor disputes over wages, working conditions, and job security periodically make headlines. The industry's capital intensity means that automation and workforce-reduction pressures have increased over time, even as production has grown.

## Strategic and Geopolitical Significance

The petrochemical industry is deemed strategically critical to South Korea's economy and has received long-term government support, particularly during downturns. The sector's dependence on imported crude oil makes it sensitive to global energy prices and geopolitical disruptions in the Middle East and Russia. The 1973 and 1979 oil crises prompted the government to diversify crude suppliers and build strategic petroleum reserves. More recently, U.S. sanctions on Iran and energy-supply security concerns related to Russia have influenced procurement strategies and diversification efforts.

## Recent Trends and Challenges

### Market Downturn and Margin Compression (2022–2025)

The early 2020s saw some of the worst profitability conditions in decades for South Korean petrochemical producers. A combination of factors compressed margins sharply:

- **Chinese overcapacity**: China built massive new ethylene and polyolefin capacity in the 2020s, adding tens of millions of tons to global supply and underpricing South Korean exports in traditionally captive Asian markets.
- **Demand slowdown**: Post-pandemic destocking and consumer spending shifts weakened demand for packaging resins and synthetic fibers.
- **Energy cost spikes**: Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine caused natural gas prices to surge, raising production costs for South Korean steam crackers.
- **Competition from recycled and bio-based plastics**: Regulatory pressure and brand-owner commitments to circular economy standards introduced structural demand headwinds.

LG Chem, Lotte Chemical, and Hanwha Solutions all reported sharp earnings declines in 2022–2024. Several producers announced capacity rationalizations, including the shutdown of older, less-efficient cracking units, and strategic reviews of their bulk-chemical businesses.

### Specialty and Value-Added Shift

South Korean producers have responded to margin compression by accelerating the transition into specialty chemicals, performance materials, and advanced polymers. LG Chem's Advanced Materials business (including battery materials and specialty resins for electric vehicles) and SK Geo Centric's urban oil refinery project (converting mixed plastic waste into fuel and chemicals) exemplify this shift. Investment in research and development for high-performance polymers, engineering plastics, biodegradable materials (e.g., polylactic acid, polyhydroxyalkanoates), and green chemistry is increasing, though commercialization at scale remains challenging.

### Sustainability and Circular Economy

South Korea's 2050 Carbon Neutrality Act (2021) and the country's commitment to achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 impose significant pressure on the carbon-intensive petrochemical sector. Industry responses include:

- **Chemical recycling**: pyrolysis of mixed plastics back to naphtha or fuel oil, which is fed back into crackers; LG Chem and SK Geo Centric have announced large-scale plants.
- **Bio-naphtha cracking**: using bio-derived feedstocks instead of fossil naphtha in existing cracking units on a pilot basis.
- **Carbon capture**: feasibility studies for CO₂ capture at large crackers in Yeosu and Ulsan.
- **Renewable energy procurement**: signing power purchase agreements (PPAs) for solar and wind electricity to reduce Scope 2 emissions at chemical plants.

### S-Oil Shaheen Project

The **S-Oil Shaheen Project** is the most concrete large-scale expansion in the sector: a ₩9.25 trillion ($6.9 billion at 2022 exchange rates) investment at the Onsan complex near Ulsan announced in 2022, partially financed by parent Saudi Aramco. The project involves construction of a steam cracker (1.4 million tpa ethylene equivalent) plus a full derivative complex including PE, PP, and MEG plants, using Saudi crude as feedstock in an integrated crude-to-chemicals (CTC) configuration. Ground-breaking occurred in 2023, with planned completion around 2026–2027.

### Regional and Trade Policy

Trade tensions, particularly U.S.–China tariffs and supply-chain localization trends, have affected export markets and raw-material sourcing. South Korea's free-trade agreements (FTAs) with major partners—including the Korea-U.S. FTA (KORUS, in force since 2012), the Korea-EU FTA (in force since 2011), and RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, in force since 2022)—provide preferential market access and bolster export competitiveness against rivals without equivalent treaty access.
