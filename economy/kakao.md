# Kakao

Kakao (*카카오*) is South Korea's second-largest internet company (after Naver) and a dominant player in the country's digital ecosystem. Founded in 2010, Kakao built its empire initially on the instant-messaging platform **KakaoTalk**, which became the de facto standard for Korean mobile communication, capturing over 90% of smartphone messaging traffic. From this communication foundation, Kakao expanded into payment services (KakaoPay), fintech (KakaoBank), gaming, music streaming, ride-hailing (Kakao Map/Kakao Taxi), e-commerce, webtoons, and entertainment—creating a sprawling platform conglomerate comparable to China's WeChat in scope and centrality to daily Korean life. Kakao represents the archetypal mobile-first internet company and has served as a model for Korean startup scaling and ecosystem expansion.

## Founding and KakaoTalk Breakthrough

Kakao was founded in 2010 by **Kim Beom-su** (김범수, b. 1966), a serial entrepreneur who previously co-founded **Hangame** (한게임), a major Korean online-gaming portal that later merged with NHN (the predecessor of Naver). Kim and his team recognized the emerging opportunity in smartphone-based instant messaging at a moment when Korea's mobile internet penetration was rising sharply and feature-phone SMS services faced disruption. The company launched **KakaoTalk** (카카오톡) on **18 March 2010**, a free, data-based messaging app leveraging Korea's rapidly expanding 3G and later 4G/LTE infrastructure (nationwide LTE deployed 2011–2012).

In 2014, Kakao merged with **Daum Communications** (다음커뮤니케이션즈), then Korea's second-largest web portal after Naver, creating **Daum Kakao Corporation** (later renamed Kakao Corp in 2015). This merger gave Kakao access to Daum's established web search, email, news aggregation, and mapping services—assets that Kakao quickly rebranded and integrated into its ecosystem.

KakaoTalk's explosive growth was unprecedented in Korean internet history. By July 2011—just fifteen months after launch—the app surpassed 10 million registered users. Within two years of launch, it became the dominant messaging platform, capturing approximately 60% of Korean smartphone users; by 2013, penetration exceeded 80%, and by the mid-2010s it held over 90% of Korean messaging traffic. The app's appeal stemmed from its free structure (using data rather than carrier SMS), intuitive interface, rich emoji/sticker support, and integration of group chat functionality. Ubiquity created a network effect: once most Koreans adopted KakaoTalk, business, government, education, and social life increasingly channeled through it. By the late 2010s, major government announcements and corporate communications were disseminated via KakaoTalk channels, underscoring its centrality to Korean information infrastructure.

## Platform Expansion and Ecosystem Building

From its commanding position in messaging, Kakao pursued aggressive diversification:

**KakaoPay (2014)**: Recognizing the potential of mobile payments, Kakao launched KakaoPay, initially enabling peer-to-peer fund transfers and merchant payments. KakaoPay rapidly became Korea's leading mobile payment platform, facilitating transactions through restaurants, retailers, public transportation, and online merchants. By the early 2020s, KakaoPay was processing tens of millions of transactions daily and held a leading share of Korea's mobile-payment market. The integration of KakaoPay directly into KakaoTalk's interface (accessed via simple menu commands) made payments frictionless and drove user adoption.

**KakaoBank (2017)**: Kakao obtained an internet-banking license and launched KakaoBank, positioning it as a mobile-first, branchless bank offering simpler deposit, lending, and savings products than traditional banks. KakaoBank targeted millennials and younger Koreans uninterested in legacy banking institutions. Its low fees, easy account opening (requiring only smartphone and ID verification), and integration with KakaoPay created a compelling proposition. KakaoBank's IPO on 6 August 2021 set a record as the largest Korean stock market debut of the year, briefly trading at a market capitalization exceeding 33 trillion won (~US$28 billion)—a valuation that surpassed long-established commercial banks such as KB Kookmin and Shinhan.

**Kakao Games and Entertainment**: Kakao acquired and launched mobile-game subsidiaries, becoming a significant player in Korea's gaming market. Games distributed through KakaoGames leveraged the KakaoTalk user base for viral growth and in-game social features. The company also expanded into webtoons (serialized web comics), acquiring platforms and investing in original content that could be adapted into K-dramas and films.

**Kakao Map and Transportation**: Kakao Maps (지도, launched as Daum Maps and rebranded) became Korea's dominant navigation and mapping platform, competing with Naver Map. Kakao Taxi (택시호출), integrated into KakaoMap, offers ride-hailing and dispatch, competing with traditional taxi companies and newer ride-sharing services. Kakao Mobility became a significant player in Korea's on-demand transportation market.

**Kakao Style and E-Commerce**: The company invested in fashion and lifestyle e-commerce, purchasing stakes in platforms offering designer goods and streetwear. Kakao Shop and related ventures attempted to capture e-commerce traffic through the Kakao ecosystem.

**Music and Content**: Kakao acquired music streaming assets, competing with Spotify and Melon in Korea's music market. The company also expanded entertainment partnerships, producing and distributing content across its platforms.

## The Naver–Kakao Duopoly

By the 2010s, South Korea's internet and digital-services market became effectively a two-company duopoly: **Naver** (포털, dominant in web search, knowledge services, and webtoons) and **Kakao** (dominant in mobile messaging, payments, and platform services). The two companies control the majority of Korean internet traffic, digital advertising spend, and digital commerce. This concentration has led to regulatory scrutiny:

- **Antitrust investigations**: Korea's Fair Trade Commission (공정거래위원회) has launched multiple investigations into both Naver and Kakao for anti-competitive practices, including self-preferencing (prioritizing their own services in app stores and search results) and exclusive-dealing contracts with merchants and content creators.
- **App store control**: Both Naver and Kakao's app stores and platforms face accusations of unfair terms with smaller competitors and developers.
- **Market dominance concerns**: Regulatory authorities have worried that the duopoly restricts innovation and choice for Korean consumers and creates barriers for new entrants.

Despite regulatory pressure, the duopoly remains intact, with both companies' market valuations among Korea's highest and their services integrated into nearly every aspect of Korean digital life.

## Organizational Structure and Subsidiaries

Kakao operates through a complex corporate structure with multiple major subsidiaries and acquired properties:

- **Kakao Corp** (the holding company)
- **Kakao Pay** (payments and fintech)
- **KakaoBank** (internet banking)
- **Kakao Games** (mobile gaming)
- **Kakao Webtoon** (webtoons and digital content)
- **Kakao Mobility** (mapping and transportation)
- **Kakao Entertainment** (entertainment, music, and drama production)
- **Kakao Ventures** (venture-capital and investment arm)

The company is majority-owned by founder Kim Beom-su and has attracted significant institutional investment from Korean pension funds and international investors.

## Challenges and Controversies

**2022 Datacenter Fire and National Outage**: On **15 October 2022**, a fire broke out at SK C&C's datacenter in Pangyo, Gyeonggi Province, destroying servers that hosted the majority of Kakao's cloud infrastructure. KakaoTalk, KakaoPay, Kakao Map, and most Kakao services went offline for hours; full restoration of all services took several days. The incident—which disrupted communications, payments, and transportation coordination for tens of millions of Koreans—exposed the fragility of the country's digital infrastructure and the systemic risk of concentrating critical services with a single company. A parliamentary inquiry followed, and the Korean government introduced legislation requiring large platform companies to maintain redundant infrastructure and disaster-recovery systems.

**Data Privacy and Monopoly Power**: Kakao has faced criticism for data-collection practices (storing vast amounts of user communication and transaction data) and for leveraging its monopoly position to compel user adoption of linked services. These concerns were dramatically amplified by the 2022 datacenter fire, which highlighted the risks of concentrating personal data with a single corporate entity.

**Regulatory Scrutiny and Founder Prosecution**: South Korean regulators have fined Kakao for anti-competitive practices. In 2020, the Fair Trade Commission fined the company approximately 16 billion won (~US$13 million) for abusing its dominant market position. In 2022, accusations arose that Kakao had pressured merchants and service partners to use its payment system exclusively. In **July 2024**, founder Kim Beom-su was arrested on charges of stock-price manipulation in connection with Kakao's 2023 acquisition contest for SM Entertainment—one of Korea's largest entertainment talent agencies. Prosecutors alleged that Kakao coordinated artificial share purchases to inflate SM Entertainment's price and block a competing bid from HYBE. The case drew significant public attention, placing South Korea's largest platform companies under intense legal and regulatory scrutiny.

**User Dependency and Lock-In**: Critics have pointed out that KakaoTalk's dominance creates switching costs and makes it difficult for alternative platforms to compete. Users adopting Kakao's services become locked into the ecosystem because nearly all their contacts, merchants, and service providers operate within it.

**Financial Sustainability and Profitability**: Despite its scale, Kakao's profitability has proven uneven. The company expanded rapidly into many markets (gaming, entertainment, fintech) with varying returns. KakaoPay's growth outpaced its profitability; KakaoBank's operations have been marginally profitable due to intense competition and regulatory constraints on interest rates. The company's diversification has sometimes resulted in lower returns than more focused competitors.

**International Expansion Difficulties**: Unlike Naver, Kakao struggled to replicate its success internationally. KakaoTalk has a significant user base among Koreans abroad and in certain diaspora communities but never achieved Naver's level of global reach (e.g., through LINE in Japan). Kakao's services have remained largely Korea-centric.

## Business Model and Revenue

Kakao's revenue derives from multiple sources:

- **Advertising**: Display ads and search ads placed within Kakao services
- **Transaction fees**: Commissions on KakaoPay transactions, KakaoBank lending, and e-commerce sales
- **Subscription services**: Premium features, music streaming, webtoon content
- **Gaming**: In-app purchases and ad revenue from casual games
- **Logistics and delivery**: Commission on transportation and food-delivery services (Kakao Taxi, Kakao Drive, meal-delivery partnerships)

The company's financial performance has been volatile; profitability peaked during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021) when digital payment and delivery services surged, then normalized as pandemic-driven growth moderated. Total revenue has generally been in the range of 4–6 trillion Korean won (~$3–5 billion USD) annually in recent years.

## Significance and Legacy

Kakao exemplifies the mobile-first internet business model that emerged in the 2010s. The company's strategy of building a superapp ecosystem—starting with a dominant communication platform and expanding into payments, banking, and commerce—parallels the strategies of WeChat (Tencent, China) and LINE (SoftBank, Japan), reflecting a global trend in digital platform development.

For South Korea specifically, Kakao represents:

1. **Mobile-First Innovation**: Kakao built for smartphones from inception, capturing the wave of rapid Korean mobile adoption and demonstrating how companies could skip legacy web-infrastructure stages.
2. **Startup Scaling**: From a messaging app founded by a serial entrepreneur, Kakao scaled to a major conglomerate, illustrating Korean startup ecosystem maturation.
3. **Digital Monopoly Risks**: The Naver–Kakao duopoly raises questions about market concentration, regulatory oversight, and the societal risks of depending on a small number of platforms for critical digital infrastructure.
4. **Financial Innovation**: KakaoPay and KakaoBank pioneered fintech and internet banking in Korea, reducing barriers to banking and payment services.
5. **Cultural Significance**: KakaoTalk has become so integral to Korean communication that its platforms and algorithms shape information flow, commerce, and social interaction in ways comparable to social media's influence in Western countries.

By the mid-2020s, Kakao and Naver remain the dominant digital platforms in Korea, controlling the digital economy and daily digital life. Regulatory efforts to promote competition and protect consumer privacy continue, but the fundamental structure of the duopoly appears resilient.
