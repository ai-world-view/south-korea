# Park Geun-hye

**Category**: People  
**Born**: February 2, 1952, Daegu, South Korea  
**Key roles**: 18th President of South Korea (2013–2017); National Assembly Member (1998–2012); First Lady (1974–1979)  
**Key figures**: Park Chung-hee (father); Choi Soon-sil (confidante, corruption-scandal central figure); Moon Jae-in (successor who granted her December 2021 pardon); Yoon Suk-yeol (prosecutor in the corruption investigation; later president)

## Summary

Park Geun-hye (박근혜, born February 2, 1952) was the 18th President of South Korea from February 2013 to March 2017, becoming the country's first and only female president and the first to be elected with a majority of the popular vote since the 1987 direct-election restoration. The daughter of Park Chung-hee — the military dictator who ruled from 1961 to 1979 — she spent her formative years at the Blue House (청와대) and served as acting First Lady from 1974 following the assassination of her mother, Yuk Young-soo, in an August 15, 1974 attack by a North Korean-linked gunman targeting her father. After her father's own assassination in October 1979, she spent the 1980s in relative political isolation before re-entering public life in the late 1990s.

Park Geun-hye's presidency ended in constitutionally unprecedented fashion: on December 9, 2016 the National Assembly voted 234–56 to impeach her, and on March 10, 2017 the Constitutional Court (헌법재판소) upheld the impeachment unanimously (8–0, with one vacancy), removing her from office. She was subsequently arrested, tried, and convicted on charges of bribery, abuse of power, and coercion in connection with a corruption scandal involving her personal confidante Choi Soon-sil (최순실). Initially sentenced to 24 years in prison and fined 18 billion won ($15 million), her combined sentences were finalized at 20 years; she was pardoned by President Moon Jae-in in December 2021 and released from custody. Her arc — from first female president to criminal conviction to pardon — encapsulates recurring tensions in South Korean democracy between institutional accountability, the chaebol-state nexus, and personal loyalty networks at the apex of power.

## Early Life and the Blue House Years

Born in Daegu into an already politically prominent family (her father was then-commanding general of the 1st Army Corps), Park Geun-hye grew up in Busan, Seoul, and the Presidential Residence. She studied electrical engineering at Sogang University — one of the few women in a technical field at the time — before transferring to graduate studies at the University of Grenoble in France in 1974. Her studies were cut short by the August 15, 1974 assassination attempt at the National Theater in Seoul: a Zainichi Korean gunman (Mun Se-gwang) acting under North Korean direction fired at her father and fatally wounded her mother instead. Park returned to South Korea and assumed the role of acting First Lady for the remaining five years of her father's rule.

These years embedded in the authoritarian apparatus formed her political worldview. She participated in diplomatic ceremonies, hosted foreign dignitaries, and managed the Blue House social functions — but was also a witness to her father's consolidation of the Yushin (유신) Constitution of 1972, which abolished direct presidential elections and allowed indefinite rule. Her mother's death and her father's assassination in 1979 by KCIA director Kim Jae-gyu shaped a disposition she later described as one of sacrifice for the nation and deep suspicion of betrayal.

## Entry into Politics (1998–2012)

Park re-entered public life in 1997, joining the conservative Grand National Party (GNP, 한나라당). She won a by-election in April 1998 in Dalseong, Daegu — a conservative stronghold — succeeding a deceased GNP incumbent. She was re-elected four times from the same constituency, building a reputation for reliability, discipline, and an almost robotic consistency that earned her the nickname "Ice Queen" (얼음공주) from the press. She cultivated a carefully managed public image — rarely photographed informally, speaking in measured cadences, and projecting an image of self-sacrifice mirroring her mother's death.

Her national prominence grew as chair of the Emergency Committee of the GNP (2004–2006), when she stabilized the party after it suffered massive losses in the 2004 parliamentary elections triggered by the failed impeachment of President Roh Moo-hyun. In 2006, during a campaign stop in Incheon, she was slashed across the cheek by a razor-wielding attacker (a 50-year-old man acting alone) — she completed the event before seeking medical treatment, reinforcing her image of stoic resilience.

She ran for the GNP presidential nomination in 2007, losing to Lee Myung-bak in a fractious primary that exposed a bitter intra-conservative split. After Lee's presidency, she reconstituted her political base through the 2012 legislative elections — in which the GNP, renamed Saenuri Party (새누리당), won a surprise majority — and won the December 2012 presidential election with 51.6% of the vote against the liberal candidate Moon Jae-in (48.0%), making her the first president elected by a majority of voters since the 1987 constitutional restoration.

## Presidency (2013–2017)

### "Creative Economy" and Economic Policy

Park's signature domestic initiative was the "Creative Economy" (창조경제), a framework intended to generate new industries through convergence of technology, culture, and traditional Korean industries. The plan established 17 "Creative Economy Innovation Centers" (창조경제혁신센터) across the country, each partnered with a major chaebol (Samsung anchoring Daegu-North Gyeongsang, Hyundai anchoring Gwangju-South Jeolla, etc.) to incubate startups and link R&D investment to regional economies. Critics argued the centers were poorly coordinated, that the chaebol involvement merely channeled subsidies to established conglomerates, and that the "creativity" branding obscured a continuation of traditional industrial policy. GDP growth during her term averaged approximately 3% annually — below South Korea's historical potential but consistent with broader global trends — while household debt surged from 88% to over 96% of GDP.

### Sewol Ferry Disaster (April 2014)

The capsize of the MV Sewol (세월호) on April 16, 2014, in which 304 of 476 passengers died (including 250 of 325 high school students from Danwon High School on a field trip to Jeju Island), devastated her presidency. Rescue operations were widely criticized as chaotic and inadequately coordinated; coast guard and maritime-safety agencies failed to mount an effective search-and-rescue effort in the critical first hours. Investigation revealed systematic regulatory failures — the ferry had been overloaded and improperly modified — and the captain and crew had abandoned ship while directing passengers to remain below deck.

Park Geun-hye's personal response deepened the crisis. She was largely absent from public view for approximately seven hours after the sinking and delivered an initial response widely seen as inadequate. The disaster became a referendum on governmental competence and the relationship between regulatory agencies and the private sector (the ferry operator, Chonghaejin Marine, had ties to a quasi-religious sect with connections to Choi Soon-sil's father, Choi Tae-min). Park's approval ratings fell from approximately 60% to under 40% within weeks and never fully recovered.

### Foreign Policy: The Park Doctrine

Park Geun-hye's "Trust-Building Process" (한반도 신뢰프로세스) framed inter-Korean relations: South Korea would engage North Korea only after demonstrable reciprocal trust-building measures, rejecting the unconditional engagement of the Sunshine Policy. In practice, this produced three years of minimal inter-Korean contact (2013–2016) punctuated by North Korean provocations:

- February 2013: North Korea's third nuclear test triggered new UN Security Council sanctions (Resolution 2094)
- August 2015: Land mine attack on South Korean soldiers near the DMZ, triggering a loudspeaker standoff
- January 2016: Fourth nuclear test (est. yield 6–10 kilotons); February 2016: Kaesong Industrial Complex closure
- September 2016: Fifth nuclear test (est. yield 10–30 kilotons)

The Kaesong closure — suspending the joint industrial zone where approximately 54,000 North Korean workers produced South Korean goods — marked the formal end of the era of unconditional engagement. Park framed it as a principled response to nuclear provocations; critics argued it eliminated South Korea's most effective leverage over North Korea while leaving 124 South Korean businesses stranded.

The THAAD deployment decision (July 2016) was the most consequential of her foreign-policy moves. The Terminal High Altitude Area Defense battery at Seongju offered protection against North Korean intermediate-range missiles, but its AN/TPY-2 radar's operational range — theoretically extending to Chinese territory — prompted Beijing's sustained economic retaliation. South Korea's diplomatic and trade relations with China deteriorated substantially and were not fully restored until late 2017 under Moon Jae-in.

Park simultaneously pursued the "Northeast Asia Peace and Cooperation Initiative" (NAPCI, 동북아 평화협력 구상) — a multilateral diplomatic framework engaging China, Japan, Russia, and North Korea on soft-security issues (climate, disaster response, public health). The framework produced limited results but signaled an attempt to construct a regional security architecture beyond the bilateral U.S. alliance.

## Corruption Scandal, Impeachment, and Trial

### The Choi Soon-sil Affair

The scandal that ended Park's presidency centered on Choi Soon-sil (최순실, who later legally changed her name to Choi Seo-won), the daughter of the late cult-leader Choi Tae-min, who had been a spiritual mentor to Park since the late 1970s following the deaths of both her parents. Park had maintained a decades-long personal relationship with Choi Soon-sil outside government channels. Investigative reporting (principally by JTBC Broadcasting, which obtained Choi's tablet computer in October 2016) revealed that Choi — with no official government role — had routinely accessed classified presidential briefings, reviewed and edited presidential speeches, and exerted influence over government policy and appointments.

Prosecutors subsequently established that Park and Choi had coerced major corporations — Samsung, Lotte, Hyundai, SK — to make donations totaling approximately 77.4 billion won ($68 million at the time) to two foundations controlled by Choi: the Mir Foundation (미르재단) and the K-Sports Foundation (케이스포츠재단). Samsung chairman Lee Jae-yong separately provided Choi's daughter with approximately 4.3 billion won in equestrian training expenses and a horse in Germany, in exchange for the National Pension Service's support of the 2015 Samsung C&T–Cheil Industries merger — a deal that consolidated the Lee family's control of Samsung Group.

### Candlelight Protests and Impeachment

The public response was unprecedented. Weekly candlelight vigils in central Seoul (Gwanghwamun Plaza, 광화문광장) drew millions from October to December 2016 — the November 12 rally alone drew an estimated 1–1.5 million participants in Seoul alone, with satellite rallies across the country. Organized by the "Candlelight Civic Action" coalition (촛불시민행동), the protests remained overwhelmingly peaceful and became a landmark of participatory democracy. Park's approval rating fell to approximately 4–5% by November 2016.

The National Assembly voted to impeach Park Geun-hye on December 9, 2016, by 234 votes to 56 (with 7 invalid and 2 abstentions), far exceeding the two-thirds threshold. Notably, approximately 60 Saenuri (her own party) lawmakers voted for impeachment. Prime Minister Hwang Kyo-ahn served as acting president during the Constitutional Court's review. On March 10, 2017, the eight-justice court upheld impeachment unanimously, the first-ever removal of a sitting South Korean president by judicial review. Elections were held within 60 days, and Moon Jae-in won on May 9, 2017.

### Trial and Sentencing

Park was arrested on March 31, 2017. Her trial before the Seoul Central District Court addressed multiple charges: bribery (accepting illicit corporate contributions), abuse of power (coercing companies into making donations), unlawful disclosure of classified information to Choi Soon-sil, and other counts. She refused to cooperate with the court for much of the proceedings, boycotting hearings.

In April 2018, she was convicted and sentenced to 24 years in prison and fined 18 billion won; in a separate July 2018 trial she received an additional eight years for unlawfully receiving intelligence-agency funds and for a 2016 election-law violation. Samsung's Lee Jae-yong was convicted in a separate trial; his sentence went through multiple appeals and remands. On appeal her bribery-and-abuse sentence was raised to 25 years, but after the Supreme Court ordered a retrial, a January 2021 Supreme Court ruling finalized her combined sentences at 20 years in prison plus fines.

### Pardon and Release

The opposition presidential candidate Yoon Suk-yeol indicated in late 2021 that he favored a pardon for Park Geun-hye on humanitarian grounds (she had been diagnosed with multiple health conditions including arthritis and dental problems). President Moon Jae-in — who had defeated Park's party candidate and whose own political career was inextricably linked to her prosecution — signed the pardon on December 24, 2021. Park was released from Seoul Prison on December 24, 2021 after four years, nine months of incarceration. She returned to her childhood home in Daegu and has maintained a low public profile since release, declining interviews and avoiding public appearances.

## Legacy

Park Geun-hye's historical significance is multifaceted and contested. As South Korea's first female president, her election in 2012 was widely celebrated as a gender milestone — yet critics note that her path to the presidency ran through dynastic inheritance and the conservative political establishment rather than feminist movement politics, and that her administration did little to advance gender-equality legislation. Her prime ministers (Chung Hong-won, followed by Hwang Kyo-ahn) and her cabinets were predominantly male, and her administration advanced little gender-equality legislation despite its symbolic gender framing.

Her prosecution is the third time a former South Korean president has faced criminal conviction (following Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo's 1996 convictions for the Gwangju massacre and corruption, and Lee Myung-bak's 2018 corruption conviction). The pattern has fueled both pride — demonstrating that no one is above the law — and concern that presidential prosecution has become a politicized instrument wielded by successors against predecessors regardless of evidence quality.

The Choi Soon-sil scandal exposed the structural vulnerability of the Korean presidency to informal networks of personal loyalty, the absence of robust gatekeeping mechanisms for who can access classified information, and the depth of the chaebol-state transactional relationship — issues that subsequent reforms only partially addressed.
