# Yoon Suk-yeol

**Yoon Suk-yeol** (윤석열; born December 18, 1960) served as the 20th president of South Korea from May 10, 2022 until April 4, 2025, when the Constitutional Court unanimously upheld his impeachment following his declaration of martial law in December 2024. A former prosecutor-general who had never held elected office before the presidency, Yoon won the 2022 election with one of the narrowest margins in South Korean democratic history—0.73 percentage points—completing the conservative return to power after Moon Jae-in's progressive five-year tenure. His three-year presidency is now most associated with the martial-law crisis of December 3–4, 2024, a constitutional rupture that ended his political career and led to criminal charges for insurrection.

## Early Life and Education

Yoon was born in Seoul into an academic family; his father, Yoon Ki-joong, was an economics professor at Yonsei University. Yoon studied law at Seoul National University, graduating in 1982, and went on to pass the rigorous Korean bar examination (사법시험), completing his judicial training in the early 1990s. His background was entirely legal rather than political: he spent the following three decades as a career prosecutor, working his way through regional offices in Daegu, Suwon, and Seoul before joining the elite Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office, which handles the most sensitive national-level criminal cases.

## Prosecutorial Career and Rise to Prominence

Yoon built his public reputation through anti-corruption investigations. As a junior prosecutor he worked on cases linked to political graft, and by the 2010s he had risen to lead special investigation units. His most consequential early assignment came under the Park Geun-hye government (2013–2017): Yoon led the 2013 investigation into the National Intelligence Service's covert online opinion-manipulation campaign, in which NIS agents had posted messages to influence the 2012 presidential election. His insistence on pursuing the case put him in direct tension with the conservative government he served; after testifying to the National Assembly that his investigation had faced political interference, he was demoted to provincial posts.

Under Moon Jae-in's administration (2017–2022), Yoon was promoted rapidly. He became head of the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office (2017–2019), where his office prosecuted former President Lee Myung-bak on bribery and embezzlement charges (Lee was convicted in 2018 and sentenced to 15 years, later increased to 17 years on appeal and finalized by the Supreme Court in October 2020) and pursued cases related to Park Geun-hye's corruption networks. His aggressive but non-partisan approach—targeting figures across the political spectrum—elevated his public standing as an independent legal figure.

Moon appointed Yoon as prosecutor-general (검찰총장) in July 2019, the apex of the prosecutorial hierarchy. The relationship between the two broke down rapidly. Yoon's office launched investigations into Moon's former justice minister Cho Kuk and several Moon administration officials over alleged academic credential fraud, the abuse of justice-ministry powers, and financial irregularities. The Cho Kuk controversy (2019–2020) produced months of mass protests both supporting and opposing the investigation, and became a defining cultural and political flashpoint about prosecutorial power and academic privilege. Yoon clashed repeatedly with Justice Minister Choo Mi-ae over investigative scope and staffing decisions; the feud became a proxy war between the presidency and the prosecution. In February 2021, Yoon announced his resignation from the prosecutor-general position, framing it as a defense of institutional independence.

## Rise to Presidency (2021–2022)

Yoon's resignation made him a conservative cause célèbre. Polls showed high name recognition and antipathy toward the Moon administration among voters tired of the progressive government's scandals, its handling of the COVID-19 pandemic economy, and its perceived leniency toward North Korea. In June 2021, after only months out of public office, Yoon announced his candidacy for the People Power Party (PPP) presidential nomination. His campaign was characterized by populist anti-establishment rhetoric despite his elite prosecutorial career, and he won the PPP nomination in November 2021, defeating former party leaders with more conventional political backgrounds.

The March 9, 2022 presidential election pitted Yoon against Lee Jae-myung, the former Gyeonggi Province governor and Democratic Party candidate. Yoon won 48.56% to Lee's 47.83%—a margin of 247,000 votes out of roughly 34 million cast, the closest result since South Korea's direct presidential elections resumed in 1987. Turnout was 77.1%. Yoon's victory drew heavily from male voters under 30 (an unusual demographic coalition), conservatives, and voters in the Daegu-Gyeongbuk region, while Lee ran strongly in Honam (South Jeolla) and among older progressive voters. Yoon was inaugurated on May 10, 2022.

## Presidency: Foreign Policy and Security

### Korea–US–Japan Trilateral Alignment

Yoon's most consequential foreign policy achievement was the Camp David Summit of August 18, 2023, where he, President Biden, and Japanese Prime Minister Kishida Fumio established a formal trilateral security framework including regular intelligence-sharing, coordinated military exercises, and joint commitments to mutual consultation in the event of a crisis. This was a historic step: South Korea and Japan, whose bilateral relationship had been frozen by disputes over wartime labor conscription and "comfort women" compensation, restored high-level diplomatic and security cooperation for the first time in years. Yoon's decision to soften historical grievances to enable strategic alignment drew intense domestic criticism from progressive parties and victims' groups.

### North Korea Policy

Yoon reversed Moon's engagement framework. His administration conditioned any talks on denuclearization, rejected the Panmunjom Declaration process, and intensified military exercises with U.S. forces. The USS Ronald Reagan aircraft carrier made multiple port calls in Busan under Yoon, signaling recommitment to extended deterrence. In 2023, the Washington Declaration formalized enhanced U.S. nuclear deterrence commitments, including consultation mechanisms and periodic deployments of nuclear-capable submarines to Korean ports.

North Korea responded with intensified weapons testing: intercontinental ballistic missile tests in 2022–2023, including the first full-ICBM range tests in years, and short-range ballistic missiles targeting South Korean coastal cities. In late 2024, Yoon's government publicly disclosed intelligence indicating that North Korea had deployed approximately 10,000–12,000 troops to Russia's Kursk region to assist Russian forces, an unprecedented extraterritorial deployment marking the deepest military cooperation between Pyongyang and Moscow since the Cold War.

### Japan Normalization

In March 2023, Yoon's government announced a third-party compensation mechanism for Korean victims of Japanese wartime forced labor, using Korean public and private funds rather than Japanese government reparations. The arrangement allowed a "solution" without Japanese companies paying directly, unfreezing diplomatic and security relations. Japan resumed export license treatment of South Korea as a "white list" nation in July 2023, restoring export approvals for critical semiconductor materials (fluorine resists, high-purity hydrogen fluoride) that Japan had restricted in 2019. The normalization was welcomed by the business community and the United States but fiercely opposed by Korean progressive groups and victims' advocacy organizations.

## Presidency: Domestic Policy

### Economic Record

Yoon inherited an economy recovering from COVID-19 but facing a semiconductor industry downturn (2022–2023) that hit Samsung and SK Hynix earnings sharply. Global DRAM prices fell more than 50% from their 2022 peak amid inventory corrections, and Samsung's semiconductor division posted its worst quarterly results in years in 2023. Yoon's government responded with targeted R&D tax credits and the K-Semiconductor Strategy (announced 2021, expanded 2023) to support the chip ecosystem. Recovery came by 2024, driven by AI-related HBM (high-bandwidth memory) demand: SK Hynix emerged as the dominant supplier of HBM3 and HBM3E chips used in NVIDIA's H100 and H200 data-center GPUs, and Samsung accelerated its own HBM development.

Inflation, which spiked globally in 2022, remained elevated domestically, particularly in food and housing. Youth unemployment remained persistently higher than headline figures suggested, and the housing market—deregulated from Moon's strict price controls—saw speculative surges in Seoul before a 2023 correction in apartment prices. Income inequality widened, a persistent structural problem that neither progressive nor conservative administrations resolved.

### Itaewon Crowd Crush (October 2022)

On October 29, 2022, a deadly crowd surge in the Itaewon district of Seoul during Halloween festivities killed 159 people—the deadliest mass-casualty disaster in South Korea in decades. The crush occurred in a narrow alleyway near Hamilton Hotel as tens of thousands gathered without adequate crowd-control planning. The tragedy exposed failures in municipal event management and national emergency response. Yoon declared a national period of mourning. The interior minister and several police officials faced accountability proceedings; the political aftermath deepened distrust of government capacity and was a formative event for Yoon's public image.

### Divided Government and Legislative Conflict

Yoon's PPP narrowly controlled the National Assembly only briefly. The April 10, 2024 legislative elections delivered a sweeping defeat: the Democratic Party and allied progressive parties won approximately 192 seats in the 300-seat Assembly, well above the 200-seat supermajority threshold when combined with smaller allies. PPP won only around 108 seats. The outcome reflected voter dissatisfaction with Yoon's governance and first-lady controversies (see below).

From late 2022 through his impeachment, Yoon governed against an opposition supermajority that could override presidential vetoes, subpoena cabinet officials, and block legislation. The Democratic Party passed multiple bills the Yoon administration opposed—including media reform legislation and criminal investigation reform bills—which Yoon vetoed repeatedly, only for the Assembly to re-pass them. This cycle of legislative activism and executive veto produced a governance deadlock that contributed to Yoon's sense of political besiegement.

### First Lady Controversies

First Lady Kim Keon-hee became a liability for the administration. In 2023, video recordings emerged showing her accepting a Dior handbag from a pastor, which critics alleged violated anti-bribery laws (gifts above 300,000 won to public figures and their spouses are illegal under the Kim Young-ran Act). The prosecution, led by Yoon-appointed officials, declined to pursue the case. More significantly, allegations arose that she had manipulated stock prices of Deutsche Motors Korea (a car dealership) in 2009–2012, profiting from artificial inflation of the company's shares. The investigation had been ongoing since the early 2020s; opposition lawmakers and civil society groups accused the Yoon administration of suppressing prosecution of its own first lady. These controversies severely damaged public trust and contributed to the 2024 election rout.

## The December 2024 Martial-Law Crisis

### Declaration and Immediate Reaction

At 10:30 PM on December 3, 2024, Yoon delivered a televised address and signed an emergency martial-law declaration (비상계엄), the first such declaration since 1980. He justified it as necessary to counter "anti-state forces" aligned with North Korea within the Democratic Party, claiming the opposition's repeated vetoes and subpoenas amounted to legislative insurgency threatening national security. No prime-ministerial countersignature was obtained, a procedural irregularity that constitutional scholars immediately flagged as illegal under Article 77 of the Constitution.

Military units moved toward the National Assembly building within hours. Special Forces soldiers attempted to restrict access to the chamber. However, members of the Assembly—including PPP members—pushed through cordons and gathered for an emergency session. At approximately 1:00 AM on December 4, the Assembly voted 190-0 (with no opposing votes and no abstentions counted, as supporters simply did not participate) to demand the immediate lifting of martial law under Article 77(5), which empowers the Assembly to void martial-law declarations. Thousands of citizens gathered outside the Assembly in spontaneous candlelight vigils that recalled the 1987 democracy movement and the 2016–2017 Park Geun-hye protests.

### Rescission and Fallout

Faced with the Assembly vote, Yoon rescinded the martial-law decree at approximately 4:30 AM on December 4, 2024. By dawn, the immediate crisis had passed, but the political situation was irreparably damaged. Constitutional scholars, former prime ministers, and even PPP leaders condemned the declaration as an unconstitutional and reckless act. Approval ratings, which had already fallen below 30%, collapsed further.

The Cabinet discussed invoking Article 65 (impeachment motion) within days. On December 14, 2024, the National Assembly passed an impeachment motion against Yoon by a vote of 204-85, with 12 PPP defections accounting for the margin above the two-thirds threshold needed to initiate the process. Yoon was suspended from presidential duties; Prime Minister Han Duck-soo assumed acting presidential functions. Yoon's security detail was reduced; he remained in the presidential residence (Cheong Wa Dae's successor compound in Yongsan) as prosecutors and the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO) initiated investigations into the insurrection charge.

On January 15, 2025, the CIO arrested Yoon—the first sitting (suspended) president in South Korean history to be physically detained—after he initially refused to comply with an investigation summons. He was held at the Seoul Detention Center while authorities built the insurrection case. Insurrection (내란) is one of only two crimes for which a sitting president has no immunity under the constitution (the other being treason), making the criminal prosecution legally straightforward even before the Constitutional Court ruled on the impeachment.

## Constitutional Court Verdict and Post-Presidency

### Removal from Office (April 4, 2025)

The Constitutional Court—sitting with eight justices, one seat left vacant amid appointment disputes—heard oral arguments and reviewed evidence from December 2024 through March 2025. On April 4, 2025, the court issued its verdict: a unanimous 8-0 decision upholding the impeachment and permanently removing Yoon Suk-yeol from the presidency. The ruling found that his declaration of martial law had failed to meet the substantive and procedural requirements of Article 77, that the deployment of military forces against the National Assembly constituted a grave violation of the constitutional order and the separation of powers, and that his actions met the threshold for removal under Article 65. The decision was read aloud by Acting Chief Justice Moon Hyung-bae in a nationally televised session.

The verdict triggered immediate reactions: supporters gathered outside the court with South Korean flags, while opposition lawmakers celebrated. Han Duck-soo remained as acting president pending the snap election required by law within 60 days of a president's removal.

### Snap Presidential Election (June 2025)

The snap election held on June 3, 2025 returned Lee Jae-myung—who had narrowly lost to Yoon in 2022—to the presidency at the head of the Democratic Party. Lee won approximately 49.4% of the vote; the PPP candidate, Kim Moon-soo, won approximately 41%, with the Reform Party's Lee Jun-seok taking roughly 8%. Lee's margin, far wider than Yoon's razor-thin 2022 win, reflected the shock of the martial-law episode. Lee was inaugurated on June 10, 2025 and immediately announced investigations into the full circumstances of the December 2024 events.

### Criminal Prosecution

As of 2026, Yoon faces criminal trial on insurrection charges. Insurrection (내란죄 — Article 87 of the Criminal Code) carries a maximum penalty of death or life imprisonment. Prosecutors allege that his martial-law declaration, the deployment of troops to the Assembly, and orders to detain opposition lawmakers constituted a deliberate attempt to subvert constitutional government. Additional charges under investigation include abuse of authority, obstruction of the National Assembly, and bribery (related to the Kim Keon-hee handbag controversy). His criminal trial is expected to extend into 2027. Former allies have largely distanced themselves; a small "Yoon supporter" bloc within the PPP maintains that the charges are politically motivated.

## Historical Assessment

Yoon Suk-yeol's presidency is assessed in the context of South Korea's democratic consolidation. On the positive side: the Camp David trilateral framework strengthened regional security architecture; the Japan normalization unlocked strategic semiconductor supply-chain cooperation; and the HBM-driven semiconductor recovery of 2024 maintained South Korea's competitive position in the AI infrastructure market. Critics point to the Itaewon disaster's response failures, a worsening housing affordability crisis, rollback of progressive labor protections, and the catastrophic overreach of the December 2024 martial-law declaration—an action that, even if motivated by genuine political frustration, had no democratic legitimacy and set back institutional trust by a generation.

His case joins Park Geun-hye (impeached 2017, convicted of corruption) and Lee Myung-bak (convicted 2018, later pardoned by Yoon himself) as the third consecutive conservative president to face post-presidency (or mid-presidency) legal accountability—a pattern that reveals both the depth of South Korean judicial independence and the fragility of democratic norms under executive stress. The pattern also illustrates the polarization of South Korean politics in the 21st century: no presidency since Roh Tae-woo (1988–1993) has ended without significant legal jeopardy for the outgoing executive or their coalition.
