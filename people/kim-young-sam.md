# Kim Young-sam

**Category**: People  
**Born**: December 20, 1927, Geoje Island, South Gyeongsang Province  
**Died**: November 22, 2015, Seoul (age 87)  
**Served**: February 25, 1993 – February 25, 1998 (14th President of South Korea)

## Summary

Kim Young-sam (Korean: 김영삼) served as the fourteenth president of South Korea from 1993 to 1998, representing the country's first civilian-led transition of power following decades of military-backed governance. A career opposition politician who spent decades challenging authoritarian rule, he ultimately reached the presidency through a controversial 1990 three-party merger with Roh Tae-woo's ruling party—a pragmatic alliance that brought him to power as the ruling-party candidate in the December 1992 election, which he won with 42.0% of the vote against opposition leader Kim Dae-jung (33.8%) and business magnate Chung Ju-yung (16.3%).

His five-year single-term presidency (the Korean constitution prohibits re-election) achieved significant milestones in democratic consolidation: the prosecution of former presidents Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo for treason and corruption, purging of the *Hanahoe* military clique, the Real-Name Financial Transaction System reform, South Korea's accession to the OECD (December 1996), and the *Segyehwa* (Globalization) policy. However, his term ended in crisis: the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis required an emergency IMF bailout of approximately $57–58 billion, and endemic corruption implicating his son Kim Hyun-chul severely damaged his legacy. Kim Young-sam is remembered as a transitional figure—embodying democratic aspiration while also exposing the persistent weaknesses of Korea's political economy.

## Early Life and Political Formation

Born on Geoje Island (거제도) in South Gyeongsang Province on December 20, 1927, Kim Young-sam grew up in a fishing and farming household. He studied philosophy at Seoul National University and graduated in 1951 amid the Korean War. He became politically active immediately after graduation, aligning with liberal and democratic traditions in the early years of the First Republic under Syngman Rhee.

In 1954, at age 26, Kim Young-sam was elected to the Third National Assembly (국회), becoming the youngest member in the assembly's history—a distinction he would carry throughout his career as a symbol of youthful democratic idealism. He was elected to the National Assembly nine times over his career—a record—representing South Gyeongsang and Busan constituencies including his native Geoje.

## Opposition Career (1950s–1987)

### Under Park Chung-hee

Kim Young-sam emerged as a prominent opposition figure during Park Chung-hee's authoritarian rule (1961–1979). He was a leader of the New Democratic Party (NDP) in the 1970s, consistently demanding restoration of democratic rights and civilian governance. His confrontational style included a 1969 attempt to block Park's constitutional amendment permitting a third presidential term.

In 1979, Kim became president of the New Democratic Party and publicly called for U.S. pressure on the Park government to restore democracy—comments that provoked Park's government to expel him from the National Assembly, an extraordinary act that helped precipitate the political crisis of late 1979 leading to Park's assassination in October 1979.

### Under Chun Doo-hwan

Following Chun Doo-hwan's military coup (December 1979) and assumption of the presidency, Kim Young-sam remained in domestic opposition despite severe restrictions. He was placed under house arrest multiple times and faced travel bans. In May 1983 he conducted a **23-day hunger strike** to protest Chun's government and demand democratic reform—an act that drew international attention and domestic sympathy. Earlier, in 1969, he had survived an acid-throwing attack on his car by unknown assailants, and he received multiple threats over the course of his opposition career.

He participated in the 1985 National Assembly election under the newly formed New Korea Democratic Party (NKDP), which won surprising voter support as the main opposition force. The 1985 results demonstrated that urban voters, particularly in Busan and Seoul, rejected Chun's authoritarian governance despite the suppression of opposition activity.

### The 1987 June Struggle and Presidential Bid

The mass protests of June 1987 (*6월 민주항쟁*), triggered by student demonstrations against Chun's regime and police violence, culminated in Roh Tae-woo's June 29 Declaration promising direct presidential elections. Kim Young-sam and Kim Dae-jung both claimed to represent the democratic opposition, but their refusal to unite on a single candidate split the opposition vote in the December 1987 election. Roh Tae-woo won with 36.6% of the vote while Kim Young-sam received 28.0% and Kim Dae-jung 27.1%. The split between the two "Kims" was widely criticized as the primary cause of the democratic opposition's defeat.

## The 1990 Three-Party Merger and Path to Power

In January 1990, Kim Young-sam made the most consequential—and most criticized—decision of his pre-presidential career: he agreed to merge his Reunification Democratic Party with Roh Tae-woo's Democratic Justice Party and Kim Jong-pil's New Democratic Republican Party, forming the **Democratic Liberal Party (민주자유당)**. Critics, including Kim Dae-jung, condemned the merger as a betrayal of democratic principles—joining the ruling party of a former military general to gain access to power. Supporters argued that working within the system was the only realistic path to civilian governance.

The merger gave the new DLP a commanding National Assembly majority and positioned Kim Young-sam as the ruling party's candidate for the 1992 presidential election. He won that election on December 18, 1992, with 42.0% of the vote versus Kim Dae-jung's 33.8% and Hyundai founder Chung Ju-yung's 16.3%.

## Presidency (February 25, 1993 – February 25, 1998)

### Military Reform: Purging the *Hanahoe*

Within days of his inauguration, Kim Young-sam moved decisively against the *Hanahoe* (하나회), a secret military fraternity founded by Chun Doo-hwan in 1963 that had dominated the Korean Army's officer corps and served as the organizational backbone of the 1979 and 1980 coups. In March 1993, Kim ordered the removal from command of *Hanahoe*-affiliated generals, effectively breaking the clique's grip on the military chain of command. This act—unprecedented in scope—is widely considered the most significant civil-military reform in South Korean history, subordinating the armed forces to genuine civilian presidential authority for the first time.

### Real-Name Financial Transaction System (금융실명제)

On August 12, 1993, Kim Young-sam issued Emergency Presidential Decree No. 16, requiring **all financial accounts to be held under real names** (*geum-yung silmyeong-je*). Previously, Koreans could hold bank accounts and conduct financial transactions under pseudonyms or third-party names, facilitating tax evasion, political slush funds, and money laundering. The real-name system was introduced overnight by surprise to prevent capital flight. It is considered one of the most structurally significant anti-corruption measures in Korean financial history, though enforcement remained imperfect.

### Prosecution of Former Presidents

In 1995–1996, Kim Young-sam's government pursued legal proceedings against Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo for their roles in the 1979 coup d'état (*Military Revolution*), the December 1979 internal coup within the military (*12.12 Military Incident*), the May 1980 Gwangju Uprising suppression, and for accumulating billions of won in slush funds from chaebol executives during their presidencies. The trials were enabled by a new law—the *Special Act on the May 18 Democratization Movement*—that retroactively allowed prosecution of the coup leaders despite statute-of-limitations concerns.

**Chun Doo-hwan** was sentenced to **death** in August 1996 for mutiny, treason, and corruption; the sentence was later reduced to life imprisonment on appeal. **Roh Tae-woo** was sentenced to **22.5 years** in prison and fined 262.8 billion won (approximately $288 million); his term was reduced to 17 years on appeal. On December 22, 1997—during the transition to Kim Dae-jung's administration—Kim Young-sam pardoned both men in consultation with president-elect Kim Dae-jung, framing the decision as necessary for "national reconciliation" ahead of the IMF crisis management. The pardons were widely criticized as premature.

### *Segyehwa* (Globalization) Policy

Proclaimed in Sydney, Australia, in November 1994—on the margins of the APEC summit held that month in Bogor, Indonesia—*Segyehwa* (세계화, "globalization") was Kim Young-sam's signature economic-cultural doctrine. It sought to modernize Korea's regulatory and administrative systems, accelerate trade liberalization, promote Korean culture internationally, and raise Korea's status in international organizations. Concrete measures included:

- Administrative reform to reduce bureaucratic red tape and align Korean standards with OECD norms
- Educational curriculum reforms to increase English-language instruction
- Support for Korean cultural exports (a precursor to the *Hallyu* wave that accelerated under Kim Dae-jung)
- Accelerated accession negotiations with the OECD

South Korea formally joined the **OECD** on December 12, 1996, as its 29th member—a milestone framed as confirmation of Korea's arrival as a developed economy.

### Financial Sector Liberalization and Seeds of Crisis

A less celebrated aspect of *Segyehwa* was the rapid liberalization of Korea's capital account and short-term foreign borrowing regulations. Following OECD membership requirements and IMF recommendations, the Kim Young-sam government removed controls on short-term capital inflows during 1993–1996. Korean banks and chaebol affiliates borrowed heavily from international lenders in short-term foreign-currency loans, investing in long-term domestic projects and rolling over debts continuously. This structural mismatch—short-term foreign liabilities financing long-term domestic investments—created extreme vulnerability to sudden capital flight.

### Corruption Scandals and the Kim Hyun-chul Affair

Despite the Real-Name Financial System reform, the Kim Young-sam administration became entangled in corruption scandals. His second son, **Kim Hyun-chul** (김현철)—widely nicknamed the "little president" (*sotongnyeong*) for his perceived behind-the-scenes influence—was implicated in influence-peddling and accepting money from businessmen; he was indicted and convicted in 1997 on tax-evasion and bribery charges. Other aides and associates were implicated in a broader network of patronage, and the collapse of the Hanbo Steel group in early 1997 exposed politically connected lending. These scandals undermined the administration's anti-corruption narrative and contributed to public disillusionment in the final years of the presidency.

Kim Young-sam's own image was further clouded by allegations, aired during the later prosecution of Chun and Roh, that his 1992 campaign had benefited from political funds channeled by Roh Tae-woo—the very slush funds for which Roh was convicted.

### The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis

The Asian Financial Crisis struck South Korea with devastating force in the second half of 1997. The Thai baht's collapse in July 1997 triggered contagion across Southeast and Northeast Asia. Foreign investors began withdrawing short-term loans from Korean financial institutions; the Korean won fell sharply against the U.S. dollar. South Korea's foreign exchange reserves, which had stood above $30 billion earlier in 1997, fell to a critically low **$7.3 billion** by November 1997—insufficient to meet immediate debt repayment obligations.

On November 21, 1997, with Kim Young-sam's term entering its final months, the South Korean government formally requested IMF assistance. The resulting IMF-led rescue package totaled approximately **$57–58 billion** (the largest IMF bailout up to that point), provided by the IMF ($21 billion), World Bank, Asian Development Bank, and bilateral lenders including the United States and Japan. The package came with severe conditionality: interest rate increases, fiscal austerity, financial-sector restructuring, and labor-market reforms.

The Korean won fell from approximately 900 KRW/USD in early 1997 to nearly **1,900 KRW/USD** at the crisis nadir in January 1998. Unemployment rose sharply; a succession of major chaebol—including Hanbo Steel, Sammi, and Kia Motors—had already collapsed or entered court receivership during 1997, and many more required government-arranged restructuring after the bailout.

Kim Young-sam transferred power to Kim Dae-jung on February 25, 1998, leaving his successor to manage the IMF program and the deep recession that followed. The crisis transformed public memory of Kim Young-sam's presidency: his earlier democratic achievements were overshadowed by the economic catastrophe of his final months.

## Legacy and Assessment

### Democratic Consolidation

Kim Young-sam's achievement in consolidating civilian governance over the military represents a foundational contribution to South Korean democracy. The dismantling of the *Hanahoe* was irreversible: subsequent presidents have operated without a comparable threat of military political intervention. The prosecution of Chun and Roh—even if ultimately reversed by pardon—established the principle that former presidents could be held legally accountable for abuses of office.

The Real-Name Financial Transaction System and administrative reforms advanced institutional modernization. OECD membership formally acknowledged Korea's transition to developed-economy status. *Segyehwa*, despite its association with the capital-account liberalization that contributed to the 1997 crisis, correctly anticipated the direction of Korea's global integration.

### Limitations and Contradictions

The 1997 financial crisis is indelibly associated with Kim Young-sam's presidency in public consciousness, even though the structural causes—chaebol over-leverage, risky financial liberalization, current-account deficits—accumulated over decades and were exacerbated by global contagion. The IMF's own 2003 retrospective acknowledged that Korea's crisis was in part driven by overly rapid capital-account liberalization encouraged by international institutions including the IMF and OECD.

The corruption scandals of the Kim Hyun-chul affair illustrated the persistence of patronage networks within Korean democratic politics—a challenge that would recur under subsequent administrations, including those of Park Geun-hye (2013–2017) and Lee Myung-bak (2008–2013).

### Historical Position

Together with Kim Dae-jung (who succeeded him and won the 2000 Nobel Peace Prize), Kim Young-sam is credited with embedding competitive party democracy and civilian authority as durable features of South Korean political life. The alternation of power between Kim Young-sam's party and Kim Dae-jung's in 1998 demonstrated that democratic transfer between civilian rivals of opposing regional and ideological backgrounds was possible—an achievement that was far from guaranteed given Korea's political polarization.

Kim Young-sam died in Seoul on November 22, 2015, at the age of 87. A state funeral was held, attended by leaders across the Korean political spectrum.
