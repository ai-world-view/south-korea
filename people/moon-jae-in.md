# Moon Jae-in

**Moon Jae-in** (문재인, born January 24, 1953) was the 10th president of South Korea (May 2017–May 2022), leading the nation through a period of diplomatic engagement with North Korea, domestic polarization, and the COVID-19 pandemic. A human-rights lawyer and Democratic politician with roots in South Gyeongsang Province, he revived the Sunshine Policy approach of Kim Dae-jung, pursued three historic inter-Korean summits, and presided over the 2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics. His presidency ended amid economic dissatisfaction and post-presidential legal jeopardy. He won the 2017 election with 41.1% of the vote in a five-candidate field following the impeachment of Park Geun-hye.

## Early Life and Legal Career

Moon Jae-in was born on January 24, 1953, in **Geoje** (거제), a shipbuilding island-city in South Gyeongsang Province. His parents were refugees from the **Korean War**, originally from South Hamgyong Province (now North Korea); they were evacuated to Geoje on one of the last refugee ships from Hungnam harbor in December 1950. This family displacement from the North—a direct consequence of the war that split the **Korean Independence Movement**'s legacy—shaped Moon's lifelong commitment to inter-Korean engagement.

Moon attended Gyeongnam High School and gained entry to **Kyung Hee University School of Law** in 1972 on a scholarship. His studies were interrupted when he was arrested and imprisoned for participating in student protests against the Park Chung-hee military regime's Yushin Constitution. After completing military service as a Special Forces paratrooper (where he placed first in his unit and was offered but declined an officer commission), he returned to Kyung Hee University and graduated with a law degree in 1980.

He passed the rigorous bar examination in 1980 but received a disqualification from the judiciary due to his prior arrest record. This disqualification channeled him into private legal practice rather than the judiciary. Moon established a practice in **Busan** with longtime friend and future President **Roh Moo-hyun** (노무현), and the two operated a prominent human-rights law firm that defended labor union organizers, political dissidents, and victims of state violence during the Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo military governments of the 1980s. Moon was again briefly imprisoned in 1980 for his role in the post–Gwangju Uprising student protests. His legal practice and activism with the **Busan Lawyers for Democracy** (부산 민주화운동) became his moral signature.

## Path to the Presidency

### Role Under Roh Moo-hyun

Moon entered national politics through his relationship with Roh Moo-hyun. When Roh won the 2002 presidential election, Moon followed him to Seoul, serving first as Senior Secretary for Civil Affairs and then, from 2007, as **Presidential Chief of Staff** (청와대 비서실장)—the highest non-cabinet staff position in the Blue House. Under Roh, Moon was involved in foreign-policy formulation, administrative reform, and the management of Roh's progressive legislative agenda. After Roh's presidency ended in 2008 and Roh returned to his hometown of Bongha Village, Moon remained close to him. When Roh committed suicide in May 2009, facing corruption investigations that his supporters characterized as politically motivated, Moon gave the eulogy at his state funeral and inherited Roh's progressive political network.

### Democratic Party Career and 2012 Presidential Bid

Moon was elected to the National Assembly in 2012, representing the Sasang constituency in Busan. He ran for the presidential nomination of the Democratic United Party and won in a contested primary. In the **December 2012 presidential election**, Moon lost to **Park Geun-hye** (the conservative candidate and daughter of Park Chung-hee) by a margin of 51.6% to 48.0%—approximately 1.08 million votes. The narrow loss was widely attributed to a last-minute withdrawal of centrist candidate Ahn Cheol-soo in Moon's favor that arrived too late to consolidate progressive voters.

### The Candlelight Revolution and 2017 Election

Park Geun-hye's presidency unraveled in 2016 due to the **Choi Soon-sil scandal**, in which it emerged that Park had shared classified government documents with and accepted policy influence from Choi Soon-sil, a private citizen with no security clearance and the daughter of a cult-like religious figure who had been close to Park's family. Samsung heir **Lee Jae-yong** was found to have bribed Choi through proxy foundations in exchange for government support for a Samsung merger. The scandal catalyzed the **Candlelight Revolution** (촛불혁명): weekly mass protests in central Seoul beginning in October 2016 that peaked at approximately 2.3 million participants on November 26, 2016—the largest single-day demonstration in South Korean history. The sustained protest movement was largely peaceful and self-organized, a demonstration of civil society's maturation.

The National Assembly voted 234–56 on **December 9, 2016** to impeach Park Geun-hye, suspending her from office. The Constitutional Court unanimously upheld the impeachment on **March 10, 2017**, removing Park from office and triggering a snap election within 60 days. Moon, having maintained party leadership during the scandal, ran as the progressive candidate and won the **May 9, 2017 presidential election** with **41.1% of the vote** against a field of five major candidates: conservative Hong Joon-pyo received 24.0%, centrist Ahn Cheol-soo received 21.4%, and two others split the remainder. Moon's victory was decisive in the pluralized field and carried a strong mandate for democratic accountability.

## Presidency (2017–2022)

### Diplomacy and Inter-Korean Relations

Moon's presidency is best known for its pursuit of diplomatic engagement with North Korea, reversing the "pressure and isolation" posture of Lee Myung-bak and Park Geun-hye. The opportunity arose in early 2018 when North Korea, having completed a series of missile and nuclear weapons tests in 2017 (including the first confirmed test of a hydrogen bomb on September 3, 2017), expressed willingness to participate in the **2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics**.

Moon facilitated an invitation to North Korea, and a joint North-South Korean team marched together under the **Korean Unification Flag** (한반도기) at the PyeongChang opening ceremony on February 9, 2018. A unified Korean women's ice hockey team competed, despite concerns from South Korean players about roster displacement. Kim Jong-un's sister, **Kim Yo-jong**, attended the opening ceremony and delivered an invitation to Moon to visit Pyongyang.

**Three inter-Korean summits** occurred under Moon:

1. **First summit (April 27, 2018)**: At the DMZ in Panmunjom, Moon and Kim Jong-un met face-to-face for the first time, with Kim crossing the Military Demarcation Line—the first North Korean leader to set foot in South Korean territory since the 1953 Armistice. They signed the **Panmunjom Declaration for Peace, Prosperity and Unification of the Korean Peninsula**, pledging cessation of hostilities, pursuit of a peace treaty, denuclearization discussions, and resumed family reunion meetings.

2. **Second summit (May 26, 2018)**: A low-key second meeting at Panmunjom, held in North Korean territory, to coordinate messaging ahead of the planned Trump-Kim summit.

3. **Third summit (September 18–20, 2018)**: The most elaborate, held in Pyongyang with an entourage of South Korean business leaders, celebrities, and media. Moon addressed a 150,000-person mass gathering in Pyongyang's May Day Stadium—the largest audience ever addressed by a South Korean president. The summit produced agreements on denuclearization measures (dismantling the Dongchang-ri missile engine test site, pledging to close the Yongbyon nuclear complex under reciprocal measures), resumption of family reunions, and economic cooperation (restarting the **Kaesong Industrial Complex** and **Mount Kumgang tourism**, contingent on sanctions relief).

Moon also brokered the **June 12, 2018 Singapore summit** between U.S. President Donald Trump and Kim Jong-un—the first-ever meeting between a sitting U.S. president and a North Korean leader. Moon positioned himself as a diplomatic facilitator. However, the February 2019 **Hanoi summit** between Trump and Kim collapsed without agreement over the scope of sanctions relief versus nuclear dismantlement, and North Korea subsequently withdrew from engagement. The Kaesong reopening and Mount Kumgang tourism never materialized; North Korean weapons testing resumed by 2019.

Moon's administration was also accused in post-presidency investigations of conducting a covert operation in 2018 to transfer approximately 8 billion Korean won (approximately $6.8 million) to North Korea through intermediaries ahead of the summits to facilitate Kim Jong-un's participation—a charge that became central to post-presidential legal proceedings.

### Domestic Policy

**Economic policy**: Moon prioritized "income-led growth" (소득주도성장), a framework built on raising minimum wages to stimulate consumer spending. The minimum wage rose from ₩6,470 (2017) to ₩8,350 (2019)—a cumulative increase of approximately 29% in two years—the steepest consecutive increase in Korean history. The policy generated intense controversy: while supporters argued it strengthened low-wage workers' purchasing power, critics (and empirical studies) found it accelerated job losses in small businesses and the service sector. The government moderated wage increases from 2019 onward. The income-led growth framework was partially successful in reducing the Gini coefficient slightly but failed to produce the employment gains promised; youth unemployment remained elevated throughout Moon's tenure.

**Chaebol reform**: Moon pledged structural reform of the chaebol system and pursued corruption cases against Samsung heir Lee Jae-yong (convicted and later paroled), but fundamental restructuring of the chaebol ownership model was not achieved.

**Real estate**: Moon's five years saw sharp real-estate inflation in the Seoul metropolitan area, despite more than 25 rounds of regulatory measures (including loan-to-value restrictions and capital gains taxes). The failure to stabilize Seoul housing prices became one of his presidency's most politically damaging failures, particularly affecting younger voters who aspired to home ownership.

**Gender and social policy**: Moon's administration secured the 2019 Constitutional Court ruling decriminalizing abortion after a legal challenge; prior law had criminalized abortion since 1953. Implementation through revised legislation was contested and incomplete during his term. The **#MeToo movement** in South Korea produced high-profile prosecutions during 2018, including of the Governor of South Chungcheong Province (An Hee-jeong), and Moon's administration broadly supported accountability, though critics noted gaps in institutional follow-through.

**Environmental policy**: Moon pledged carbon neutrality by 2050 and launched a **Korean New Deal** in July 2020 that included a "Green New Deal" component focusing on renewable energy, electric vehicles, and smart grid investment. His government announced plans to phase out coal-fired power plants and expand offshore wind energy off the Jeju and southern coasts.

### COVID-19 Pandemic Response

South Korea was one of the first countries outside China to face large-scale COVID-19 transmission, with a major cluster at the **Shincheonji Church** in Daegu in February 2020. Moon's administration responded with:
- **Mass testing**: Korea deployed drive-through PCR testing infrastructure rapidly, processing hundreds of thousands of tests per day at peak demand
- **Digital contact tracing**: The government used credit card data, CCTV, and phone location data to reconstruct infection chains—effective epidemiologically but controversial for privacy implications
- **Transparent communication**: Daily government press conferences and public dashboards tracking case counts became a model cited internationally

South Korea's early pandemic management was internationally praised as a "**K-quarantine**" (K-방역) model, and the case avoided the lockdowns seen in much of Europe and the United States through spring 2020. However, subsequent waves—particularly the late 2020 and 2021 Delta wave driven by clusters in prisons, religious gatherings, and nightlife venues—strained the health system and eroded public patience. The COVID-19 economic disruption weighed on household finances, particularly for small business owners, throughout Moon's final two years.

### PyeongChang Winter Olympics (February 2018)

The **2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics** (February 9–25, 2018) in Pyeongchang County, Gangwon Province, became a symbol of national achievement and diplomatic opening. South Korea won 5 gold, 8 silver, and 4 bronze medals—its best Winter Olympics performance. The joint Korean march and unified ice hockey team, coordinated by Moon and Kim Yo-jong's Pyongyang overture, produced the defining diplomatic image of his presidency. International Olympic Committee President Thomas Bach praised the "Olympic Truce." The Olympics attracted 92 delegations and approximately 2,952 athletes—the largest Winter Games participation to that point.

## Post-Presidency Challenges

Moon left office on May 10, 2022, with approval ratings in the low 40s—eroded by real-estate inflation, perception of economic policy failure, and regional political polarization. He retired to **Yangsan** in South Gyeongsang Province, near his hometown. His successor, **Yoon Suk-yeol** (a conservative former Prosecutor General), ran on a platform of stricter North Korea policy, free-market economics, and anti-corruption.

### Legal Proceedings

After leaving office, Moon faced a series of investigations and indictments primarily pursued under the Yoon administration's justice apparatus:

- **Covert North Korea transfer**: Accused of violating the Foreign Exchange Act by allegedly directing the transfer of funds to North Korea via intermediaries in 2018; indicted in 2024
- **Abuse of power**: Investigated for allegedly directing National Intelligence Service operations for political purposes and for abuse of presidential authority
- **Bribery allegations**: Related to the employment of Moon's former son-in-law by an airline whose founder was appointed to a public post during Moon's tenure, raising questions about whether Moon used presidential influence

Moon denied all charges and characterized them as political persecution—a claim given some credibility by the pattern of South Korean presidential legal jeopardy across party lines (both Lee Myung-bak and Park Geun-hye from the conservative side also faced prosecution after leaving office).

In December 2024, amid the political crisis triggered by **President Yoon Suk-yeol's own brief declaration of martial law** (December 3, 2024) and subsequent impeachment, calls for a "grand pardon" of politically prosecuted figures across party lines gained momentum as part of proposed national reconciliation measures. Moon's legal proceedings remained unresolved and politically contested.

## Legacy and Political Impact

Moon's presidency is evaluated through competing lenses:

**Progressive assessment**: Moon attempted to break cycles of presidential prosecution that criminalizes political opponents; revived peace diplomacy with North Korea, reducing military tensions and enabling cross-border family reunions; championed socio-economic reform and women's rights; and built South Korea's international profile as a model of democratic governance and pandemic response. He respected civil society, did not use the security apparatus against political opponents, and served a constitutionally mandated single term without incident.

**Conservative assessment**: Moon's Sunshine Policy failed to achieve denuclearization—North Korea continued developing nuclear capabilities throughout his engagement—and may have bolstered Kim Jong-un's negotiating position. His income-led growth policy produced economic disruption without the promised gains. His real-estate regulatory failures worsened housing affordability for a generation. His Honam-centric political appointments deepened regional divides in a system already fragmented by regionalism. His diplomacy raised security concerns in the U.S.–South Korea alliance relationship by prioritizing engagement over pressure.

Moon remains an influential reference point in South Korean progressive politics. His approach to North Korea—dialogue first, sanctions relief as incentive—remains the defining alternative to the conservative deterrence-first framework, and the debate between these approaches continues to define South Korean foreign-policy elections. The prosecutorial cycle he embodied, where each administration investigates its predecessor, highlighted systemic tensions in South Korea's democratic consolidation—a pattern the Korean Independence Movement's successors would likely have seen as a corruption of the democratic republic they built.
