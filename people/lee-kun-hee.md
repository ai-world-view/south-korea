# Lee Kun-hee

**Category**: People  
**Key figures**: Lee Byung-chul (father), Lee Jae-yong (son), Lee Boo-jin and Lee Seo-hyun (daughters)

## Summary

Lee Kun-hee (이건희, 1942–2020) was a South Korean business magnate who transformed Samsung, the conglomerate founded by his father Lee Byung-chul, from a primarily domestic Korean enterprise into one of the world's largest electronics manufacturers and the architect of South Korea's position as a global technology leader. As Samsung Group chairman from 1987 until his death in 2020, Lee oversaw the company's rise to dominance in semiconductors, displays, smartphones, and consumer electronics, making Samsung synonymous with Korean innovation and quality engineering worldwide. His strategic decisions—including the diversification away from textile and automobile focus into semiconductor and display technologies—fundamentally shaped South Korea's economic trajectory and established the template for the modern chaebol operating model.

Lee Kun-hee's tenure was marked by both transformative business accomplishments and significant controversies, including corruption charges, succession disputes within the family, and governance conflicts over management control and wealth transfer. His death in October 2020 triggered a transition of power to his son, Lee Jae-yong, and highlighted the ongoing tension between the dynastic chaebol system and modern corporate governance standards. Despite these controversies, Lee remains one of the most consequential figures in South Korean business history and a central architect of the nation's post-war economic miracle.

## Early Life and Education

Lee Kun-hee was born in 1942 as the third son of Lee Byung-chul (1910–1987), the founder of Samsung. Lee grew up in a family of considerable wealth and influence but not the eldest son destined for succession (his older brother Lee Maeng-hee was the designated heir). However, following family and business dynamics, Lee Kun-hee eventually became the favored successor.

Lee attended Waseda University in Tokyo, Japan, where he studied economics and developed an early exposure to Japanese industrial practices and management philosophy. This international education distinguished him from many Korean business leaders of his generation and exposed him to post-war Japanese economic growth models, which would later influence Samsung's strategic direction. He returned to Korea in the 1960s and initially worked in Samsung subsidiaries, learning the family business from the ground up.

## Rise to Samsung Leadership (1960s–1987)

Lee Kun-hee joined Samsung's management structure during the 1960s and 1970s, a period when Samsung was primarily engaged in textiles (the original Samsung), sugar manufacturing, insurance, and early automotive ventures. The company was successful but remained predominantly Korean-focused and dependent on traditional manufacturing.

Under Lee Byung-chul's founding vision, Samsung had diversified into various sectors, but the company's direction in the 1970s and early 1980s remained somewhat diffuse. Lee Kun-hee, with his international exposure and strategic vision, began advocating for a more focused, technology-oriented strategy. This vision aligned with South Korea's developmental-state policy under Park Chung-hee, which prioritized high-value-added manufacturing and export-driven growth.

In 1987, following Lee Byung-chul's death, Lee Kun-hee was formally named Samsung Group chairman. Unlike his father's founding vision, which had embraced diversification across multiple industries, Lee Kun-hee would prune Samsung's portfolio and refocus the conglomerate on core technology sectors, particularly semiconductors and consumer electronics.

## Strategic Transformation (1987–2000s)

### The Semiconductor Pivot

Lee Kun-hee's most consequential strategic decision was the massive investment in semiconductor manufacturing, particularly DRAM (dynamic random-access memory). In the mid-1980s, South Korea lagged behind Japan (Mitsubishi, Toshiba, NEC) and the United States (Intel, Micron) in memory-chip production. Lee committed Samsung to close this gap through sustained capital investment, technology acquisition (through partnerships and reverse-engineering), and aggressive hiring of talent.

Key milestones:

- **1983**: Samsung entered the DRAM market, initially as a follower of Japanese and U.S. designs.
- **1987**: Lee's appointment coincided with Samsung's decision to pursue its own DRAM process technology.
- **1992**: Samsung became the world's largest DRAM manufacturer (by volume), surpassing Japanese rivals. This achievement required billions of dollars in investment and took nearly a decade to achieve.
- **Late 1990s–2000s**: Samsung expanded into NAND flash memory and began developing next-generation chip architectures, consolidating its position as a memory-chip leader.

This pivot proved transformative for Samsung and South Korea. The semiconductor industry became one of the nation's most valuable export sectors, and Samsung's success inspired imitators and followers (SK Hynix, Hyundai Electronics). Lee's willingness to invest heavily in a sector where Samsung initially lagged, and to sustain losses for years before profitability, became a defining characteristic of his leadership philosophy.

### Display Technology (LCD and OLED)

Following the semiconductor success, Lee directed Samsung into liquid-crystal display (LCD) manufacturing in the 1990s. At the time, Japan (Sharp, Toshiba) dominated LCD production for televisions and monitors. Again, Lee committed massive capital and R&D resources to developing Samsung's LCD capabilities.

By the 2000s, Samsung had become a world leader in LCD production, supplying panels to television manufacturers worldwide and establishing Samsung Electronics as a major supplier of critical components to the broader electronics industry. Later, Lee oversaw Samsung's early investments in OLED (organic light-emitting diode) display technology, positioning the company to lead the premium smartphone and television display market in the 2010s.

### Consumer Electronics and Brand Building

In parallel with component manufacturing, Lee pursued a strategy of vertical integration and brand leadership in consumer products. Samsung developed its own line of televisions, refrigerators, washing machines, air conditioners, and other home appliances, using its component advantages (chips, displays) to deliver superior products at competitive prices.

By the 1990s, Samsung Electronics had become one of the world's top consumer electronics brands, competing directly with Sony, Philips, and other established manufacturers. Lee invested heavily in marketing, design, and customer service, transforming Samsung from a "cheap Korean copy" brand to a symbol of quality and innovation.

## The Smartphone Era and Global Dominance (2000s–2010s)

With the rise of smartphones, Samsung faced a critical choice: Samsung Electronics was not a major player in the mobile phone market (that space was dominated by Nokia, Motorola, and later Apple with the iPhone). However, Lee's earlier investments in semiconductors and displays proved crucial.

Samsung supplied memory chips and displays to Apple (and other smartphone manufacturers), generating enormous profit margins. Simultaneously, Samsung developed its own smartphones using Android, launching the Galaxy line, which became the world's best-selling smartphone series. By the early 2010s, Samsung was the world's largest smartphone manufacturer (by volume) and a major component supplier to competitors.

This dual-strategy approach—being both a supplier to competitors and a competitor itself—became emblematic of the Samsung model Lee established. The company's scale and integration allowed it to profit across multiple layers of the electronics value chain.

## Expansion and Diversification

Under Lee's tenure, Samsung also expanded into:

- **Telecommunications infrastructure** — Network equipment, 5G infrastructure
- **Batteries and energy storage** — Including Samsung SDI and Samsung SDS (data center and logistics services)
- **Biotechnology** — Samsung Biologics became a major contract manufacturing organization (CMO) for pharmaceutical companies
- **Petrochemicals** — Samsung Fine Chemicals

This expansion maintained Samsung's status as a major conglomerate while focusing core resources on high-margin technology sectors.

## Controversies and Governance Issues

### Corruption Scandal (1996–1997)

In the mid-1990s, Lee Kun-hee faced corruption allegations related to secret off-the-books funds (slush funds) used for tax evasion and political contributions. These practices were widespread among Korean chaebols but became a focal point of prosecution after South Korea's democratization and the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis.

In 1996, Lee was convicted of tax evasion and bribery and sentenced to a suspended prison term. However, in 1997, following the IMF crisis and amid national emergency, he was pardoned by the government as part of a broader amnesty for businessmen whose return to active management was deemed necessary for economic recovery. This pardon reflected the state-chaebol relationship and the state's dependence on these companies for employment and export revenue, but it also highlighted the lack of equal justice and accountability that plagued the chaebol system.

### Succession and Family Disputes

Lee Kun-hee had several children, including:

- **Lee Jae-yong** (이재용, born 1968) — Son, groomed as the heir apparent to Samsung Electronics
- **Lee Boo-jin** (이부진, born 1970) — Daughter, president and CEO of Hotel Shilla and previously a senior executive at the Everland resort operation
- **Lee Seo-hyun** (이서현, born 1973) — Daughter, later chair of the Samsung Welfare Foundation and the Samsung Foundation of Culture

(Lee Myung-hee, chairwoman of the Shinsegae retail group, is Lee Kun-hee's younger sister, not his daughter.)

The succession planning was not straightforward. Lee Jae-yong faced his own legal troubles in the 2010s and 2020s, including investigations into accounting fraud and embezzlement allegations. These disputes raised questions about governance, corporate transparency, and the concentration of power in family hands within the chaebol system.

### Second Resignation and Return (2008–2010)

In April 2008, Lee Kun-hee resigned as Samsung Group chairman for the second time, following special-prosecutor investigations into secret slush funds allegedly used to bribe prosecutors, judges, and government officials over many years. The funds were estimated to total approximately 100 billion Korean won. Lee was indicted on charges of tax evasion and breach of trust. In July 2008 he was convicted and given a three-year suspended sentence. President Lee Myung-bak (no relation) pardoned him in December 2009, again citing national economic interests — a pardon that drew criticism from civic groups for perpetuating impunity for chaebol leaders. Lee Kun-hee returned to the Samsung chairmanship in March 2010.

### The Park Geun-hye Scandal (2016–2017)

During the impeachment crisis of President Park Geun-hye, investigations revealed that Samsung had donated approximately 43.3 billion Korean won (~US$37 million) to foundations controlled by Park's associate Choi Soon-sil in exchange for government support of a Samsung corporate merger. Lee Jae-yong (Lee Kun-hee's son and Samsung heir apparent, who had taken on operational control following Lee Kun-hee's 2014 stroke) was arrested in February 2017 and convicted of bribery. He received a five-year prison sentence in August 2017, later suspended on appeal. The case intensified public debate about the chaebol-government relationship and Lee Kun-hee's broader legacy.

### Tax and Wealth Transfer Disputes

Lee Kun-hee accumulated vast personal wealth, making him one of South Korea's richest individuals. Questions about tax avoidance, transfer of wealth to family members, and the blurred line between personal and corporate assets (common in the chaebol system) became subject to investigation and public scrutiny, particularly after his death. At the time of his death in 2020, his estate was valued at approximately 26 trillion Korean won (roughly US$22 billion), making it the largest inheritance case in South Korean history. His family faced an inheritance tax bill estimated at approximately 12 trillion Korean won (~US$10 billion), to be paid in installments over five years — the largest inheritance tax in South Korean corporate history and a significant test of whether chaebol family succession could be managed legally and transparently.

## Management Philosophy and Legacy

Lee Kun-hee pioneered a management philosophy that emphasized:

1. **"New Management" and the Frankfurt Declaration (1993)** — In February 1993, Lee gathered Samsung executives in Frankfurt, Germany, for an intensive management retreat. After watching a video showing Samsung products being passed over by consumers in favor of Japanese goods in a US electronics store, Lee delivered a three-day, impromptu speech that became known as the "Frankfurt Declaration." Its most quoted phrase—"Change everything except your wife and children"—encapsulated his demand for radical quality improvement over incremental adjustment. The full speech totaled approximately 1.8 million words in transcript form, and its text was printed and distributed across Samsung affiliates. The Frankfurt Declaration transformed Samsung's internal culture from a volume-driven manufacturing mentality to a design-and-quality-first philosophy.

2. **The 1995 Phone Burning** — In March 1995, Lee received a complaint that a batch of Samsung mobile phones given as gifts to dignitaries were defective. In response, he ordered roughly 150,000 handsets (and other products), valued at approximately 50 billion Korean won (~US$50 million at the time), collected in the courtyard of the Gumi factory. Workers wore headbands reading "Quality First"; the phones were then publicly destroyed by hammer, then incinerated. The event was photographed and widely circulated internally. Former Samsung employees describe it as a defining cultural moment that made product quality a matter of personal and corporate honor.

3. **"Segyehwa" (Globalization)** — Launched 1993–1995, this initiative positioned Samsung as a global corporation rather than merely an export-driven Korean manufacturer. It involved restructuring overseas subsidiaries, hiring international designers and engineers, and mandating that executives spend extended periods abroad understanding foreign markets. By the mid-2000s, Samsung had design centers in London, Milan, Tokyo, Los Angeles, and Shanghai.

4. **"Only the Paranoid Survive"** — Lee adopted Intel co-founder Andy Grove's philosophy, maintaining a deliberate sense of urgency about technological disruption and competitive threats. He instituted practices such as periodic "zero-base" strategic reviews in which entire business units had to justify their existence from scratch.

5. **Vertical Integration and Scale** — Building Samsung's capabilities across the entire value chain (chips, displays, batteries, manufacturing equipment, distribution) to maintain control and profitability. This model allowed Samsung to cross-subsidize nascent business lines—for example, using semiconductor profits to fund display capacity expansion—in ways that purely public-market-financed competitors could not match.

6. **Capital Intensity** — Willingness to deploy enormous capital for long-term projects, accepting years of losses before profitability, as exemplified by the semiconductor and display strategies. Between 1983 and 1992, Samsung spent an estimated US$2 billion on semiconductor investment before reaching global leadership in DRAM.

## Death and Succession (October 2020)

In May 2014, Lee suffered a heart attack and underwent emergency surgery at Seoul National University Hospital. He remained hospitalized and in largely vegetative condition for the remaining six years of his life. During this period, Lee Jae-yong effectively assumed operational control of Samsung Electronics, though his authority was complicated by his own legal troubles.

Lee Kun-hee died on October 25, 2020, at age 78. His death triggered South Korea's largest-ever inheritance-tax proceedings: his estate included a controlling stake in Samsung Life Insurance, shares in Samsung Electronics and other affiliates, and an extensive personal collection of approximately 23,000 artworks — valued at around 3 trillion Korean won — that his family donated in 2021 to national museums including the National Museum of Korea and the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art. The collection included works by Monet, Picasso, Dalí, and major Korean artists including Park Soo-keun, Lee Jung-seob, and Kim Whan-ki, and was the largest single art donation in South Korean history.

Lee Jae-yong formally assumed leadership of Samsung Electronics, serving as vice-chairman and later chairman (formally appointed October 2022), though his tenure continued to be shadowed by legal disputes.

## Personal Interests and Cultural Patronage

Lee Kun-hee was a dedicated art collector from the 1970s onward, acquiring both Western modernist works and Korean art from the Joseon and modern periods. He was also a lifelong motorsport and horse-racing enthusiast, maintaining thoroughbred stables under the Samsung Everland brand. He competed in international car rallies in the 1970s and 1980s, and Samsung later sponsored Formula 1 and motorsport events in Korea. Lee developed a reputation as a cinephile; his late-night viewing habit was said to be a source of multiple Samsung product ideas, including high-definition television technology. His 1993 mandate that Samsung designers should study films, art, and product design in Italy and France as part of the Segyehwa program stemmed from his own aesthetic sensibility.

## Economic and Historical Impact

Lee Kun-hee's 33-year tenure (1987–2020) as Samsung chairman transformed the company from a large Korean conglomerate into one of the world's most valuable corporations and a symbol of South Korean technological excellence. His strategic choices—particularly the semiconductor and display gambles—directly shaped South Korea's position as a global technology leader. Samsung's success and profitability funded much of South Korea's R&D ecosystem, influenced government technology policy, and created a template for the modern developmental-state corporation.

However, Lee's legacy is also tied to the continuation and adaptation of the chaebol system in modern South Korea, with its associated governance concerns, wealth concentration, and tensions between family control and professional management. The scandals surrounding corruption, tax evasion, and succession disputes underscore ongoing debates about corporate accountability and the future of the chaebol model in a democratized South Korea.
