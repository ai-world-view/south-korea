# Syngman Rhee

**Category**: People
**Dates**: March 26, 1875 – July 19, 1965
**Key figures**: John R. Hodge, Kim Gu (Kim Koo), Philip Jaisohn (Seo Jae-pil), Douglas MacArthur, Dwight D. Eisenhower, Yi Ki-bung

## Summary

Syngman Rhee (이승만, *Yi Seung-man*; March 26, 1875 – July 19, 1965) was the founding and longest-serving president of the Republic of Korea, holding office from its establishment on August 15, 1948 until his forced resignation on April 26, 1960. His twelve-year presidency shaped both South Korea's institutional foundations and its authoritarian political culture; he is simultaneously credited with securing Korean statehood during an extraordinarily dangerous Cold War moment and condemned for the repression and electoral fraud that ultimately drove him from power.

### Early Life and Education (1875–1919)

Rhee was born in the village of Hagchon, Pyongsan County, Hwanghae Province (now in North Korea), to a yangban (scholar-official) family claiming descent from the royal Yi dynasty. His early education was in the Chinese classical curriculum. After contact with American Methodist missionaries in Seoul, he converted to Christianity and enrolled in the Paejae School (배재학당), a Methodist institution founded by Henry Appenzeller, where he received a modern Western education.

In 1896–1898 Rhee became politically active, participating in the Independence Club (*Dongnip Hyeophoe*) led by Philip Jaisohn (Seo Jae-pil), which advocated for Korean sovereignty and constitutional reform during the waning years of the Joseon dynasty. He was arrested in 1898 for his role in anti-government agitation and imprisoned for five and a half years, during which he converted more fully to Christianity and reportedly preached to fellow prisoners. Released in 1904, he was sent to the United States as a delegate to petition President Theodore Roosevelt for support for Korean independence in the context of the Russo-Japanese War. Roosevelt declined to intervene.

Rhee remained in the United States for graduate education, earning a BA from George Washington University (1907), an MA from Harvard University (1908), and a PhD in International Law from Princeton University (1910)—the same year Japan formally annexed Korea, extinguishing the Korean state Rhee sought to represent. He became the first Korean to earn a doctorate from an American university.

### Independence Movement in Exile (1919–1945)

After Japan's annexation, Rhee based himself largely in Hawaii, leading the Korean nationalist community in the United States. Following the March 1st Movement of 1919—a mass pro-independence uprising across Korea brutally suppressed by Japanese colonial authorities—Korean exile communities established the **Korean Provisional Government (KPG)** in Shanghai. Rhee was elected the KPG's president in 1919, but his insistence on lobbying great powers (especially the United States) rather than organizing armed resistance brought him into conflict with more militant figures, particularly **Kim Gu** (Kim Koo), who led resistance operations from China.

Rhee was effectively removed from the KPG presidency in 1925 due to internal disputes and accusations of financial mismanagement and unilateralism, but he retained the loyalty of the Korean-American community and continued to present himself internationally as the voice of Korean independence. During World War II, he lobbied American officials and the U.S. State Department to recognize the KPG and support Korean independence, with limited success. The United States largely treated the KPG as one exile faction among several rather than a legitimate government-in-waiting.

### Return and Rise to Power (1945–1948)

Japan's surrender on August 15, 1945 ended thirty-five years of colonial rule but immediately replaced one set of problems with another: Korea was divided at the 38th parallel, with Soviet forces occupying the north and American forces occupying the south under Lieutenant General **John R. Hodge**'s U.S. Army Military Government in Korea (USAMGIK). Rhee returned to Korea in October 1945, flown in on General Douglas MacArthur's personal aircraft—a signal of American sponsorship. In the ideologically polarized occupation, Rhee positioned himself as the supreme anti-communist conservative alternative to both leftists aligned with the Soviet-backed north and more moderate nationalists like Kim Gu, who hoped for a unified independent Korea.

Rhee's relationship with the American occupation was complex: he was useful to the Americans as a reliable anti-communist figurehead, but his demands for immediate independence and his inflammatory rhetoric sometimes alarmed more cautious U.S. officials. His speech in June 1946 effectively calling for partition—if the north would not unify on terms compatible with a democratic south, the south should go it alone—was a decisive moment that accelerated the trajectory toward separate states.

When the UN-supervised elections of May 10, 1948 were held in the south (the north refused participation), Rhee's political organization swept the seats, and the new National Assembly elected him president. The **Republic of Korea** was formally proclaimed on **August 15, 1948**—the third anniversary of liberation from Japan. The competing **Democratic People's Republic of Korea** was proclaimed in the north on **September 9, 1948**, completing the division.

### Presidency: State-Building and Authoritarianism (1948–1953)

Rhee's first years in office combined genuine state-building with systematic political repression. The **National Security Law** (*Gukga Boan Beop*), passed in December 1948, criminalized support for communism or any organization deemed to threaten the state, and was used broadly to silence political opposition. Rhee's government conducted purges of suspected leftists and communists within the military and police, sometimes through summary executions. The **Jeju April 3rd Incident** (1948–1949), a left-wing uprising and its suppression, cost an estimated 14,000–30,000 lives. The **Bodo League massacre** of 1950—conducted at the outbreak of the Korean War, when security forces killed tens of thousands of suspected leftist sympathizers—occurred under Rhee's authority and was suppressed for decades.

The **Korean War** (June 25, 1950 – July 27, 1953) fundamentally reshaped Rhee's presidency. North Korea's invasion on June 25, 1950 initially drove the Republic of Korea and allied UN forces to the Pusan Perimeter before the Inchon landing (September 15, 1950) reversed the tide. The war strengthened Rhee's anti-communist argument and tightened his hold on political power; wartime conditions justified repressive measures. However, Rhee's relationship with UN Commander General Douglas MacArthur and, after MacArthur's dismissal, with subsequent UN command was tense: Rhee wanted the war fought to total victory and the unification of Korea, while the U.S. and UN moved toward an armistice. Rhee sabotaged armistice negotiations in June 1953 by unilaterally releasing 27,000 North Korean prisoners of war who had refused repatriation, hoping to derail the agreement—an act that infuriated the Eisenhower administration but ultimately failed to prevent the armistice signed on July 27, 1953.

### Constitutional Manipulation and Electoral Fraud (1952–1960)

By 1952, Rhee faced the prospect of losing an indirect presidential election through the National Assembly, whose members had grown critical of his governance. He responded by declaring martial law in May 1952 in Busan (the wartime capital) and forcing through a constitutional amendment introducing **direct popular election of the president**—calculating that he could win a mass vote more easily than an Assembly vote. The "Pusan Political Crisis" of 1952 established the template for the rest of his rule: when institutional processes threatened his power, he bypassed or coerced them.

The **1954 constitutional revision** (the "rounding error" amendment) illegally passed a term-limit exception for the incumbent president, allowing Rhee to run for a third term. In 1956 he won re-election but his vice-presidential candidate lost—a warning sign of declining popular support. By 1960, the regime was desperate. The presidential election of **March 15, 1960** was blatantly rigged: ballot boxes were stuffed, independent poll watchers were barred, and the results were fabricated. Rhee won with an improbable 88.7% of the vote; his vice-presidential candidate Yi Ki-bung was declared the winner as well.

### April Revolution and Exile (1960)

Popular outrage at the March 15 election fraud ignited protests beginning in Masan (now Changwon) on the day of the election itself, when police fired on demonstrators and killed at least nine people. On April 11, 1960, the body of Kim Ju-yul—a high school student who had been shot in the face with a tear gas canister—was recovered from Masan harbor, provoking a nationwide explosion of protest. On **April 19, 1960** (*Sa-il-gu*, the "4-19 Revolution"), massive student demonstrations converged on the presidential residence (Gyeongmudae) in Seoul; police fired on crowds, killing approximately 185–186 people and wounding thousands. But the scale of the uprising—involving not only students but professors (who marched in protest on April 25) and citizens across the country—exceeded the government's capacity to suppress it.

On **April 25**, Korean Army Chief of Staff General Song Yo-chan refused to order his troops to fire on protesters. General Magruder, the U.S. Military Commander in Korea, urged Rhee to restore order by legitimate means. The U.S. Ambassador Walter P. McConaughy warned Rhee that continued repression would not have American support. Isolated, Rhee announced his resignation on **April 26, 1960**. He went into exile in Hawaii on May 29, 1960, where he died on July 19, 1965, and was buried at the National Cemetery in Seoul in 1965.

## Significance

Syngman Rhee's legacy is contested in ways that resist simple resolution. He presided over the founding of a state that survived a devastating war, built functioning (if repressive) institutions, forged the U.S.-Korea alliance, and secured international recognition during an existential moment. His anti-communism, though used cynically to suppress legitimate dissent, also reflected genuine conviction formed over decades of watching the Soviet Union absorb neighboring states. Korean economic development and eventual democratization occurred within the institutional frame he created—including the 1954 Mutual Defense Treaty with the United States that underwrites South Korean security to the present day.

At the same time, Rhee's systematic repression—of leftists, of political rivals, of the press—established a culture of presidential abuse that his successors, especially Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan, would inherit and amplify. The massacres of suspected leftists under his authority (Jeju, the Bodo League killings) resulted in tens of thousands of deaths and were suppressed in national memory for decades. His willingness to rewrite constitutions, rig elections, and deploy security forces against citizens normalized behaviors that would recur across subsequent administrations.

His overthrow in 1960—the first popular mass uprising to oust a South Korean leader—established a precedent for democratic resistance that would resurface in 1979–1980 (the [Gwangju Uprising](../history/gwangju-uprising.md)), in the June 1987 struggle, and in the 2016–2017 candlelight protests that removed President Park Geun-hye. The April Revolution is officially commemorated in South Korea as **April 19 Memorial Day**, recognizing the sacrifice of the students and citizens who forced the country's first democratic transition.
