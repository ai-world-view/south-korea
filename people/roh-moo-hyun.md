# Roh Moo-hyun — President of South Korea and Human-Rights Advocate

**Roh Moo-hyun** (노무현; September 1, 1946 – May 23, 2009) was the 16th president of South Korea, serving from February 25, 2003 to February 25, 2008. He was the first president to be born after Korea's liberation from Japanese rule — born during the chaotic transitional year in which the American military government was consolidating its occupation and Korean political factions were battling for the peninsula's future (see [Korean Liberation and Founding, 1945–1948](../history/liberation-and-founding.md)). A self-taught human-rights lawyer who rose from a modest provincial background to the nation's highest office, he embodied the aspirations and contradictions of South Korea's democratization. His presidency, bookended by idealism and legal scandal, left a contested legacy: celebrated by progressives for his inter-Korean diplomacy and egalitarian rhetoric, and criticized by conservatives for what they saw as naïve engagement with the North and economic mismanagement. His post-presidential legal jeopardy and tragic death profoundly shaped South Korean politics and public memory of democratic leadership.

## Early Life and Legal Career (1946–1992)

Roh Moo-hyun was born on September 1, 1946, in Kimhae (김해), South Gyeongsang Province, into a family of modest means. His father was a farmer turned merchant; his childhood coincided with the Korean War, which displaced his family temporarily and left the household poor. Roh attended **Busan Commercial High School** (부산상업고등학교), one of the prominent vocational secondary schools in the Gyeongsang region, graduating in 1966. He did not attend university; instead, he began working as a customs officer and tax official while self-studying law — one of the most demanding professional paths in a country where legal education was normally gated by Seoul National University.

In 1975, Roh passed the **national judicial examination** (사법시험) — Korea's grueling bar exam, then requiring years of intense preparation — becoming a licensed attorney without ever having enrolled in a university law program. His self-taught status was central to his biography and political identity: he was, in his own telling and in public perception, an ordinary person who had achieved professional credentials through determination rather than elite credentials. This stood in pointed contrast to the yangban-descended and Seoul National University-trained elite that dominated the Korean legal and political establishment.

He established a law practice in Busan, representing dissidents, workers, and the accused poor. In the 1970s and 1980s, while Korea was under Park Chung-hee's and then Chun Doo-hwan's authoritarian regimes, Roh became deeply involved in human-rights activism: defending political prisoners, student activists, and labor organizers arrested under emergency decrees and the National Security Law. He co-founded the **Busan Democratic Lawyers' Group** (부산민주변론인단) and participated in the defense of activists arrested in the aftermath of the **Busan Democratization Movement** (부산민주화운동) of 1979. This activity placed Roh in personal legal jeopardy — he was interrogated, investigated, and professionally threatened by security services.

His partnership with other progressive lawyers, including the human-rights advocate **Moon Jae-in** (문재인; who would later serve as his Chief of Staff and eventually become president, 2017–2022), forged networks that would shape his political trajectory. See [Moon Jae-in](moon-jae-in.md) for the connection. Roh's years in Busan also formed his view that South Korean democracy was fundamentally incomplete — that formal electoral institutions could not guarantee justice so long as the police, prosecution, and judiciary remained instruments of the state against civil society.

## Rise Through Regional and National Politics (1988–2001)

Roh's entry into electoral politics came with South Korea's democratic transition. In the April 1988 general election he won a seat in the **National Assembly** (국회, Gukhoe) representing a district in Busan, and later that year he gained national fame during the televised hearings into the abuses of Chun Doo-hwan's Fifth Republic, where his sharp, principled questioning of business and political figures made him a recognizable reformist voice. Through the 1990s he repeatedly ran for office in the conservative-leaning Busan–Gyeongsang region — deliberately challenging Korea's entrenched regionalism rather than seeking a safer seat — and lost a string of races for the National Assembly and the Busan mayoralty. These defeats earned him the affectionate nickname "Roh Moo-hyun the Fool" (바보 노무현) among admirers and cemented his image as a politician of conviction; he returned to the National Assembly through a 1998 by-election.

His reputation grew as an advocate for the dispossessed and a voice against military-era legacies and corporate corruption. Unlike many opposition politicians who used nationalist rhetoric or chauvinism to build support, Roh cultivated a persona of moral conviction and personal humility — he was known for his simple lifestyle, his refusal of luxury, and his direct communication style.

## The Road to the Presidency (1997–2002)

Roh's path to the presidency was unexpected. In the 1997 presidential election, he initially supported another progressive candidate. However, after the [**Asian Financial Crisis of 1997**](../economy/banking-and-finance.md), which devastated South Korea's economy and necessitated a massive International Monetary Fund (IMF) bailout, political coalitions shifted. The election was won by [**Kim Dae-jung**](kim-dae-jung.md), the first president from the opposition-party tradition (the National Congress for New Politics). Roh served in Kim Dae-jung's cabinet as Minister of Maritime Affairs and Fisheries (해양수산부 장관) from 2000 to 2001, gaining administrative experience and a national profile.

For the 2002 presidential election, Roh entered the Millennium Democratic Party's open primaries as an underdog and, powered by an enthusiastic grassroots following, defeated the early front-runner Rhee In-je to capture the nomination — a genuine upset. He positioned himself as the voice of reform, generational change, and the interests of ordinary Koreans (he termed this "participatory government" and "the age of the common man"). Against expectations, he won the December 2002 election with **48.9%** of the vote, defeating the conservative rival Lee Hoe-chang (**46.6%**).

The 2002 campaign was notable for **Roh's strong showing among younger voters**, particularly the 20–30 demographic organizing via the newly popular internet and digital media. His online fan club, **Nosamo** (노사모, "People Who Love Roh Moo-hyun"), was among the first mass-scale political internet communities in Korean history, mobilizing hundreds of thousands of small donors and volunteer organizers through early social media and bulletin-board platforms. This represented a generational shift: Roh's victory signaled the political rise of voters born after the Korean War who had no memory of authoritarian rule and prioritized economic egalitarianism and diplomatic opening over nationalist grievance. The race's final hours were dramatic: the independent candidate **Chung Mong-joon**, who had agreed to merge his campaign with Roh's, abruptly withdrew his endorsement the night before the vote — a move widely credited with galvanizing Roh's supporters to turn out. Younger voters coordinated a get-out-the-vote push through mobile text messages and online networks, and Roh won on December 19, 2002, in one of the closest and most closely watched elections of the democratic era.

## Presidency (2003–2008)

### Policy Platform and Early Moves

Roh campaigned on a platform of **anti-corruption**, **economic egalitarianism**, **administrative decentralization**, and **inter-Korean rapprochement**. His inaugural address on February 25, 2003, emphasized "participatory democracy" and moral governance, positioning himself as a reformer.

Key early policy initiatives included:
- **Administrative capital relocation** — Roh proposed moving the national capital from Seoul to a new city, Sejong (세종), in central Korea, ostensibly to reduce overcentralization and regional disparities. The project was highly controversial, politically divisive (conservatives opposed the enormous cost), and ultimately delayed and modified; Sejong eventually emerged as a planned city with government ministries beginning relocation in the 2010s.
- **Anti-corruption investigations** — Roh's government pursued high-profile corruption and illegal-campaign-financing probes, most prominently the 2003–2004 investigation into illicit corporate donations to the major parties' 2002 presidential campaigns, which implicated fundraisers in both Roh's own camp and the conservative opposition. Roh framed the effort as an attempt to break an entrenched cycle of money politics.
- **Chaebol reform** — Roh attempted to rein in the power of large conglomerates through tax policy and regulatory measures, pushing back against the concentrated economic power of Samsung, Hyundai, and LG. This proved economically counterproductive during a period of global competition.

### Economic Policy and the "Participatory Government"

Roh's economic vision was egalitarian but ultimately ineffectual. He pursued policies aimed at reducing inequality and spreading wealth downward:
- **Real estate policy** — an attempt to cap property prices and redistribute housing opportunities, which proved unpopular and ineffective, as property prices surged during his presidency
- **Labor-friendly policies** — greater tolerance for union organizing and strikes, and attempts to strengthen workers' rights, which generated business opposition and a wave of labor unrest
- **Welfare expansion** — increased social spending and health-insurance coverage, positioning the government as a guarantor of social security

However, the global economic downturn (2004–2008), rising oil prices, and aggressive competition from China undercut Roh's economic agenda. Economic growth slowed, unemployment rose in certain sectors, and the business community grew restive under what they perceived as hostile policies. By the end of his presidency, Roh's economic record was viewed as a mixed record at best.

### Inter-Korean Diplomacy and the "Sunshine Policy"

Roh inherited [Kim Dae-jung's **Sunshine Policy**](../government-politics/north-korea-relations.md) of gradual engagement with North Korea. Roh continued this approach, sustaining economic cooperation (notably the Kaesong Industrial Complex) and humanitarian exchange, but his presidency coincided with North Korea's accelerating nuclear-weapons program (the 2005 nuclear declaration, the October 2006 nuclear test) and rising tension with the George W. Bush administration, whose "Axis of Evil" framing Roh publicly resisted.

Late in his term, Roh held the second-ever inter-Korean summit, traveling overland across the Demilitarized Zone to Pyongyang on **October 2–4, 2007**, where he and Kim Jong-il issued the **October 4 Declaration** on peace and economic cooperation. Coming in his final months in office, however, it produced no lasting breakthrough comparable to Kim Dae-jung's 2000 summit, and most of its initiatives were shelved by the conservative government that succeeded him.

### Constitutional Impeachment (2004)

In March 2004, the opposition-controlled **National Assembly** impeached Roh on two counts: (1) **illegal election campaigning** — accusation that his 2002 campaign violated election laws, and (2) **breach of political neutrality** — claims that he had endorsed a political party in advance of legislative elections. Roh was temporarily stripped of his presidential powers while the **Constitutional Court** reviewed the impeachment.

The impeachment was widely understood as conservative retaliation for Roh's reform agenda, and it provoked a public backlash that helped his allies win the April 2004 legislative elections. The Constitutional Court **rejected the impeachment** in May 2004, restoring Roh's powers. The episode nonetheless intensified partisan polarization for the remainder of his term.

### The Korea–United States Free Trade Agreement (KORUS FTA)

One of the most consequential and politically contradictory achievements of Roh's presidency was the negotiation of the **Korea–United States Free Trade Agreement** (한미 자유무역협정, KORUS FTA), signed on June 30, 2007. The agreement — the largest bilateral free trade pact the United States had concluded since NAFTA — committed South Korea to opening its agricultural, manufacturing, and service sectors to American competition in exchange for enhanced access for Korean exports (especially automobiles and electronics) to the American market.

The KORUS FTA was intensely controversial in Korea. Roh's progressive base strongly opposed it: labor unions, farmers, and leftist parties feared that cheap American agricultural imports (especially beef, rice, and pork) would devastate Korean farming communities, and that financial liberalization would expose Korean workers to precarious employment. Mass street protests occurred in 2006–2007. The agreement was not ratified by either the Korean National Assembly or the U.S. Congress until 2011–2012, under the subsequent conservative governments of Lee Myung-bak (Korea) and Barack Obama (United States), who added automotive provisions before final passage.

Roh's willingness to sign a major free trade agreement with the United States despite fierce opposition from his own base illustrated the contradictions of his "participatory democracy" rhetoric — and his ultimate belief that Korean economic competitiveness required integration with the global trading system.

### Military and Security

Roh pursued a policy of enhanced Korean military self-reliance and negotiated a transition of **wartime operational control** (전시작전통제권, OPCON) of South Korean forces from the U.S. Combined Forces Command to a South Korean general, scheduled for 2012 (subsequently deferred multiple times by later administrations). He supported indigenous weapons development and technology transfer, aiming to reduce South Korea's military dependency on the United States. However, these moves proved controversial domestically and internationally; full operational control transfer has remained a subject of negotiation into the 2020s.

The **Yeonpyeong incident** (November 2010, which occurred after Roh's presidency) and other North Korean provocations in the 2000s constrained Roh's engagement policy.

### Later Years

By 2005–2008, Roh's presidency had lost momentum. The opposition controlled the National Assembly; the media (largely conservative-leaning) portrayed him as idealistic but ineffectual; and his support collapsed. By his final year, his approval rating had fallen to the low teens. His administration was seen as having failed to deliver on its economic egalitarianism promises while pursuing unpopular initiatives like the capital relocation.

## Post-Presidential Crisis and Death (2008–2009)

Roh left office on February 25, 2008, turning power over to [**Lee Myung-bak**](lee-myung-bak.md), a conservative who had pledged a tougher stance on North Korea and economic nationalism (his "MB Doctrine"). Lee's incoming administration moved quickly to reverse Roh's policies and investigate alleged corruption from Roh's tenure.

### Legal Investigations and Trial

Within weeks of leaving office, Roh and his family members were investigated for alleged corruption and bribery, charges that his supporters saw as politically motivated retaliation. Prosecutors alleged that Roh and his aides had accepted bribes from business figures during his presidency. In 2009, Roh was indicted on multiple charges including **bribery, tax evasion, and abuse of power**. The trial proceeded as Roh defended himself against the allegations.

The legal onslaught was unprecedented for a recent former president in Korea's democratic era. Unlike [Kim Dae-jung](kim-dae-jung.md), who was also prosecuted post-presidency but benefited from eventual presidential pardons, Roh faced a hostile conservative administration with no intention of clemency.

### Suicide (May 23, 2009)

On **May 23, 2009**, at the age of 62, Roh died by **suicide**, jumping from a cliff near his home in Gimhae. He left a note indicating despair about the legal proceedings and the humiliation of his family. His death shocked the nation and provoked an outpouring of grief among his supporters, who saw him as a martyr to partisan injustice. Conservative observers offered more mixed responses, some expressing sympathy for his personal tragedy while others defended the legal process.

Roh's suicide became a cultural moment in South Korea, prompting national debate about the treatment of former leaders, the politicization of the judiciary, and the psychological toll of political defeat.

## Legacy

Roh Moo-hyun's presidency and life left a complex legacy:

### Political Impact

- **Generational politics** — His election symbolized the rise of the post-war generation and the eclipse of the war-generation politicians. Subsequent elections saw youth voters becoming a politically pivotal demographic.
- **Progressive coalition fracturing** — His presidency highlighted tensions within the left between egalitarian, labor-friendly progressives and pragmatic, technocratic moderate reformers, a divide that persists in Korean politics.
- **Inter-Korean engagement** — His administration maintained (if cautiously) the engagement framework inherited from Kim Dae-jung, keeping inter-Korean dialogue alive despite heightening North Korean provocation.

### Personal and Cultural Impact

- **Symbol of integrity and tragedy** — Despite his presidency's mixed record, Roh became, in progressive memory, a symbol of moral conviction betrayed by partisan justice. He remains a heroic figure for left-wing activists.
- **Cautionary example** — His post-presidential persecution also served as a cautionary tale about the instability of democratic institutions in Korea, illustrating the danger of victor's justice and the need for constitutional constraints on executive power.

### Intellectual Legacy

- Roh's emphasis on "participatory democracy" and decentralization influenced subsequent Korean political discourse, particularly among progressives. The administrative capital relocation became a continuing project across administrations.
- His early espousal of economic egalitarianism prefigured later discussions of inequality and welfare-state expansion in Korea.
