# Roh Tae-woo — President (1988–1993) and the Bridge to Democracy

**Roh Tae-woo** (노태우, 1932–2021) was the thirteenth president of South Korea (1988–1993), a career military officer who made the decisive move to allow direct presidential elections in 1987 and oversaw the early consolidation of democratic governance, though his tenure was marked by continued use of security-state powers, a massive corruption scandal, and his own deep complicity in the authoritarian system he nominally reformed. His presidency bridged the Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan military regimes and the civilian democratic system, serving simultaneously as a symbol of the military's managed retreat from politics and as a figure permanently tainted by his participation in the December 1979 coup and institutional responsibility for the suppression of the Gwangju Uprising.

## Early Life and Military Career

Roh was born on **December 4, 1932**, in Gisan-ri, Gongsan-myeon, **Dalseong County, North Gyeongsang Province** (달성군, 경상북도) — now incorporated into Daegu Metropolitan City — into a family of modest rural means. His father died young, leaving the family in difficult financial circumstances, and Roh pursued military education partly as a path to stability and advancement. He entered the **Korean Military Academy** (육군사관학교, KMA) and graduated in its **11th class** (11기) in 1955, alongside **Chun Doo-hwan**, establishing a lifelong military and political relationship that would define both men's careers.

After graduating, both Roh and Chun pursued advanced military training in the United States at the Infantry School at Fort Benning, Georgia. They rose through the officer corps during the Vietnam War era, with Korean forces deployed to support the United States in Vietnam (1965–1973). Both men were members of the **Hanahoe** (하나회), an informal fraternity of Korean Military Academy officers who shared a common commitment to maintaining military influence over South Korean politics, regardless of civilian government. The Hanahoe operated as a network of mutual promotion and loyalty within the military that paralleled and often superseded the formal chain of command.

By the late 1970s, Roh had risen to major general commanding the **9th Infantry Division** (백마부대), a frontline army division. He served under General Park Chung-hee's government, which had ruled South Korea since the 1961 coup, building his reputation as a reliable, operationally competent officer with strong ties to the security establishment.

## The 1979 Coup and Gwangju

### The December 12 Coup

On **December 12, 1979** — just two months after Park Chung-hee's assassination by KCIA director Kim Jae-gyu — Roh and Chun led what became known as the **Coup of December Twelfth** (12·12 군사반란). While Chun seized operational command of the Defense Security Command (국군보안사령부) and coordinated the overall coup, Roh commanded the 9th Infantry Division, which he ordered into Seoul — pulling a frontline unit off the DMZ without authorization — to help capture or neutralize loyal senior officers who opposed the takeover.

The coup was technically conducted without authorization from the acting president or the chain of command, constituting an act of mutiny under military law. However, no officer dared challenge the conspirators' armed control, and the coup quickly succeeded. Chun became the de facto supreme commander; Roh was rewarded with command of the Capital Garrison Command (수도경비사령부).

### The Gwangju Uprising

In **May 1980**, as prodemocracy protests spread across South Korea following the expansion of martial law, the largest uprising occurred in **Gwangju** (광주), the capital of South Jeolla Province. The military dispatched elite airborne troops — drawn partly from units under Roh's earlier command network — who committed brutal atrocities against protesters and bystanders from May 18–27, 1980. The official death toll was at least 144 civilians killed, though many historians estimate the true toll exceeded 600.

Roh bore institutional military responsibility as a senior commander within the command structure that approved the deployment of paratroopers and the decision to suppress the uprising by force. His precise role in the May 17–18 decisions remained contested in later prosecutions and historical investigations, but the evidence established that he was aware of the deployments and did not oppose them.

After Gwangju, Roh served as head of the **Defense Security Command** (1980–1981) — the primary military intelligence and counter-intelligence organization — and then as Chief of Staff of the Army (1981–1982). Throughout Chun's authoritarian presidency (1980–1988), he remained one of Chun's closest political allies and was openly groomed as his successor within the regime's inner circle.

## The June Democratic Struggle and the June 29 Declaration

### The Crisis of 1987

By 1987, South Korea faced an unprecedented crisis of legitimacy. The Chun regime planned for an indirect election, with Roh to be chosen by the rubber-stamp **Indirect Election Consultative Conference** — reproducing the mechanism that had elevated Chun in 1980. The tipping point came in January 1987 when it was revealed that Seoul National University student **Park Jong-chul** had been tortured to death during interrogation by the police; the regime attempted a cover-up, which outraged the public. Additional revelations about police brutality and the regime's contempt for democratic norms drove millions into the streets.

The **June Democratic Struggle** (6월 민주항쟁) erupted in early June 1987, with massive demonstrations in Seoul, Busan, Daegu, Gwangju, and across the country. Millions of people — students, workers, middle-class professionals, religious communities — participated. The military discussed deploying troops, but Roh (apparently in consultation with the United States, which privately signaled it would not support another military crackdown comparable to Gwangju) concluded that using force would be catastrophic and that a political solution was necessary.

### The June 29 Declaration

On **June 29, 1987**, Roh appeared on national television to announce the **June 29 Declaration** (6·29 선언), also known as his Democratization Statement. Its key commitments included:

- Restoration of direct popular election for president.
- Release of political prisoners, including leading opposition figures.
- Restoration of civil liberties curtailed under martial law.
- Guarantee of freedom of the press.
- Local autonomy and decentralization.
- Constitutional revision to embed democratic guarantees.

The announcement was both a strategic concession to popular pressure — designed to prevent the collapse of all regime authority — and a genuine acknowledgment that overt military rule had become untenable. It shocked Chun and hard-liners within the regime who had wanted to deploy the military. Roh's move effectively transferred the political initiative from the streets to the electoral arena, where the regime believed it had competitive advantages.

## The 1987 Presidential Election

Roh stood as the ruling Democratic Justice Party's candidate in the **presidential election of December 16, 1987** — the first direct popular presidential election since 1971. The opposition, under intense pressure to unite around a single candidate, split fatally: **Kim Dae-jung** (김대중) and **Kim Young-sam** (김영삼), both running with strong regional bases (Kim DJ from the Honam/Jeolla region, Kim YS from the Yeongnam/Gyeongsang region), both refused to withdraw in favor of the other.

Final results:

| Candidate | Party | Vote Share |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Roh Tae-woo | Democratic Justice Party | 36.6% |
| Kim Young-sam | Reunification Democratic Party | 28.0% |
| Kim Dae-jung | Party for Peace and Democracy | 27.0% |
| Kim Jong-pil | New Democratic Republican Party | 8.1% |

Roh won with a plurality, not a majority, benefiting almost entirely from the split opposition. His victory inaugurated the **Sixth Republic** (제6공화국) under a new constitution adopted in October 1987. The Sixth Republic constitution restored direct elections, provided for a single five-year presidential term with no re-election, strengthened the legislature's independence, and entrenched judicial review — genuine democratic advances over the constitutional arrangements of the Fifth Republic (1980–1988) under Chun.

## The Presidency (1988–1993)

### The Seoul Olympics and Early Legitimacy

Roh's presidency began against the backdrop of the **1988 Seoul Summer Olympics** (September 17 – October 2, 1988), which had been awarded to Seoul in 1981 as part of the regime's effort to project international legitimacy. The games were hosted successfully and drew 159 nations (the largest Olympic participation to date), providing a significant boost to South Korea's international image and contributing to Roh's early approval ratings. Roh leveraged the Olympics narrative to brand his administration as Korea's entry into the ranks of developed nations.

### Democratic Procedures Under Strain

The Sixth Republic formally guaranteed multiparty competition and a legislature with real power, but Roh's government continued to deploy the security apparatus against political opponents. The **National Security Law** (국가보안법, NSL) was used extensively to prosecute union organizers, journalists, and pro-democracy activists. Labor disputes were often met with riot police. Despite the constitutionally strengthened National Assembly, the executive dominated legislation, and intelligence agencies continued to monitor political opposition.

In **1990**, Roh orchestrated the **Three-Party Merger** (삼당 합당), a backroom realignment in which his ruling Democratic Justice Party merged with Kim Young-sam's Reunification Democratic Party and Kim Jong-pil's New Democratic Republican Party. The merger was technically legal but widely condemned as antidemocratic: it produced a supermajority ruling bloc (renamed the **Democratic Liberal Party**) without any intervening election, effectively neutralizing opposition legislative power. Kim Young-sam, who joined the merger, gained the ruling party's presidential nomination in 1992, while Kim Dae-jung remained outside.

### Northern Breakthrough Policy (Nordpolitik)

Roh's most significant foreign-policy achievement was the **Northern Breakthrough Policy** (북방 정책, Bukpang Jeongchaek), a diplomatic opening toward the communist states of Eastern Europe and ultimately the Soviet Union and China. Drawing on West Germany's *Ostpolitik* as a model, Roh pursued normalization despite the Cold War context. Key diplomatic milestones:

- **1989** — Diplomatic relations with Hungary and Poland, the first Eastern Bloc states to recognize South Korea.
- **1990** — Diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union (September 30, 1990), after a summit between Roh and Mikhail Gorbachev in San Francisco in June 1990. A $3 billion South Korean economic assistance package facilitated the deal.
- **1991** — South Korea and North Korea simultaneously joined the **United Nations** (September 17, 1991), ending the long stalemate over Korean representation in international bodies.
- **1991** — The **Agreement on Reconciliation, Non-Aggression, and Exchanges and Cooperation** (기본합의서, "Basic Agreement") between North and South Korea was signed in December 1991, committing both sides to non-aggression and cooperation in principle — though implementation remained limited.
- **1992** — Diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China (August 24, 1992), ending decades of mutual non-recognition.

The Northern Breakthrough dramatically expanded South Korea's international partnerships and reduced its Cold War isolation, though it did not fundamentally alter North-South relations, which remained tense.

### Economic Policy and the Chaebol

Roh's economic strategy largely continued the developmental-state model while adapting to a middle-income economy. He supported the chaebol's expansion into high-technology sectors — semiconductors, electronics, automotive — which accelerated Samsung and Hyundai's rise to global prominence. GDP per capita rose from approximately $3,500 in 1988 to $8,000 by 1993, and exports expanded rapidly.

However, the period also saw inflation in housing and land prices, wage pressures as Korea's labor movement strengthened after 1987, and growing current-account deficits. The speculative real-estate bubble of the late 1980s became a serious social problem, with housing affordability declining sharply for middle-class Koreans despite rapid wage growth.

## Post-Presidential Prosecution and Conviction

After leaving the presidency on February 25, 1993, and transferring power to civilian successor Kim Young-sam, Roh lost presidential immunity. The Kim Young-sam government launched broad investigations into the conduct of the Chun and Roh administrations.

In **October 1995**, Roh publicly admitted to having accumulated a secret political slush fund (비자금) of approximately **500 billion won** (roughly $625 million at the time) during his presidency, received as payments from major business conglomerates seeking government contracts, regulatory favors, and political access. The admission triggered criminal investigations.

In **August 1996**, a Seoul district court convicted Roh on four counts:

1. **Treason** — for his role in the December 12, 1979 coup.
2. **Mutiny** — for the military conspiracy against the constitutional chain of command.
3. **Crimes related to the Gwangju Uprising** — for his military command responsibility.
4. **Bribery and corruption** — for the 500-billion-won slush fund.

He was sentenced to **22.5 years** in prison; on appeal, the sentence was reduced to **17 years**. Chun Doo-hwan received a death sentence (commuted to life imprisonment on appeal). Both men were held at Seoul Detention House.

In **December 1997**, with South Korea in the grip of the IMF financial crisis — the most severe economic contraction since the Korean War — outgoing President Kim Young-sam granted a full pardon to both Roh and Chun, framed as a gesture toward national reconciliation at a moment of crisis. The pardon, which Kim Young-sam coordinated with incoming president Kim Dae-jung's acquiescence, was deeply controversial. Pro-democracy activists and Gwangju survivors condemned it as a betrayal of accountability. Roh had served approximately **two years and three months** of his sentence when released.

Roh Tae-woo died on **October 26, 2021**, at age 88 from complications of multiple chronic illnesses. His death renewed debate about his contested legacy. The government provided a national funeral with limited official honors, reflecting the unresolved tension between his role in Korea's democratic transition and his responsibility for authoritarian abuses.

## Historical Significance

Roh Tae-woo's presidency represents the threshold between military authoritarianism and democratic consolidation in South Korea. He did not initiate the transition — the June Democratic Struggle of 1987 forced his hand — but he executed the political decision to allow direct elections and multiparty competition when his military peers wished to use force. This made him simultaneously:

- A figure who recognized the unsustainability of overt military rule and accommodated democratic demands at a critical moment.
- A continuity candidate who sought to preserve military influence through civilian constitutional frameworks and the Three-Party Merger.
- A participant in the authoritarian abuses of 1979–1987 never fully held accountable — released after only 2.3 years of a 17-year sentence.
- A corrupt figure who systematically extracted hundreds of millions of dollars from business conglomerates using presidential power.
- A bridge builder between authoritarianism and democracy whose personal moral failings undermined the moral authority needed to lead the transition.

The institutional reforms of the Sixth Republic — single five-year presidential term, independent legislature, constitutional judicial review — proved durable and were not reversed by subsequent governments. But the incomplete accountability for Gwangju and the early pardons left deep wounds in South Korean civic culture that resurfaced repeatedly in later political controversies, including during the impeachment of Park Geun-hye and beyond.
