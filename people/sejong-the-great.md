# Sejong the Great

**Sejong the Great** (세종대왕, r. 1418–1450) was the fourth king of the Joseon dynasty and one of history's most accomplished monarchs. In a thirty-two-year reign, he created Hangul—the Korean writing system still in use today—oversaw a flowering of science and technology, reformed the military and civil administration, and expanded Korea's northern frontier to the Tumen and Yalu rivers. He is consistently ranked in surveys of Korean history as the greatest ruler the peninsula has produced and is commemorated on the 10,000-won banknote.

## Early Life and Accession

Born Yi Do on May 15, 1397, as the third son of King Taejong (r. 1400–1418), Sejong was not initially the designated successor. His eldest brother Yi Je was crown prince. Taejong, who had seized power through two ruthless succession struggles (the Strife of Princes, 1398 and 1400), recognized early that his third son's intellectual gifts and moral character outstripped his siblings'. In 1418, Yi Je voluntarily yielded the crown-prince position after a personal failing; Taejong elevated Yi Do and then, later that year, abdicated himself—a calculated move that allowed Taejong to retain real military authority (and execute political enemies) while Sejong governed the civil realm.

Sejong thus ascended at twenty-two with seasoned advisors and a powerful father still active behind the scenes. Taejong died in 1422, leaving Sejong in full command. The four years of shared power gave the young king governing experience that proved invaluable.

Sejong's Confucian education was exceptional. He reportedly read each classical text one hundred times and memorized vast passages; contemporaries recorded that he worked past midnight studying even when ill. This personal discipline shaped his expectations of his court.

## The Hall of Worthies and Royal Scholarship

In 1420, Sejong dramatically expanded the royal research institute known as the **Hall of Worthies** (Jiphyeonjeon, 집현전), transforming it from a modest advisory body into a state-funded scholarly academy. He recruited young scholars of exceptional promise and gave them paid leave for years of undisturbed research—an early form of academic fellowship.

The Hall's alumni included some of Joseon's finest intellects:

- **Jeong In-ji** (정인지, 1396–1478) — compiler of the official history *Goryeo-sa* (History of Goryeo, 1451) and a key collaborator on *Hunminjeongeum*
- **Sin Suk-ju** (신숙주, 1417–1475) — linguist and diplomat who studied Chinese and Japanese phonology, contributing expertise to the Hangul phonological system
- **Park Paeng-nyeon** (박팽년, 1417–1456) — poet and scholar who later died resisting the Sejo coup
- **Seong Sam-mun** (성삼문, 1418–1456) — renowned for loyalty and classical scholarship, also later executed by Sejo

The Hall produced encyclopedic works on history, geography, medicine, music, and astronomy. It was dissolved by Sejong's son Sejo in 1456 after scholars plotted to restore the deposed boy-king Danjong, and six of its members were executed—an episode that cemented their posthumous reputation as loyal martyrs.

## The Creation of Hangul (1443–1446)

Sejong's most enduring contribution to humanity is **Hangul** (한글), the Korean phonetic alphabet. He commissioned the system around 1443, working intensively and in secrecy with a select group from the Hall of Worthies, and promulgated it in 1446 through the royal document *Hunminjeongeum* (훈민정음, "The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People").

### The Problem Hangul Solved

Before 1446, Korea had no indigenous writing system. The educated elite wrote in Classical Chinese (hanja), which required years of study and bore no systematic relationship to spoken Korean. Ordinary Koreans—merchants, farmers, women—had no practical access to written communication. Administrative texts, laws, and medical guides were inaccessible to those who most needed them.

### Design Principles

Hangul's design is scientifically distinctive among the world's writing systems:

- **Featural phonetics** — each consonant symbol depicts the position of the tongue, lips, and throat during articulation. The symbol ㄱ (g/k) represents the tongue blocking the back of the throat; ㄴ (n) shows the tongue touching the upper palate; ㅁ (m) represents closed lips. Related sounds share visual elements.
- **Syllabic clustering** — individual letters are grouped into syllable blocks rather than written linearly, a structural concession to Korean phonological patterns.
- **Vowel system** — based on three philosophical elements (heaven, earth, human) rendered as a dot, horizontal line, and vertical line, from which all vowel symbols are composed.

The original system comprised 17 consonants and 11 vowels (modern Hangul uses 14 and 10, respectively). The *Hunminjeongeum Haerye* ("Explanatory Notes on Hangul"), discovered only in 1940, provided scholarly commentary on the system's phonological and philosophical foundations. It is inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register (1997).

### Reception and Spread

Conservative yangban scholars, led by figures like Choe Manri, formally petitioned Sejong in 1444 to abandon Hangul, arguing it would undermine Chinese cultural ties and reduce the scholarly monopoly on literacy. Sejong rejected the petition with characteristic patience, explaining that Hangul was not intended to replace hanja but to provide commoners access to practical knowledge.

He immediately used Hangul to translate Buddhist texts (*Worin Cheongang Jigok*, "Songs of the Moon's Reflection on a Thousand Rivers"), legal codes, and agricultural manuals into accessible form. His daughters and court women became among the earliest Hangul users. Over the following centuries, Hangul spread steadily; women's correspondence (*Eongan*), popular fiction, and administrative notices increasingly used the script. By the late Joseon period, the term *eonmun* (vernacular script) sometimes carried mild condescension, but Hangul's practical dominance was irreversible. The 1894 Gabo Reforms elevated Hangul to co-equal status with hanja in government documents; by the mid-twentieth century, hanja was largely marginal.

## Science and Technology

Sejong's court produced a burst of scientific achievement concentrated in the 1430s–1440s, driven in part by the remarkable craftsman-inventor **Jang Yeong-sil** (장영실, c. 1390–c. 1450). Jang was of enslaved origin (his mother was a government slave), but Sejong personally insisted on his elevation to royal service, overriding bureaucratic resistance.

### Jang Yeong-sil's Inventions

- **Cheugugi** (측우기, rain gauge, 1441) — the world's first standardized rain gauge, placed at government offices across the kingdom to provide systematic precipitation data for agricultural planning. This predates Western standardized rain measurement by approximately two centuries.
- **Jagyeongnu** (자격루, self-striking water clock, 1434) — an automated clepsydra that used bronze balls rolling down ramps to trigger time-indicating figures; it struck bells and gongs at set intervals without human intervention. Placed at the main palace gate to regulate public time.
- **Angbuilgu** (앙부일구, hemispherical sundial, 1434) — a bronze bowl-shaped sundial engraved with hour lines and seasonal markers, installed in public spaces in Seoul so commoners could tell time. Notable for its accessibility to all social classes.
- **Honui** (혼의, armillary sphere) — astronomical instrument for measuring celestial positions, refined to Korean standards and used for independent astronomical observation.

### Astronomical Calendar Reform

Sejong commissioned a complete revision of the Korean astronomical calendar. Korean scholars had long relied on Chinese astronomical calculations, which were imprecise at Korean latitudes. The *Chiljeongsan* (七政算, "Calculation of the Seven Celestial Bodies," 1442–1444) was a two-volume astronomical manual incorporating Arab, Chinese, and independent Korean observations. It produced a calendar accurate to Korean meridians rather than Chinese ones—a declaration of astronomical independence that reinforced Joseon's cultural autonomy within the tributary system.

### Agricultural and Medical Texts

- **Nongsa Jikseol** (농사직설, "Straight Talks on Farming," 1429) — Korea's first indigenous agricultural manual, compiled by surveying experienced farmers rather than relying on Chinese texts. It described Korean-specific crop varieties, soil types, irrigation techniques, and planting schedules.
- **Hyangyak Jipseongbang** (향약집성방, "Compilation of Native Korean Prescriptions," 1433) — an 85-volume medical encyclopedia systematizing indigenous Korean herbal medicine with guidance from Chinese and Korean medical traditions. Made medical knowledge accessible to provincial physicians and potentially literate commoners.

## Military Affairs and Northern Expansion

### The Gihae Eastern Expedition (1419)

In June 1419, Joseon forces under admiral Yi Jong-mu launched a preemptive strike against Tsushima Island (對馬島), the base of *waegu* (Japanese pirates) who had raided the southern Korean coast. The expedition, authorized by Taejong just before his abdication and implemented under Sejong, landed approximately 17,000 troops and destroyed hundreds of ships and pirate installations. Although Joseon forces eventually withdrew without occupying Tsushima permanently, the campaign largely ended large-scale *waegu* raiding for decades and demonstrated the kingdom's willingness to project force across the Korea Strait.

### The Four Northern Outposts and Six Garrison Forts

The more lasting military achievement was consolidation of Korea's northern frontier:

- **Four Northern Outposts** (사군, Sagun) — established 1433–1443 along the upper Yalu River under generals Choe Yun-deok and Yi Cheon, pushing the Jurchen tribes back from the region.
- **Six Garrison Forts** (육진, Yukchin) — established 1434–1437 along the Tumen River under Kim Jong-seo, creating a fortified belt that defined Korea's northeastern border. The Tumen River remains the boundary between North Korea and China to this day.

These campaigns required substantial military investment and skilled diplomacy. Sejong combined deterrence with tributary incorporation of cooperative Jurchen leaders, offering titles and trade rights to those who acknowledged Joseon suzerainty. The consolidation permanently added substantial territory to the Korean state.

### Military Technology

Sejong sponsored improvements in gunpowder weaponry, shipboard artillery, and military organization. Korean cannon designs were systematized and standardized; coastal artillery was deployed to defend harbors. A revised military code (*Byeongjeon*) reorganized command structures and supply chains, laying groundwork for the military system Yi Sun-sin would inherit over a century later (see [Yi Sun-sin](yi-sun-sin.md)).

## Civil Governance

### The Sase System and Equitable Taxation

In 1444, after twenty years of deliberation and nationwide surveys, Sejong implemented the *Gongbeop* taxation reform, replacing the older annual fixed-rate grain tax with a sliding scale tied to harvest quality and regional productivity—a proto-progressive agricultural tax system. The reform took twelve years of research, including a national survey of approximately 170,000 households. This was among the most extensive empirical research projects in premodern Korean governance.

### Remonstrance and Advisory Channels

Sejong formalized channels for ministers and censors to criticize royal decisions. The Office of the Censor-General (Saganwon, 사간원) had authority to remonstrate with the king; Sejong accepted criticism from officials who disagreed with him, and records show him modifying decisions in response to principled objections. This culture of consultative governance was exceptional for the era.

### Legal Codification

Building on earlier Joseon legal texts, scholars under Sejong's direction worked toward a comprehensive legal code. The *Jiphyeon Six Codes* project (later completed as the *Gyeonggukdaejeon* under Sejo in 1485) compiled civil, criminal, and ceremonial law into a coherent unified code—the legal foundation of the Joseon state.

## Personal Life

Sejong's primary consort was **Queen Soheon** (昭憲王后, 1395–1446), of the Cheongsong Sim clan, who bore him eight sons and two daughters. Queen Soheon was herself regarded as a woman of considerable learning; she is credited with composing Buddhist hymns in Hangul. She died eight months before the public promulgation of *Hunminjeongeum*, and Sejong, reportedly devastated, declined formal mourning events.

Sejong suffered increasingly poor health in his later years—likely complications of diabetes, given historical descriptions of excessive thirst and recurring vision problems. He delegated more duties to Crown Prince Munjong from the mid-1440s. He died in 1450, at age fifty-two (Korean counting), having reigned thirty-two years. Munjong succeeded him without dispute.

## Legacy and Cultural Memory

Sejong is commemorated throughout modern Korea:

- His portrait has appeared on the 10,000-won banknote since the 1970s.
- **Sejong the Great-class destroyers** — Korea's most advanced warships carry his name.
- **Sejong City** — a new administrative capital district south of Seoul, designated in 2012, bears his name.
- **Sejong Center for the Performing Arts** — Seoul's premier concert and arts venue.
- **The Sejong Institute** — Korean language and culture centers worldwide, analogous to France's Alliance Française.
- **Hangul Day** (한글날, October 9) — a national public holiday commemorating the 1446 promulgation of *Hunminjeongeum*.

In popular culture, he has been depicted in major television dramas (*Tree with Deep Roots*, 2011) and films. International historians of science rank the Joseon scientific flourishing under Sejong alongside the Renaissance as an example of state-sponsored intellectual achievement.
