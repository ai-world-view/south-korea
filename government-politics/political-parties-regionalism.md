# Political Parties and Regionalism in South Korea

**Category**: Government & Politics  
**Key figures**: Kim Dae-jung, Kim Young-sam, Roh Moo-hyun, Moon Jae-in, Lee Myung-bak, Park Geun-hye, Yoon Suk-yeol

## Summary

South Korean politics since democratization (1987) has been structured around two competing political blocs rooted in regional divisions and historical ruptures. The **regional divide** between TK (영남—Yeongnam, comprising Daegu and North/South Gyeongsang provinces) and Honam (호남—the Jeolla region of southwestern Korea) has been the dominant axis of political identity, often overshadowing ideology. The **Democratic Party lineage** (민주당, led by figures like Kim Dae-jung, Kim Young-sam, and Roh Moo-hyun) has traditionally drawn support from Honam and has advocated for reconciliation with North Korea, labor rights, and a progressive social agenda. The **conservative bloc** (보수진영, anchored by the Democratic Republican Party and later the Grand National Party and People Power Party) has dominated TK and has emphasized anti-communism, security, and developmental economics. The party system has undergone multiple reorganizations, mergers, and rebrandings (1995 three-party merger, 2012 realignments), yet regionalism has proven remarkably persistent as a predictor of voting behavior, even as younger voters show signs of weakening regional attachment.

## Regional Foundations and Historical Origins

The TK-Honam divide has deep historical roots. TK (particularly Daegu and North Gyeongsang) became the political and economic heartland of the developmental state under Park Chung-hee (r. 1961–1979), a native of Gumi in North Gyeongsang. Park's regime poured investment into TK industries, military recruitment, and bureaucratic positions, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of regional advantage and political loyalty. Honam (Gwangju and the Jeolla provinces), by contrast, was marginalized and neglected economically; the region became associated with anti-Park activism and democratic resistance, including the 1980 Gwangju Uprising. This historical divergence—development vs. marginalization, military loyalty vs. resistance—crystallized into a durable regional cleavage. Subsequent democractic leaders from Honam (Kim Dae-jung, a Jeolla native; Roh Moo-hyun, though from the Yeongnam port city of Busan but with strong leftist credentials) struggled to overcome the regional barrier; Kim Dae-jung's 1997 victory required an unprecedented coalition with Kim Jong-pil's United Liberal Democrats, demonstrating just how powerful regional voting blocs had become.

## The Democratic Party Lineage

The Democratic Party traces its lineage to the democratic opposition during military rule and democratization. Kim Dae-jung's Party for Peace and Democracy (평화민주당, PPD) emerged from the 1987 split in the democratic opposition; it advocated for a peace-oriented approach toward North Korea, stronger labor protections, and regional development in Honam. After the 1990 three-party merger consolidated the conservative bloc (see below), Kim Dae-jung reorganized his supporters as the Democratic Party (민주당, 1991) and continued as the leading opposition force. His political survival through the 1990s required extraordinary coalition-building: the 1997 presidential campaign saw Kim form an unprecedented alliance with Kim Jong-pil (자유민주연합, United Liberal Democrats), a former KCIA director and founder of the Park Chung-hee regime, in order to split the conservative vote and secure a national majority despite regional barriers. Kim Dae-jung's 1997 victory—with 40.3% of the vote against Lee Hoi-chang's 38.7%, a margin of only 1.6 percentage points—was the first transfer of presidential power to an opposition party in Korean history. It was structurally enabled by: Kim Jong-pil's Chungcheong province bloc (delivering roughly 4–5% nationally), generational shifts in Seoul, and regional consolidation in Honam (where KDJ received over 92% in South Jeolla province).

Subsequent rebrandings—Uri Party (열린우리당, 2003–2007), Democratic Party (2008–2014), Minjoo Party of Korea (더불어민주당, 2015–present)—reflected both organizational consolidation and the party's attempt to broaden its coalition beyond Honam. The Uri Party, formed around Roh Moo-hyun's reformist supporters after a 2004 split, unexpectedly won 152 seats in the April 2004 legislative elections (a landslide following Roh's populist impeachment by the GNP-controlled assembly), marking the center-left's strongest legislative performance. Roh Moo-hyun's 2003–2008 presidency advanced labor reforms, inter-Korean engagement (the 2007 inter-Korean summit and the 2007 Kaesong Industrial Complex expansion), and administrative decentralization. Roh's successor, Moon Jae-in (2017–2022), was a human rights lawyer who had been Roh's chief of staff; he won the 2017 snap election (following Park Geun-hye's impeachment) with 41.1% and pursued the 2018 inter-Korean summits (Panmunjom, Pyongyang) and summit diplomacy with Donald Trump. The party won a sweeping 180-seat majority (including satellite party seats) in the April 2020 legislative elections—the largest single-party majority since democratization—driven partly by Moon's successful early COVID-19 response. However, growing public discontent over housing prices, youth unemployment, and perceived favoritism led to Yoon Suk-yeol's 2022 presidential victory by a margin of 0.73 percentage points (247,077 votes out of 34 million cast).

## The Conservative Bloc and TK Dominance

The conservative bloc crystallized around the Democratic Republican Party (민주공화당) during Park Chung-hee's rule and survived his 1979 assassination and the 1980 Gwangju crisis under Chun Doo-hwan and Roh Tae-woo. Roh Tae-woo's 1987 election—with only 36.6% of the vote, possible because the democratic opposition split between Kim Dae-jung and Kim Young-sam—marked the first direct presidential election under democratic rules; Roh's victory depended on TK support and the divided opposition.

The pivotal organizational moment for the conservative bloc came in **January 1990**, when the **three-party merger** (삼당합당) united Roh Tae-woo's Democratic Justice Party (DJP), Kim Young-sam's Reunification Democratic Party (RDP), and Kim Jong-pil's New Democratic Republican Party (NDRP) into the **Democratic Liberal Party** (민주자유당, DLP). This merger—which critics labeled a "political coup without weapons"—was engineered to neutralize the centrist opposition and consolidate institutional power. Crucially, it brought Kim Young-sam (from Busan, a southeastern port city adjacent to TK but not classically "TK") into the conservative coalition, enabling his 1992 presidential victory with 42% of the vote. Kim Young-sam's presidency (1993–1998), while deploying reformist rhetoric, was ultimately embedded in the conservative developmental-state tradition; his administration's liberalization initiatives (segyehwa, OECD accession) created the vulnerabilities that contributed to the 1997 financial crisis.

The DLP restructured as the New Korea Party (신한국당, 1995–1997), then as the Grand National Party (한나라당, GNP, 1997–2012). The GNP lost the 1997 presidential election—its candidate Lee Hoi-chang received 38.7% to Kim Dae-jung's 40.3%—and again in 2002 when Lee Hoi-chang again ran and received 46.6% to Roh Moo-hyun's 48.9%. But the party won a decisive legislative majority in 2004 before losing it after Roh's populist mobilization; its candidate Lee Myung-bak won the 2007 presidential election with 48.7% to Chung Dong-young's 26.1%, a landslide driven by economic discontent and Roh's low popularity.

Daegu and North Gyeongsang became so reliably conservative that the region earned the label "TK fortress" (TK 요새)—polls showed 60–80% conservative support in metropolitan Daegu through the 2000s–2010s. Lee Myung-bak's presidency (2008–2013) presided over the Four Major Rivers Project (4대강 사업, a $17 billion waterway infrastructure program that became a major controversy) and the 2010 G20 Seoul Summit. The 2012 presidential election saw Park Geun-hye (a native of Daegu and daughter of Park Chung-hee) win with 51.6%—the first woman elected president in Korean history—on the back of overwhelming TK support and a fragmented opposition. Park's presidency (2013–2017) ended in the massive candlelight vigils of 2016–2017 and her impeachment in December 2016 (upheld by the Constitutional Court in March 2017) over a corruption and influence-peddling scandal involving confidante Choi Soon-sil.

The GNP/Saenuri Party restructured as the **People Power Party** (국민의힘, PPP, 2020–present) following the chaos of Park's impeachment. Yoon Suk-yeol, a former Prosecutor General with no prior electoral office, won the 2022 presidential election by the narrowest margin in Korean democratic history (0.73 percentage points).

## Regional Voting Patterns and Persistence

Electoral data from presidential and legislative contests consistently demonstrate the power of regionalism. In the 2002 presidential election, Roh Moo-hyun won nationwide (48.9%) but secured 79% in South Jeolla and merely 27% in Daegu. In 2007, Lee Myung-bak won with 53% nationally but carried 74% in Daegu and North Gyeongsang vs. 29% in South Jeolla. The 2012 election reinforced this divide: Park Geun-hye won 60.6% nationally but took 76% in her home Daegu and North Gyeongsang, while Moon Jae-in secured 68% in Honam. These regional disparities—often exceeding 40–50 percentage points—far exceed ideological or programmatic differences and suggest that regional identity (and attendant historical grievances) overwhelms swing voting. Legislative elections show similar patterns: in 2016, the Minjoo party won 43 of its 123 seats from the five southeastern/southwestern provinces, concentrated in Honam. Younger voters (ages 18–40) and residents of Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province show somewhat weaker regional attachment and higher volatility, but the effect remains significant even among Gen Z. The persistence of regionalism despite rapid urbanization, generational turnover, and economic modernization suggests deep historical and cultural roots rather than purely instrumental calculation.

## Contemporary Party System and the 2024–2025 Crisis

As of 2024–2025, South Korea's two-party system remains bifurcated along regional and historical lines, though with extraordinary volatility driven by institutional crisis and generational shifts.

### April 2024 Legislative Elections

The **22nd National Assembly elections** (April 10, 2024) delivered a sweeping rebuke of President Yoon Suk-yeol's People Power Party (PPP). The Democratic Party of Korea (DP) and its satellite, the Democratic Alliance, won 175 seats combined against the PPP's 108, giving the DP-led bloc its second consecutive supermajority. Turnout was 67.0%, the highest for a legislative election since 1992. Regional patterns held: Daegu and North/South Gyeongsang delivered 60–70% conservative vote shares, while the Jeolla provinces gave 75–80% to DP candidates. However, the swing in Seoul (DP took 37 of 48 seats) and Gyeonggi Province (DP took 55 of 60 seats) reflected deep dissatisfaction with Yoon's governance on cost-of-living, housing prices, and healthcare policy.

### The December 2024 Martial Law Crisis and Yoon's Removal

On **December 3, 2024**, President Yoon Suk-yeol declared emergency martial law (비상계엄) at approximately 10:30 PM, citing what he characterized as "anti-state" forces within the National Assembly obstructing his governance. This was the first martial-law declaration since 1980. The National Assembly voted within hours—190 to 0, with the 190 members who reached the chamber all in favor—to invalidate the declaration, as required by Article 77 of the Constitution. Yoon lifted martial law by 4:30 AM on December 4. The episode triggered a constitutional crisis. The National Assembly voted to impeach Yoon on **December 14, 2024** (204 votes for, 85 against, requiring a two-thirds majority of the 300-member assembly), with approximately 12 PPP members crossing party lines. Prime Minister Han Duck-soo served as acting president. On **April 4, 2025**, the Constitutional Court upheld the impeachment unanimously (8–0), removing Yoon from the presidency—only the second presidential removal in Korean history (after Park Geun-hye in 2017).

### The June 2025 Snap Presidential Election

A snap election was held on **June 3, 2025** following the 60-day constitutional deadline. The Democratic Party nominated **Lee Jae-myung** (이재명), a Gyeonggi-native former governor and long-time party leader who had narrowly lost the 2022 race. The PPP nominated **Kim Moon-soo** after internal divisions. Regional voting patterns persisted: Daegu and TK delivered 55–65% for the PPP candidate, while Honam gave Lee Jae-myung 80%+ support. Lee won the election, becoming the 21st President of South Korea.

### Electoral Architecture: Satellite Parties and PR Threshold

The 2020 election introduced "satellite parties" (위성정당) as a workaround to circumvent the mixed-member proportional representation (준연동형 비례대표제) adopted in December 2019. Both the DP and GNP created separate proportional-representation parties to capture list seats while their main parties competed in single-member districts—a maneuver that essentially nullified the reform's intent. The 2024 elections repeated this pattern, with DP and PPP each fielding satellite parties. Minor parties—the Justice Party (정의당, the progressive left), the New Reform Party (개혁신당, centrist liberal), and the Green Party—remain marginal due to the dominant single-member-district system and the 3% threshold for proportional representation. Term-limit rules (a single five-year presidency, no re-election) have intensified internal party realignments, as successive presidential elections function as quasi-referendums on the incumbent's performance rather than long-term party governance.

## Gender and Generational Cleavages

Beyond region, two additional cleavage dimensions have emerged as analytically significant. **Gender** has become an increasingly contested axis: in the 2022 presidential election, exit polls showed men aged 18–29 voting for Yoon Suk-yeol at approximately 59%, while women in the same age group voted for Lee Jae-myung at approximately 58%—an extraordinary intra-cohort gender gap driven in part by debates over feminist policy, mandatory military service, and economic competition between young men and women. This "gender war" (젠더 갈등) dynamic, though often overstated in media coverage, represents a genuine departure from the prior pattern in which TK–Honam regionalism was the primary cleavage.

**Generational cleavage** has also grown. Voters in their 20s–30s ("MZ generation") show less intense regional attachment than voters over 50; they are more likely to swing between parties based on economic conditions (housing affordability, youth unemployment, which spiked above 10% during 2015–2020) and individual candidate characteristics. Seoul's 2021 mayoral by-election—where the PPP's Oh Se-hoon won 57.5% of the vote in a traditionally competitive city, with strong support from young men—demonstrated the volatility of the youth vote. However, the 2024 legislative elections showed young voters swinging back decisively toward the DP, suggesting that generational volatility is real but not uniformly conservative. The persistence of the TK–Honam divide among voters over 60, who turn out at higher rates, means that regional voting remains the structural anchor of the system even as generational dynamics add volatility at the margins.
