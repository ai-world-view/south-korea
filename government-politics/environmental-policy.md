# Environmental Policy and Sustainability in South Korea

**Category**: Government & Politics  
**Key figures**: Lee Myung-bak (Green Growth initiative), Moon Jae-in (carbon neutrality pivot), Yoon Suk-yeol

## Summary

South Korea's environmental policy reflects the tension between rapid industrialization, resource constraints, and rising global commitments to sustainability. As a resource-poor nation dependent on imports, South Korea has historically prioritized economic growth over environmental protection, yet has increasingly embraced climate action as a strategic imperative. The government's major environmental initiatives include the 2008–2017 Green Growth program under Lee Myung-bak (pursuing economic expansion through green technology), the 2020 Green New Deal under Moon Jae-in (pivoting toward carbon neutrality), and the Yoon Suk-yeol administration's dual focus on carbon-neutral goals while reversing the nuclear phase-out. Environmental regulation covers air and water quality, industrial emissions, waste management, and nature preservation, with enforcement challenges in industrial regions like Ulsan and Yeosu. South Korea ranks among the OECD's highest in air pollution episodes (seasonal fine particulate matter / *hwangsa*, or yellow dust, originating from China and domestic sources), water pollution in industrialized zones, and plastic waste generation, yet has also developed renewable energy capacity (solar, offshore wind) and adopted ambitious net-zero commitments aligned with global climate frameworks.

## Significance

Environmental policy is central to South Korea's dual challenges: maintaining competitiveness in energy-intensive industries (semiconductors, petrochemicals, steel, shipbuilding) while meeting stringent climate targets under the Paris Agreement and the UN Sustainable Development Goals. The 2020 commitment to carbon neutrality by 2050 and the 2030 NDC target of 40% emissions reduction (from 2018 baseline) shape industrial regulation, energy strategy, and corporate investment. Environmental concerns also intersect with public health (respiratory disease, water safety), regional politics (industrial zones' impact on neighboring communities), and South Korea's soft-power positioning in a climate-conscious global economy. The reversal of the nuclear phase-out and expansion of renewables represent ongoing policy contestation between short-term industrial stability and long-term ecological sustainability. Environmental governance has gradually shifted from reactive compliance to proactive sustainability planning, reflected in the elevation of the Ministry of Environment (established in 1994 from the earlier Environment Administration and progressively granted authority over water, waste, and climate policy), the adoption of corporate ESG (environmental, social, governance) reporting, and the emergence of environmental NGOs (the Korean Federation for Environmental Movement, Greenpeace Korea) as policy stakeholders.

## Key Policies and Initiatives

### Green Growth (2008–2017)

President Lee Myung-bak's Green Growth initiative (녹색성장, "Low Carbon, Green Growth") was unveiled in August 2008 and elevated to a constitutional-level commitment through the **Framework Act on Low Carbon, Green Growth** (enacted in January 2010). The initiative committed approximately 107 trillion won ($85 billion) over five years to green sectors including renewable energy, electric vehicles, energy-efficient buildings, and green technology R&D. The centerpiece infrastructure project was the **Four Major Rivers Restoration Project** (4대강 살리기 사업, 2009–2011), a 22-trillion-won program to dredge and reconstruct the Han, Nakdong, Geum, and Yeongsan rivers, creating 16 weirs (moveable dams), reservoirs, and riverside parks. Proponents argued it would prevent floods, secure water resources, and revitalize the rural economy. Critics—including environmental organizations, local residents, and later government auditors—found that the weirs disrupted natural river flow, caused algal blooms (particularly on the Nakdong and Geum rivers), degraded aquatic ecosystems, and did not meaningfully reduce flood risk. Subsequent administrations spent additional billions on ecological restoration and weir removal. The Green Growth initiative also established South Korea's first **Renewable Portfolio Standard** (RPS, 2012), requiring power utilities to source a rising percentage of electricity from renewables, and a national commitment to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 30% below business-as-usual (BAU) projections by 2020—a target that was partially met.

### Korea Emissions Trading System (K-ETS, 2015–present)

South Korea launched the **Korea Emissions Trading Scheme** (K-ETS, 한국 배출권 거래제) in January 2015, becoming the first country in East Asia to operate a cap-and-trade carbon market. The K-ETS covers approximately 70% of South Korea's total greenhouse gas emissions, applying to over 600 companies in energy-intensive sectors (power generation, steel, cement, petrochemicals, aviation). Phase 1 (2015–2017) distributed allowances largely for free; Phase 2 (2018–2020) introduced a small share of auctioned allowances; Phase 3 (2021–2025) increased auction proportions and tightened the emissions cap. Carbon credit prices have fluctuated from as low as 5,000–8,000 won per tonne CO₂ to over 25,000 won in periods of scarcity. The K-ETS has been studied as a policy model by China, Japan, and other Asian economies developing carbon markets.

### Carbon Neutrality and the Green New Deal (2020–2022)

Moon Jae-in's administration announced a **2050 carbon-neutrality goal** in October 2020—one of the first Asian governments to commit to net-zero by mid-century—and introduced the **Korean New Deal** (한국판 뉴딜), incorporating a Green New Deal component with planned investments of 73.4 trillion won ($60 billion) through 2025. Green New Deal components included: installation of 2.4 million solar and wind energy facilities; retrofitting 225,000 public buildings for energy efficiency; building 1.13 million smart homes and schools; electric vehicle infrastructure (electric buses, hydrogen fuel cell vehicles); and ocean and coastal ecosystem restoration. In October 2021, South Korea submitted an enhanced **Nationally Determined Contribution** (NDC) to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, raising the 2030 emissions reduction target from the previous 26.3% below 2017 levels to **40% below 2018 baseline levels** (approximately 291 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent)—a significantly more ambitious target that placed South Korea among the more aggressive OECD commitments. The Moon administration also legislated the **Carbon Neutrality and Green Growth Act** (탄소중립·녹색성장 기본법) in August 2021, enshrining the 2050 net-zero goal in law and establishing the Carbon Neutrality and Green Growth Commission as a presidential advisory body. Under Moon, the government committed to phasing out coal-fired power (approximately 26 coal plants to close by 2034) while simultaneously pursuing a nuclear phase-out—a combination that energy analysts noted created significant tension in the energy mix, as replacing both coal and nuclear with renewables within a few decades would require extraordinary grid investment.

### Energy Transition and Nuclear Reversal (2022–2026)

The Yoon Suk-yeol administration (inaugurated May 2022) reversed course on nuclear energy within months of taking office. The 10th Basic Plan for Electricity Supply and Demand (제10차 전력수급기본계획, finalized January 2023) raised nuclear's share of the electricity mix to 32.4% by 2036 (up from Moon's target of 23.9%), approved extending the operational licenses of aging reactors including Wolsong Unit 1 (which had been prematurely shut down under Moon), and confirmed construction of two new **APR-1400** (Advanced Power Reactor 1,400 MW) units at the Shin Hanul site in North Gyeongsang Province. South Korea's **APR-1400** reactor design has achieved global export success: four APR-1400 units were built at Barakah in the United Arab Emirates (completed 2020–2024), representing the largest South Korean energy export contract in history (approximately $20 billion). The Yoon administration's nuclear-friendly stance also positioned Korea as a vendor for new nuclear projects in Central and Eastern Europe (Poland, Czech Republic, Romania). Simultaneously, Yoon's government maintained the 2030 NDC target of 40% emissions reduction, intending to achieve it primarily through a combination of nuclear (as low-carbon baseload), accelerated offshore wind development, and green hydrogen. Offshore wind targets were set at 14.3 GW by 2030, concentrated in the waters off Jeollanam-do and the West Sea. Critics noted that the administration's reliance on nuclear and the slowdown of solar deployment risked missing the 2030 NDC and weakened South Korea's global climate credibility. The Constitutional Court's impeachment ruling against Yoon in April 2025 introduced political uncertainty into long-term energy planning.

### Environmental Regulation and Compliance

South Korea's environmental regulatory framework includes:
- **Air Quality Regulation**: National standards for PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide (NOx), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Industrial facilities (refineries, petrochemical plants, steel mills) operate under emission permits and real-time monitoring via the Tele-Monitoring System (TMS).
- **Water Quality Standards**: River basin management under the Four Major Rivers Project (controversial), municipal wastewater treatment, industrial discharge controls targeting heavy metals and organic pollutants, and coastal protection policies.
- **Waste Management**: Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) for packaging and electronics; waste separation mandates; landfill restrictions; and growing emphasis on circular economy and plastic reduction.
- **Nature Preservation**: Designation of national parks (Jirisan, Seoraksan, etc.), wetland protection, marine conservation in coastal zones, and the DMZ Biosphere Reserve as an ecological corridor.

### International Commitments

South Korea has ratified major environmental accords including the Paris Agreement (2015), the Kyoto Protocol, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and regional agreements on air and marine pollution. The 2030 NDC targets 40% emissions reduction from 2018 levels, positioning South Korea among OECD leaders in stated climate ambition. However, implementation gaps persist, and South Korea remains dependent on energy-intensive exports, complicating decarbonization.

## Environmental Challenges

### Air Pollution and *Hwangsa*

South Korea experiences two distinct forms of severe air pollution: **hwangsa** (황사, "yellow dust" or "Asian dust"), seasonal mineral dust originating from Mongolia and China's Gobi Desert, transported by spring winds and carrying sand particles, heavy metals, and microorganisms; and **fine particulate matter** (초미세먼지, PM2.5), a year-round hazard peaking in winter months, driven by a combination of domestic coal combustion, industrial emissions, vehicle exhaust, and long-range transport from China.

South Korea's annual mean PM2.5 concentration in Seoul averaged approximately 23–26 μg/m³ in the early 2020s (the WHO guideline is 5 μg/m³ annual mean), placing it among the most polluted capital cities in the OECD. The government tightened the national PM2.5 standard from 25 μg/m³ to 15 μg/m³ annual mean (effective March 2018) and adopted emergency measures including temporary shutdown of older coal power plants during *"high-concentration days"* (비상저감조치). The government-commissioned **Joint Research on Air Quality** with China and Japan (2000s–present) has confirmed that 30–50% of South Korea's PM2.5 on typical days and up to 70% during severe pollution events originates from Chinese emissions sources—a politically sensitive finding that complicates bilateral environmental diplomacy.

Public health studies by the Korea Environment Institute and the Seoul National University School of Public Health have estimated that air pollution costs the South Korean economy approximately 20–30 trillion won annually in healthcare costs and productivity loss. Air quality monitoring is managed through **AirKorea** (에어코리아), a real-time national network of over 500 monitoring stations that provides hourly PM2.5, PM10, ozone, and nitrogen dioxide readings via mobile apps widely used by the population.

### Industrial Pollution Hotspots

Industrial cities and zones concentrate severe environmental challenges:
- **Ulsan**: Petrochemical and oil refining complex; high concentrations of volatile organic compounds and particulate matter; residents' health complaints and legal disputes over industrial liability.
- **Yeosu**: Petrochemical and shipbuilding hub; coastal water pollution; chemical spills and accidents (e.g., 2012 chemical plant leak).
- **Daesan**: Major petrochemical cluster; air and water quality impacts on neighboring regions.
- **Gumi**: Electronics manufacturing zone; heavy-metal contamination and waste management challenges.

### Water Quality and Aquatic Ecosystems

River systems, particularly the Nakdong (largest South Korean river), have faced pollution from agricultural runoff, industrial discharge, and urban sewage. The Four Major Rivers Project's dam construction altered ecological flow patterns and raised concerns about eutrophication and algal blooms. Coastal waters face overfishing pressure, illegal discharge, and impacts from aquaculture. The government has implemented river-basin management plans and wastewater treatment expansion, but ecological restoration remains incomplete.

### Plastic Waste and Circular Economy

South Korea generates approximately 88 kg of plastic waste per capita annually—one of the highest rates among OECD nations—driven by an extensive single-use packaging culture (convenience store bento boxes, food delivery, individual-serving packaging, e-commerce packaging). The country's high density of convenience stores (GS25, CU, 7-Eleven—numbering approximately 50,000+ outlets by 2022), food delivery apps (Baemin, Coupang Eats), and consumer electronics culture amplify packaging waste generation.

China's **National Sword** policy (January 2018), which banned imports of low-quality recyclables including mixed plastics, created a crisis in South Korea's recycling sector: domestic recycling operators temporarily refused to collect plastics, causing a collection breakdown in spring 2018. The government responded by tightening recycling labeling requirements, mandating **Extended Producer Responsibility** (생산자책임재활용제도, EPR) compliance for manufacturers and retailers, and banning single-use plastic bags at large supermarkets from 2019. A **deposit-refund system** for beverage containers (glass, PET, can) has operated since 1985 and achieves over 90% return rates for beverage containers—one of the highest in the world. South Korea's plastics and recycling policy has been noted as a partial success story: the paper and glass recycling rates exceed 85%, but plastic recycling rates (for contaminated mixed plastics) remain lower, around 50–60%. Waste export to Southeast Asia has grown as an alternative to domestic processing, drawing criticism from recipient countries and environmental NGOs about exploiting regulatory arbitrage.

### Demographic and Economic Constraints

Population aging and demographic decline have reduced domestic consumption pressure, yet South Korea's export-dependent model and global supply-chain positions in energy-intensive sectors (semiconductors, chemicals, shipbuilding, steel) tie decarbonization to competitive feasibility. Corporate investment in green technologies and renewable energy is growing, but the pace lags behind climate targets.

## Environmental Governance Structure

The **Ministry of Environment** (환경부) oversees environmental policy, standard-setting, permit issuance, and compliance enforcement. Key sub-agencies and research bodies include:
- **National Institute of Environmental Research** (국립환경과학원, NIER): Conducts scientific research on air and water quality, climate, and environmental health.
- **Korea Environment Institute** (한국환경연구원, KEI): Policy research think tank advising government on environmental strategy and cost-benefit analysis.
- **Korea Environmental Industry & Technology Institute** (KEITI): Manages green technology certification, eco-product labeling, and environmental industry support.
- **Korea Meteorological Administration** (KMA): Provides hwangsa and fine-dust forecasting systems used in public health alerts.

The **Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy** (산업통상자원부, MOTIE) handles energy policy, including power generation mix, industrial emissions allocations, and energy transition planning. MOTIE's **Korea Energy Agency** (KEA) manages energy efficiency standards and building codes.

**Local governments** (metropolitan cities, provinces) implement regional environmental plans, manage municipal waste and water systems, and enforce local emission ordinances. Seoul Metropolitan Government has established its own climate action plan targeting carbon neutrality by 2050 and has invested in urban air quality monitoring, electric bus fleets, and green roof programs.

Environmental **citizens and NGOs** play an active role: the **Korean Federation for Environmental Movement** (환경운동연합, KFEM, founded 1993) and **Greenpeace Korea** engage in advocacy, litigation, and public campaigns. Several landmark environmental lawsuits (including cases against the Four Major Rivers Project and coal plant expansions) have been brought by civil society coalitions.

### Corporate ESG and Chaebol Commitments

South Korea's major **chaebol** have adopted ESG reporting and carbon-reduction pledges under investor and regulatory pressure:
- **Samsung Electronics** committed to carbon neutrality in its global operations by 2050, with interim targets for its semiconductor fabs (which are highly energy-intensive, consuming approximately 24 TWh of electricity annually).
- **POSCO** (steel) committed to carbon neutrality by 2050 and announced a transition roadmap to hydrogen-reduction steelmaking (HyREX technology), which would replace coal-based coke in the blast furnace process.
- **Hyundai Motor Group** committed to carbon-neutral vehicle manufacturing by 2045 and has accelerated electrification across Hyundai, Kia, and Genesis brands.
- **SK Group** committed to reducing GHG emissions by 200 million tonnes CO₂ globally by 2030 and has invested in clean energy, hydrogen, and battery businesses.

## International Dimensions

South Korea participates in several bilateral and multilateral environmental frameworks:

- **Tripartite Environment Ministers' Meeting (TEMM)**: Annual forum with China and Japan addressing transboundary air pollution, marine debris, and biodiversity; launched in 1999.
- **Northeast Asian Subregional Programme for Environmental Cooperation (NEASPEC)**: UN-facilitated cooperation on desertification, marine pollution, and biodiversity.
- **Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)**: South Korea hosted the CBD COP12 in Pyeongchang (2014), during which the Pyeongchang Road Map on biodiversity targets was established.
- **Global Green Growth Institute (GGGI)**: South Korea founded and headquartered this international organization in Seoul (established 2012) to support green economic development in developing nations; it has grown to membership of over 45 countries.

## Future Directions

South Korea's environmental trajectory through the late 2020s will be shaped by several intersecting forces:

- **Energy mix and decarbonization**: Balancing nuclear expansion, accelerated offshore wind deployment (14.3 GW by 2030 target), and coal phase-down to meet the 2030 NDC of 40% emissions reduction from 2018 levels (requiring reduction from approximately 727 million tonnes in 2018 to 436 million tonnes by 2030).
- **Green hydrogen**: Investment in domestic green hydrogen production and import infrastructure (Australia, Middle East partnerships) as a decarbonization pathway for steel, petrochemicals, and transportation.
- **Carbon border adjustment**: South Korea's export-oriented industries face potential carbon border taxes from the EU (CBAM, phasing in 2026–2034) on steel, aluminum, and chemicals, creating economic incentives for faster industrial decarbonization.
- **Biodiversity and nature restoration**: Following CBD COP15 (Kunming-Montreal, December 2022) commitments to protect 30% of land and ocean by 2030 ("30x30"), South Korea must expand its protected-area network beyond the current ~16% of terrestrial area.
- **Urban heat and climate adaptation**: Seoul and other dense urban areas are investing in urban greening (expanding the Cheonggyecheon stream restoration model), cooling centers, and heat-alert systems as climate change increases heat wave frequency and intensity.
