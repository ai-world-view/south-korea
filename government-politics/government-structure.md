# Government Structure of South Korea

The **government of South Korea** is organized as a **presidential republic** with a unicameral legislature, an independent judiciary, and a complex system of checks and balances shaped by the nation's history of authoritarianism, military rule, and democratic transition. The current constitutional framework, the **Constitution of the Republic of Korea** (adopted 1987 and unamended since), establishes a separation of powers among the executive, legislative, and judicial branches. The president serves as both head of state and head of government, wielding significant executive authority, while the **National Assembly** (국회; Gukhoe) functions as the sole legislative body. A **Constitutional Court** sits above the regular judiciary to interpret constitutional matters and review the constitutionality of laws.

## Constitutional Framework

### Historical Constitutions

South Korea has **revised its constitution nine times** since its founding in 1948, a reflection of the nation's turbulent political history — far more than stable democracies typically require. Each major political transition generated a new or substantially revised constitution:

- **First Constitution (1948)** — Established a mixed presidential-parliamentary system under President **Syngman Rhee**, with a unicameral legislature and significant presidential power.
- **1960 Constitutional Amendment (after April Revolution)** — Shifted toward a parliamentary system with a figurehead president, following Rhee's overthrow.
- **1961 Constitutional Amendment (after Park Coup)** — Reversed the shift, restoring strong presidential authority for **Park Chung-hee**.
- **Yushin Constitution (1972)** — Introduced the authoritarian **Yushin System** (유신; "revitalization"), concentrating power in the president and allowing indefinite re-election.
- **Constitution of 1980 (Chun Doo-hwan era)** — Continued authoritarianism, imposing a single seven-year term.
- **Constitution of 1987** — The current framework, adopted following the June Democracy Movement; established a single five-year presidential term, democratic protections, and fundamental rights. This constitution remains substantially in force.

The sheer frequency of constitutional revision reflects a pattern: each military or authoritarian regime rewrote the document to legitimize itself, whereas stable democracies typically amend sparingly and preserve constitutional continuity.

## The Executive Branch

### The President

The **president** of South Korea serves as the chief executive and commander-in-chief of the armed forces. The presidency has been a position of enormous power, a legacy of authoritarian governance under Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan. The current constitution (1987) imposes two critical constraints unknown in South Korea's earlier republics:

- **Single Five-Year Term** — Presidents serve exactly five years and are **ineligible for re-election**. This was a deliberate democratic safeguard to prevent the indefinite incumbency seen under Park and Chun. The consequence is that outgoing presidents are often vulnerable to prosecution for corruption or abuse of office once they leave the executive protection that the presidency affords.
- **Term Limits** — No one may serve more than one term as president. This is enshrined in the constitution and is a firm rule (unlike in some presidencies worldwide where third terms have been attempted).

**Presidential Powers** include:
- Appointment of cabinet ministers and major bureaucratic officials (subject to legislative confirmation for the Prime Minister in some cases)
- Direction of foreign policy and diplomacy
- Command of the military and authority to declare emergencies
- Issuance of presidential decrees and executive orders (within constitutional bounds)
- Veto power over legislative bills (overridable by a two-thirds supermajority in the National Assembly)
- Pardon and clemency authority

Presidents have historically wielded these powers assertively. The five-year single term, however, introduces a "lame duck" dynamic: an outgoing president has limited political capital for major initiatives in their final year and faces the prospect of prosecution or disgrace once immunity expires.

### The Prime Minister and Cabinet

The **Prime Minister** (국무총리; Gungmuchongni) is the second-highest executive officer, appointed by the president and confirmed by the National Assembly. The Prime Minister heads the **State Council** (국무회의), which comprises cabinet ministers and is the principal deliberative body for policy. Unlike in parliamentary systems, the Prime Minister in South Korea does not control the legislature and cannot be removed by a vote of no-confidence; the prime minister serves at the pleasure of the president.

The **Cabinet** typically comprises 15–20 ministers, including:
- Minister of Interior and Safety
- Minister of Foreign Affairs
- Minister of National Defense
- Minister of Strategy and Finance
- Minister of Education
- Minister of Health and Welfare
- Minister of Trade, Industry and Energy
- Minister of Environment

Cabinet appointments reflect political balance and alliance-building. Changes to the cabinet are common during a presidency as political conditions shift or as particular ministers lose effectiveness.

## The Legislative Branch

### The National Assembly

The **National Assembly** (국회; Gukhoe) is the sole legislative body of South Korea, a unicameral (single-chamber) parliament. It comprises **300 members** (as of 2020 redistricting), elected for four-year terms. The assembly has sole authority to:

- Pass laws and constitutional amendments (by supermajority)
- Approve the national budget and government expenditures
- Ratify international treaties (by majority or supermajority depending on the treaty type)
- Conduct oversight and impeachment proceedings against the president and other officials
- Confirm or reject certain presidential appointments (Prime Minister, Constitutional Court justices, heads of major boards)

**Electoral System:**
The National Assembly uses a **mixed electoral system** combining single-member district representation and proportional representation:
- Approximately **254 seats** are filled by plurality vote in single-member constituencies (district representatives elected by simple majority).
- Approximately **46 seats** are allocated by proportional representation to parties that clear a **3% national vote threshold**, allowing smaller parties and ensuring some degree of proportional distribution.

This hybrid system tends to produce a dominant two-party or two-bloc system, as the single-member districts favor large parties. In practice, South Korea's legislature has been dominated by two major parties that have periodically renamed themselves and realigned, broadly representing conservative and progressive blocs.

**Legislative Terms and Sessions:**
- Four-year terms, with general elections typically held in April; local elections run on a separate four-year cycle rather than concurrently.
- Regular sessions convene twice yearly (spring and autumn). The President or a majority of Assembly members can convene special sessions.
- A quorum requires a majority of the 300 members for most business; constitutional amendments require a two-thirds supermajority (200+ votes).

### Party System

South Korea's party system has historically been characterized by **factionalism, personality-driven leadership, and regional polarization** rather than stable ideological platforms. Major parties have frequently merged, split, or rebranded:

- **Democratic Party** (and its predecessors and successors) — Generally center-left, progressive or centrist coalition; has included various parties such as the Democratic Party, United Democratic Party, and Minjoo Party.
- **Republican Party / Conservative Parties** (various names) — Generally center-right, conservative; has included the Democratic Republican Party (under Park), the Democratic Justice Party (under Chun), and the People Power Party (in recent years).

Party loyalty is often weak; individual politicians switch parties frequently. Electoral fragmentation has led to constant party reorganization, mergers, and rebranding. Unlike Western democracies with stable party systems, South Korean parties are often vehicles for individual politicians or regional blocs rather than coherent ideological coalitions.

**Regional Polarization:**
Politics in South Korea is heavily inflected by regional identity. The **Honam region** (Jeolla Provinces — southwest) has been a stronghold of progressive and democratic-movement parties, partly due to the region's history of being marginalized during Park Chung-hee's era and its role in pro-democracy activism. The **Yeongnam region** (Gyeongsang Provinces — southeast) has been a conservative stronghold, producing many military officers and supporting authoritarian figures like Park and Chun. Seoul and other urban areas are more politically volatile, swinging between progressives and conservatives.

## The Judicial Branch

### Court System

South Korea's judiciary comprises several tiers:

- **Constitutional Court** (헌법재판소) — A specialized nine-member court that reviews the constitutionality of laws, resolves disputes between state bodies, and hears impeachment cases. The nine justices are appointed as follows: **three** are appointed by the president, **three** are elected by the National Assembly, and **three** are designated by the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court — a tripartite structure designed to ensure no single branch dominates. Justices serve renewable six-year terms, with a mandatory retirement age of 70. Six of the nine justices must agree to invalidate a law or uphold a presidential impeachment, a supermajority requirement that sets a high bar for the most consequential decisions. The Constitutional Court is independent and wields significant power to strike down legislation or overturn presidential actions.
- **Supreme Court** (대법원) — The highest court of ordinary jurisdiction, headed by a Chief Justice. It handles appeals and has authority to interpret law.
- **Appellate Courts** (고등법원) — Six regional high courts hearing appeals.
- **District Courts** (지방법원) — First-instance courts at the district level, handling most civil and criminal cases.
- **Specialized Courts** — Including the Administrative Court (for disputes between citizens and the government), the Patent Court, and the Labor Court.

**Judicial Independence and Concerns:**
The judiciary has historically been subject to political pressure from powerful presidents and has been used as a tool of state repression during authoritarian eras. Judges were purged or dismissed after the 1961 coup and again after 1979. Since the 1987 democratic transition, formal judicial independence has been strengthened — judges cannot be removed except through impeachment or retirement, and the Constitutional Court has struck down numerous laws and presidential actions. However, concerns about political influence persist, and the appointment process for senior judges remains contested between political factions.

## Checks and Balances

South Korea's governmental system includes several mechanisms of mutual oversight:

1. **Presidential Veto and Legislative Override** — The president can veto bills; the National Assembly can override with a two-thirds supermajority.
2. **Impeachment** — The National Assembly can impeach the president, prime minister, or Constitutional Court justices. An impeachment is tried by the Constitutional Court. In 2004, the Constitutional Court acquitted President **Roh Moo-hyun** of impeachment charges brought by a hostile legislature; in 2017, it convicted President **Park Geun-hye** and upheld her removal.
3. **Confirmation Powers** — The National Assembly confirms the Prime Minister and other key officials, giving it leverage over executive appointments.
4. **Constitutional Review** — The Constitutional Court can strike down laws or presidential decrees as unconstitutional, a powerful check on both branches.
5. **Budget Authority** — The legislature must approve the annual budget; withholding budgetary approval can constrain the president's agenda.

## Decentralization and Local Government

### Provincial and Local Structure

South Korea is divided into **17 regional subdivisions**: **1 special city** (Seoul), **6 metropolitan cities** (Busan, Daegu, Daejeon, Gwangju, Incheon, Ulsan), **1 special self-governing city** (Sejong, the administrative capital), and **9 provinces** (도; do), two of which — Jeju and Gangwon — hold special self-governing status. Each has an elected **governor** (or mayor) and an elected **provincial (or metropolitan) assembly**. Below the provincial level, cities and counties have their own elected mayors and councils.

**Local Autonomy:**
Since the 1991 restoration of local elections (after a 30-year hiatus during authoritarian rule), local governments have gained power in education, welfare, public works, and other areas. However, the central government in Seoul retains control of major fiscal policy, defense, foreign affairs, and appointments of many senior bureaucrats. Korea's system is **centralized relative to federal systems** but has been moving toward greater devolution since democratization.

## Electoral System and Democratic Participation

### Presidential Elections

Presidential elections occur every five years. Candidates must be nominated by registered political parties; independent candidates require signatures from a large number of voters. The president is elected by **simple plurality** (not a runoff), so the candidate with the most votes wins, regardless of whether they achieve an absolute majority.

### National Assembly Elections

Elections to the National Assembly occur every four years. The mixed electoral system (single-member districts plus proportional representation) is designed to encourage broad coalition-building, though large parties have typically dominated.

### Voter Participation

Voter turnout in South Korean elections has historically been high — typically **60–70%** in presidential elections and **50–60%** in legislative elections — reflecting strong civic engagement. However, turnout has been declining among younger voters, a trend common across democracies.

## Administrative Bureaucracy

### Civil Service

South Korea has a large, hierarchical civil service, staffed by career bureaucrats. Entry is through competitive examination, and advancement is based on seniority and merit. Bureaucrats are expected to be politically neutral, though in practice senior appointments (ministers, vice-ministers, heads of major agencies) are often filled with political allies of the sitting president.

The presidential executive office — historically the **Blue House** (Cheong Wa Dae) in Jongno District — was relocated in 2022 under President Yoon Suk-yeol to a compound in **Yongsan District**, Seoul; it is staffed by presidential advisors and aides who work closely with the president on daily governance.

### Central Ministries

The central government operates through a **State Council** (국무회의) chaired by the Prime Minister, comprising cabinet ministers who oversee major functional areas: defense, foreign affairs, justice, finance, education, health, environment, commerce, and others.

### The Audit and Inspection Board

The **Board of Audit and Inspection** (감사원; BAI) is a constitutional organ independent of the three branches, reporting directly to the president but endowed with constitutional autonomy. Established by the 1948 Constitution and strengthened by subsequent revisions, the BAI:
- Audits the accounts of all central and local government agencies, state-owned enterprises, and organizations receiving state funds
- Investigates the legality and effectiveness of government administration
- Can refer cases of malfeasance or corruption to prosecutors
- Issues annual reports to the National Assembly

The BAI's independence is formally guaranteed by the Constitution, but in practice its oversight effectiveness depends heavily on political conditions. It has been criticized for selectively targeting opponents of the sitting president and for failing to investigate allies. The Chief of the BAI is appointed by the president and confirmed by the National Assembly.

## Democratic Safeguards and Ongoing Debates

### Protections Against Authoritarianism

The 1987 Constitution introduced several safeguards against a return to military dictatorship or authoritarian rule:

- **Single five-year presidential term** — Prevents indefinite reelection and the accumulation of unchecked power.
- **Guaranteed civil liberties and human rights** — Includes freedom of speech, assembly, and press; protection against torture and arbitrary detention.
- **Independent Constitutional Court** — Can strike down unconstitutional laws or executive actions.
- **Local autonomy and decentralization** — Reduces concentration of power in the executive.
- **Parliamentary oversight** — The National Assembly retains budget authority, confirmation powers, and impeachment authority.

### Persistent Critiques

Despite these safeguards, South Korean democracy remains contested:

1. **Presidential Accountability** — Outgoing presidents have frequently faced corruption investigations or prosecution, leading some to argue that the single-term limit creates perverse incentives (a president with nothing to lose in their final year) and that the system encourages vindictive prosecution of defeated opponents.
2. **Party Instability and Regional Division** — Chronic party reorganization and regional polarization (Honam vs. Yeongnam) reduce the coherence of national political debate.
3. **Judicial and Prosecutorial Independence** — The independence of judges and prosecutors remains questioned, with accusations that these institutions are used as political tools by whichever faction holds power.
4. **National Security and Civil Liberties Tension** — Strong national security concerns (related to North Korea) have historically justified broad surveillance and restrictions on civil liberties (e.g., the National Security Act). Debates continue over how to balance security and freedom.
5. **Gender Representation and Minority Rights** — Women and religious/ethnic minorities remain underrepresented in political leadership; LGBTQ+ rights remain contentious.

## Constitutional Stress Test: The 2024 Martial Law Crisis

The resilience of South Korea's constitutional framework was dramatically tested in **December 2024**, when President **Yoon Suk-yeol** declared **martial law** on the night of **December 3, 2024** — the first martial law declaration since 1980. Citing what he called "anti-state forces" in the National Assembly that were paralyzing governance, Yoon invoked emergency powers and dispatched troops and special forces to surround the National Assembly building in Yeouido, Seoul.

The constitutional system responded with remarkable speed:

1. **National Assembly Override** — The constitution requires a majority of National Assembly members to vote to lift martial law. Despite soldiers blocking entrances to the assembly building, 190 members of the 300-member assembly gathered within the chamber overnight and voted **190–0** to revoke the martial law declaration at approximately **1:00 a.m. on December 4, 2024**. Yoon, legally bound by the constitution's martial law provisions, was compelled to withdraw the declaration within approximately six hours.

2. **Presidential Impeachment** — The National Assembly voted to impeach Yoon on **December 14, 2024**, with 204 of 300 members supporting impeachment — well above the two-thirds threshold required. The vote included a significant number of members from Yoon's own **People Power Party (PPP)**, reflecting the depth of the constitutional crisis. Yoon's presidential powers were suspended pending the Constitutional Court's review.

3. **Constitutional Court Review** — The Constitutional Court took up the impeachment case. On **April 4, 2025**, the Constitutional Court issued a unanimous **8–0 decision** upholding the impeachment and permanently removing Yoon from the presidency — the second time in South Korea's democratic history that a sitting president had been removed through impeachment (after Park Geun-hye in 2017).

The 2024 martial law crisis demonstrated several features of South Korea's constitutional design working as intended: the assembly's martial law veto operated within hours, the impeachment process functioned as designed, and the Constitutional Court rendered a clear verdict. It also raised questions about presidential accountability, the prosecution of former presidents (Yoon faced criminal charges for insurrection), and the stability of a political system in which the executive and legislative branches are controlled by opposing parties with no built-in mechanism for resolving deadlock short of constitutional confrontation.

## Relationship with North Korea and Security

South Korea's government structure is shaped profoundly by security concerns related to the ongoing division with North Korea and the armistice agreement of 1953. The **Security, Unification, and Foreign Affairs Committee** of the National Assembly maintains oversight of inter-Korean relations and defense policy. The **Joint Chiefs of Staff** and the Ministry of National Defense coordinate defense under ultimate presidential command, with residual American military presence in South Korea (approximately 28,000 U.S. troops) as of 2024.

The **National Security Law** (국가보안법), inherited from the authoritarian era and amended but not repealed, criminalizes "anti-state" activities and has been invoked to suppress dissent and criticism of North Korea. Its constitutional validity and necessity remain subjects of heated debate.
