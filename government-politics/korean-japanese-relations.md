# Korean-Japanese Relations

**Korean-Japanese relations** comprise one of East Asia's most historically fraught, economically interdependent, and strategically significant bilateral relationships. South Korea and Japan share geographic proximity across the Korea Strait (approximately 200 km at its narrowest point), deep historical entanglement, substantial cultural exchange, massive trade flows, and a shared U.S.-anchored security alliance. Yet the relationship is persistently shadowed by historical grievances stemming from the Japanese colonial occupation (1910–1945), unresolved war-reparations and responsibility questions, territorial disputes, and conflicting narratives about shared history. Contemporary relations oscillate between robust economic cooperation and dramatic diplomatic crises driven by historical, nationalist, and domestic political factors.

## Historical Context: From Kingdom Contact to Colonial Subjugation

Korean-Japanese contact dates to ancient times, with Goryeo (918–1392) and Joseon (1392–1910) kingdoms maintaining intermittent diplomatic and trade relations, punctuated by the devastating **Imjin War** (1592–1598), when Japanese warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi's two invasions (1592 and 1597–1598) killed an estimated 100,000–200,000 Koreans and displaced millions. Japanese forces destroyed hundreds of Buddhist temples, ransacked Confucian academies, and carried off thousands of Korean artisans—particularly ceramic potters—whose skills transformed Japanese ceramic arts. The Korean admiral Yi Sun-sin (1545–1598) achieved legendary status for defeating Japanese naval forces using armored "turtle ships" (geobukseon), and his legacy as the supreme symbol of Korean national resistance is embedded in public memory (his statue occupies the central position on Sejong-daero in Seoul). The Imjin War trauma provided an emotional substrate for Korean responses to later Japanese colonialism and continues to be invoked in contemporary debates about bilateral trust. The Joseon recovery period (late 1600s–1800s) saw limited contact as Korea pursued a "Hermit Kingdom" isolationist policy.

The modern relationship began with forced opening: Japan's **Meiji Restoration** (1868) transformed Japan into a modernizing imperial power, while Korea faced domestic instability and foreign pressure. The **Ganghwa Treaty** (1876), imposed by Japan on Korea, established unequal trade relations and opened Korean ports. A series of treaties and military interventions followed, including:

- **Eulsa Treaty** (1905) — Japan established a "protectorate" over Korea, reducing Korean sovereignty to a nominal figurehead and establishing Japanese control over foreign policy.
- **Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty** (1910) — Korea's formal colonization by Japan, marking the beginning of 35 years of colonial rule under the Government-General of Korea (Japanese: Chōsen Sōtokufu; Korean: Joseon Chongdokbu).

## The Colonial Period and Its Legacies (1910–1945)

The Japanese colonial period left profound and contested legacies that continue to shape Korean-Japanese relations:

**Economic Transformation** — Japan modernized Korea's infrastructure (railways, roads, ports), developed industrial capacity (particularly in the north), and expanded agricultural production through large-scale irrigation. However, this came at the cost of Korean economic subjugation: Japanese companies and settlers monopolized the most profitable sectors, colonial taxation was extractive, and Korea became a supplier of raw materials and labor rather than an autonomous economy.

**Cultural Suppression** — Japanese authorities pursued "assimilation" (naisen ittai, "Japan and Korea as one body") policies, most notoriously the wartime Sōshi-kaimei campaign that pressured Koreans to adopt Japanese-style surnames:
- Korean-language education was restricted in favor of Japanese; Korean newspapers and publications were censored or banned.
- State Shinto worship was imposed, requiring reverence to the Japanese emperor and attendance at Shinto shrines.
- Korean cultural practices (traditional arts, music, dress) were discouraged or prohibited.
- However, these policies also catalyzed anti-colonial cultural nationalism; Korean intellectuals preserved and developed Korean language, history, and identity through underground networks.

**Military Mobilization** — During World War II, Japan mobilized Korean human resources:
- Approximately 200,000 Koreans were conscripted as military personnel, with thousands dying in combat across Asia and the Pacific.
- An estimated 200,000+ Korean women were coerced into the "comfort women" system (sexual slavery for Japanese military personnel), one of the war's most notorious atrocities.
- Tens of thousands of Koreans were conscripted as forced laborers in Japanese mines, factories, and construction projects.

**Anti-Colonial Resistance** — Koreans resisted throughout the period:
- The **March 1st Independence Movement** (1919) saw nationwide protests suppressed violently, killing thousands.
- The Korean Provisional Government-in-Exile (based in Shanghai) conducted armed and diplomatic resistance from 1919 onward.
- Communist and nationalist guerrilla movements operated in Manchuria and within Korea.
- Cultural and intellectual resistance through underground publishing and education preserved Korean identity.

## Postwar Relations: From Division to Normalization (1945–1965)

After Japan's 1945 surrender and Korea's liberation, South Korea initially had minimal formal relations with Japan. The Korean War (1950–1953) complicated the picture: Japan served as a U.S. logistics base and supply hub, benefiting economically from American procurement, while South Korea fought for survival. The two countries remained technically in a state of hostility, with no diplomatic relations.

The **Treaty on Basic Relations between Japan and the Republic of Korea** (1965) normalized bilateral relations, but on terms that have remained contentious. Key provisions included:

- **Diplomatic and economic normalization** — Establishment of formal relations and mutual recognition.
- **$800 million Japanese economic aid package** — Structured as "economic cooperation" rather than explicit war reparations, a linguistic choice that avoided acknowledging Japanese responsibility for colonial exploitation and wartime damage.
- **Territorial clause** — Recognition of South Korea as the legitimate government of the peninsula, and agreement to resolve disputes (the Liancourt Rocks/Dokdo issue) through international legal mechanisms.

The 1965 normalization agreement was controversial in South Korea, with opposition parties and civil society arguing that it granted insufficient reparations and allowed Japan to evade historical accountability. The Park Chung-hee government, however, prioritized rapid economic development and saw Japanese capital, technology transfer, and market access as essential to industrialization.

## Cold War Era: Economic Interdependence, Historical Distance (1965–1990s)

Despite diplomatic normalization, Korean-Japanese relations remained cool and formal during the Cold War. However, economic ties deepened dramatically:

**Trade and Investment** — Japan became South Korea's primary source of capital, technology, and intermediate goods. Japanese companies invested heavily in South Korean manufacturing, particularly in semiconductors, petrochemicals, and automotive production. By the 1980s, Japan was South Korea's second-largest trading partner (after the United States) and a crucial source of manufacturing expertise.

**Cultural Exchange** — Limited and controlled, particularly during the authoritarian Park and Chun eras, when Korean governments restricted Japanese cultural imports (music, film, publications) as a nationalist policy, though illegal imports and underground interest persisted.

**Comfort Women and Historical Justice Issues** — The comfort women system remained largely hidden from public discourse in both countries until the 1990s. In 1992, newly declassified Japanese military documents confirmed the system's systematic nature, triggering the **Wednesday Demonstrations** (begun in January 1992 outside the Japanese Embassy in Seoul), which continue to the present day, demanding Japanese government acknowledgment and reparations.

**Territorial Disputes** — The **Dokdo/Liancourt Rocks** dispute remained dormant during the Cold War but resurfaced periodically. The two small volcanic islands in the Korea Strait are claimed by both countries; South Korea occupies and administers them, but Japan contests South Korean sovereignty and has occasionally sent coast guard vessels in response to South Korean activities.

## Contemporary Relations: Oscillation Between Cooperation and Crisis (2000s–Present)

Post-Cold War Korean-Japanese relations have experienced dramatic swings between economic pragmatism and historical nationalism:

**Hallyu and Cultural Exchange** — Beginning in the early 2000s, Korean popular culture (K-dramas, K-pop, K-beauty) achieved massive popularity in Japan, reversing decades of cultural one-sidedness. This created new people-to-people connections, though also competitive anxieties in both countries about cultural influence.

**Apology and Acknowledgment Cycles** — Japanese Prime Ministers have issued varying degrees of apology:
- **1993 Kono Statement** — Chief Cabinet Secretary Yohei Kono acknowledged coercion in the comfort women system.
- **1995 Murayama Statement** — Prime Minister Tomiichi Murayama offered a formal apology for colonial rule and wartime aggression, establishing a diplomatic baseline.
- However, subsequent Japanese leaders have diluted or distanced themselves from these statements, generating recurring diplomatic incidents.

**Textbook and Historical Narrative Conflicts** — Disputes over how WWII and colonial history are taught in schools periodically erupt. Japanese history textbooks are criticized in South Korea for minimizing colonial atrocities, comfort women, and forced labor. Conversely, Korean textbooks are sometimes criticized in Japan for one-sided narratives.

**Territorial and Maritime Disputes** — Beyond Dokdo:
- The **EEZ (Exclusive Economic Zone) delimitation** in the Korea Strait remains contentious, with overlapping claims.
- Japanese whaling and fishing practices in shared waters generate periodic friction.
- Island sovereignty and naming conventions (e.g., Korean names vs. Japanese names for shared geographic features) remain sensitive.

**GSOMIA and Military Intelligence Sharing** — The General Security of Military Information Agreement (GSOMIA) between South Korea and Japan, signed in November 2016, formalized intelligence-sharing on North Korea's military activities. The agreement was nearly terminated by the Moon Jae-in government in August 2019 as diplomatic retaliation for Japan's export restrictions, generating alarm in Washington about alliance cohesion. South Korea ultimately maintained GSOMIA in November 2019 but with "conditional" status, a diplomatic awkwardness that persisted until the 2023 normalization restored normal functioning.

**Trade Volumes and Economic Interdependence** — Bilateral trade between South Korea and Japan reached approximately $84 billion in 2022, making Japan South Korea's third-largest trading partner (after China and the United States). South Korea consistently runs a trade deficit with Japan—approximately $24 billion in 2022—reflecting Japanese superiority in precision machinery, chemicals, and advanced materials. Japan's export controls on semiconductor materials in 2019 exposed South Korea's vulnerability to disruption of this supply relationship, accelerating South Korean government and private investment in domestic production of fluorinated polyimide, EUV photoresists, and high-purity hydrogen fluoride.

**High-Profile Diplomatic Incidents** (2010s–2020s):

- **2012 Dokdo visit by South Korean President Lee Myung-bak** — The president's visit to Dokdo was seen as nationalist provocation, triggering strong Japanese protests.

- **2015 Comfort Women Agreement** — The Japanese and South Korean governments negotiated a "final and irreversible" settlement on the comfort women issue, with Japan expressing "deep remorse and a heartfelt apology" and pledging 1 billion yen (~$8.3 million) to a South Korean healing fund. However, South Korean civil society, particularly comfort women survivors and their advocates, rejected the agreement as inadequate, leading to its partial invalidation by the South Korean government in 2018.

- **2019 Trade War** — Japan imposed export restrictions on semiconductor manufacturing materials (fluorinated polyimide, photoresists, hydrogen fluoride) crucial to South Korea's semiconductor industry, citing national security concerns. The move was widely interpreted as political retaliation over historical and territorial disputes, triggering South Korean boycotts of Japanese goods and escalated diplomatic tension. The restrictions were gradually lifted by 2021.

- **2021 Forced Labor Rulings** — South Korean courts ruled that Japanese companies must compensate victims of forced labor during colonial rule. Japan rejected the rulings as violations of the 1965 agreement, which it argued settled all historical claims. This re-opened debates about the adequacy of the 1965 normalization settlement.

- **Comfort Women Statue Disputes** — Controversy over statues erected in South Korea (including one near the Japanese Embassy in Seoul) and in the United States (particularly in California with large Korean diaspora populations) to memorialize comfort women victims. Japan has repeatedly protested these as inflammatory and demanded their removal.

## Contemporary Strategic Alignment and Divergence

Despite historical tensions, South Korea and Japan share significant strategic interests:

**U.S. Alliance** — Both countries are U.S. allies with security treaties (the ROK-U.S. Mutual Defense Treaty and the Japan-U.S. Security Treaty). The U.S. has encouraged trilateral coordination, though this has been complicated by Korean-Japanese historical tensions.

**China and North Korea** — Both countries face a rising China and a nuclear-armed North Korea, creating mutual security concerns. Trilateral coordination on North Korea policy and Chinese strategic challenges is periodically attempted but limited by bilateral tension.

**Technology and Supply Chain** — Japan remains a crucial supplier of advanced materials, machinery, and components to South Korean semiconductor, automotive, and chemical industries. Both countries are positioning themselves as alternatives to China in global supply chains, though this creates competitive pressures.

**Demographic and Economic Decline** — Both countries face aging populations, shrinking workforces, and slowing growth, creating opportunities for cooperation on technology, workforce solutions, and labor migration, though immigration remains politically sensitive in both societies.

## Persistent Grievances and Obstacles

**Comfort Women and Forced Labor** — Survivor testimonies, civil society activism, and ongoing court cases keep historical justice issues alive. South Koreans view Japanese government reluctance to acknowledge and compensate as evidence of unrepented imperialism; Japanese governments argue that the 1965 treaty settled all claims and that continuing demands undermine bilateral relations.

**Textbooks and Revisionism** — South Koreans perceive Japanese textbooks as whitewashing colonialism and WWII atrocities, particularly under conservative Japanese governments. Conversely, some Japanese critics argue that South Korean textbooks present exaggerated or one-sided narratives.

**Dokdo/Territorial Pride** — The Dokdo dispute is not primarily about natural resources or strategic value but about national sovereignty and dignity. For Koreans, Dokdo symbolizes resistance to Japanese expansionism; for Japanese nationalists, it represents the legitimacy of their historical territorial claims. Neither side is willing to compromise, making the dispute intractable through normal diplomacy.

**Historiography and Nationalism** — Both countries' educational systems emphasize national narratives that can marginalize the other's perspective. Korean nationalism incorporates anti-Japanese resistance as a core element of national identity (particularly the independence movement and comfort women issues); Japanese nationalism sometimes frames the colonial period as modernizing and beneficial. These narratives collide when historical education is debated.

**Public Opinion and Generational Factors** — Older Koreans who experienced colonialism or its immediate aftermath tend to harbor stronger historical grievances; younger generations may be less emotionally invested in historical disputes but are also shaped by nationalist education. Conversely, younger Japanese often have limited knowledge of colonialism and WWII details, making historical reconciliation more challenging.

## Prospects and Ongoing Challenges

Korean-Japanese relations remain characterized by **structural stability undermined by periodic crises**:

- **Economic ties** create incentives for cooperation and stability.
- **Security alliance with the U.S.** constrains the degree of escalation either country can risk.
- **Historical grievances and nationalist politics** periodically override pragmatic interests, triggering diplomatic incidents.
- **Demographic and geopolitical shifts** (China's rise, North Korea's nuclear weapons, U.S. strategic rebalancing) may force closer cooperation, but mutual trust remains limited.

Potential paths forward include:

- **Truth commissions and historical acknowledgment** — Models from other post-conflict regions (South Africa, Germany, Rwanda) suggest that structured acknowledgment of historical wrongs, even without comprehensive compensation, can reduce grievance intensity.

- **Educational exchange and curriculum harmonization** — Jointly developed history textbooks and educational exchange programs could expose younger generations to more nuanced, bilateral perspectives on shared history.

- **Binding arbitration on territorial disputes** — International legal mechanisms (similar to the International Court of Justice processes used by other states) could settle the Dokdo dispute, though both countries' nationalist constituencies make this politically difficult.

- **Generational change** — As direct victims and perpetrators age out of the political system, newer generations may be more willing to move beyond historical grievances, though nationalist education policies could perpetuate conflict.

### The 2023 Bilateral Reset

A significant diplomatic shift occurred in 2023 under South Korean President Yoon Suk-yeol and Japanese Prime Minister Kishida Fumio. In March 2023, Yoon visited Tokyo for the first summit between the two leaders since 2019, proposing a **"third-party payment" solution** to the forced-labor compensation dispute: a South Korean public foundation (the Foundation for Victims of Forced Labor) would compensate court-ordered victims using contributions from South Korean companies that benefited from the 1965 normalization treaty, effectively removing the immediate legal obligation from Japanese companies. The South Korean government framed this as breaking the diplomatic "deadlock" and prioritizing forward-looking relations; critics in South Korea—including the main opposition Democratic Party and some victim families—condemned it as capitulation, arguing Japan had conceded nothing.

In May 2023, Kishida made a reciprocal visit to Seoul, and the two leaders agreed to restore "shuttle diplomacy" — regular reciprocal summit meetings that had been suspended since 2011 due to bilateral tensions. Export restrictions imposed in 2019 were formally removed by July 2023, and Japan restored South Korea to its "whitelist" of trusted trading partners.

The most significant milestone was the **Camp David Trilateral Summit** in August 2023, when U.S. President Biden hosted Yoon and Kishida at Camp David for a leaders' meeting—the first formal U.S.-ROK-Japan trilateral summit. The three countries committed to regular trilateral consultations, enhanced information sharing on North Korea, joint military exercises, and coordination on technology supply chains. The summit institutionalized trilateral security cooperation that had previously been informal and intermittent, representing a qualitative shift in the U.S.-Asia alliance architecture.

In 2023, the **Fukushima treated water ocean release** controversy added a new bilateral friction point: Japan's discharge of ALPS-treated water from the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant, beginning August 2023, drew strong criticism in South Korea from opposition parties and civil society, who demanded halting imports of Japanese seafood. The Yoon government, in contrast, supported Japan's position that the water met international safety standards, creating a domestic political divide over how to balance scientific assessment and nationalist sentiment in the bilateral relationship.

As of 2025–2026, Korean-Japanese relations are in a cautiously improved phase relative to 2017–2022 lows, with restored trade whitelist status, active shuttle diplomacy, and deeper trilateral security coordination. However, underlying historical grievances—comfort women statues, forced labor rulings, textbook disputes, and Dokdo—remain unresolved and periodically resurface in diplomatic friction. Trade continues, cultural exchange has deepened, and security coordination has structurally strengthened, but historical reconciliation remains incomplete.
