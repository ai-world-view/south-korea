# December 2024 Martial Law Crisis and Constitutional Crisis

The **December 2024 Martial Law Crisis** represents the most severe constitutional and
political crisis in South Korea since the 1987 democratic transition. On December 3, 2024,
President Yoon Suk-yeol declared emergency martial law, suspending constitutional liberties
and imposing military authority over the National Assembly, judiciary, and media. The
decree—rescinded within six hours after a dramatic defiance vote by the National Assembly—
triggered a national constitutional confrontation, mass street protests, a parliamentary
revolt that included members of Yoon's own party, and an impeachment and Constitutional
Court process that culminated in Yoon's removal from office in April 2025. Yoon was
subsequently arrested on charges of insurrection, making him the first sitting or former
South Korean president to face criminal detention since Chun Doo-hwan.

## Background and Context

### Yoon Suk-yeol's Presidency (2022–2024)

Yoon Suk-yeol (윤석열, born 1960) was elected South Korea's twentieth president on
March 9, 2022, by a margin of only **0.73 percentage points** (24.33 million votes, or
48.56%, versus 24.09 million for Democratic Party candidate Lee Jae-myung). This was the
narrowest presidential election margin in South Korean history, and it reflected a deeply
polarised electorate.

Yoon, a career prosecutor who served as Prosecutor General (2019–2021) under President
Moon Jae-in before breaking publicly with him, ran on a conservative, anti-corruption
platform. His People Power Party (PPP, 국민의힘) was the minority party in the 300-member
National Assembly throughout his term: in the 21st Assembly (elected April 2020) the
Democratic Party held a commanding majority, leaving the PPP with roughly 115 seats. The
subsequent **April 2024 general election** produced an even more adverse legislative
environment: the opposition Democratic Party and allied parties won **192 of 300 seats**,
a near-supermajority that made it arithmetically possible to impeach judges, cabinet
ministers, and even the president with relative ease.

### Legislative Gridlock and Political Conflicts

The Yoon administration's 32-month presidency was marked by sustained confrontation
with the opposition-controlled Assembly:

- The DP-controlled Assembly impeached a succession of senior officials appointed or
  backed by Yoon — including cabinet ministers, prosecutors, and the head of the
  broadcasting regulator — moves Yoon condemned as unconstitutional overreach by the
  legislature into executive functions.
- The Assembly repeatedly rejected or watered down Yoon's budget proposals, including
  large-scale cuts to social welfare and public-broadcasting funding that the opposition
  labelled politically motivated.
- Investigations into **First Lady Kim Keon-hee** on suspicion of stock-market
  manipulation (the *Deutsch Motors* share price scheme, allegedly involving false
  recommendations that inflated the stock in 2009–2010) and acceptance of luxury goods
  (a Dior handbag from a pastor-activist in 2022, caught on hidden camera) kept the
  presidency under sustained reputational pressure. The DP introduced a special
  prosecutor bill targeting Kim Keon-hee; Yoon vetoed it.
- Attempts to pass a special prosecutor law investigating Yoon's administration's
  alleged interference in the 2022 election and related scandals were blocked by
  presidential veto and PPP parliamentary manoeuvres.

### Security and Geopolitical Posture

Yoon maintained a hardline posture toward North Korea, withdrawing from inter-Korean
engagement (the Moon Jae-in era's inter-Korean liaison office in Kaesong had already been
blown up by North Korea in 2020), reinforcing the US-South Korea military alliance
(including the Washington Declaration on extended deterrence, April 2023), and pursuing
trilateral security cooperation with the United States and Japan (the Camp David summit,
August 2023, the first standalone US–Japan–South Korea trilateral summit). North Korea
during this period conducted multiple ICBM tests (including the Hwasong-17 in March 2022,
the Hwasong-18 solid-fuel ICBM in April 2023) and continued artillery provocations near
the Northern Limit Line.

## The Martial Law Declaration (December 3–4, 2024)

### The Announcement

At **10:23 PM on December 3, 2024**, President Yoon appeared in a brief televised
address from the presidential office and declared **emergency martial law** (비상계엄,
*bisang gyeeom*) under **Article 77 of the Constitution**. His stated justifications were:

1. Threats from **"anti-state forces"** and North Korean sympathisers within South Korea.
2. The National Assembly's alleged obstruction of governmental functions through
   repeated budget cuts and impeachments of executive appointees.
3. A claimed collapse of constitutional order requiring emergency executive intervention.

Legal analysts immediately noted that Article 77 authorises martial law only in cases of
war, armed conflict, or a state of national emergency analogous to armed conflict—
conditions that critics argued were self-evidently absent.

### Military Deployment

Within minutes of the announcement, approximately **800 soldiers and special-forces
personnel** from the Army's Special Warfare Command were deployed:

- To the **National Assembly building** (Yeouido, Seoul), where troops cordoned off
  entrances and attempted to prevent legislators from entering.
- To the **National Election Commission** (Gwacheon), where soldiers entered the building,
  citing Yoon's claim of electoral fraud systems.
- To the headquarters of the **KBS, MBC, and YTN** broadcasting stations, where soldiers
  attempted to halt anti-government programming.
- To the **Gyeonggi Provincial Police Agency** and other law-enforcement offices.

The operation was directed by Defence Minister **Kim Yong-hyun** (김용현), who had
reportedly drafted the martial law plan with Yoon. Special Warfare Command units under
commander **Kwak Jong-geun** (곽종근) were deployed to the National Assembly, while former
Defense Intelligence Command commander **Noh Sang-won** (노상원) was subsequently implicated
as a key civilian planner of the operation.

### The Assembly's Defiance

The National Assembly, which under Article 77(5) of the Constitution has the power to
lift martial law by a simple majority vote, convened an emergency session with remarkable
speed. The opposition Democratic Party's secretary-general circulated emergency texts,
and members raced to the National Assembly compound.

Military personnel blocked the main gates, but at least **190 of the 300 Assembly
members** scaled fences or entered through side gates. At **1:00 AM on December 4**, the
Assembly voted **190 to 0** (with zero opposing votes and no abstentions—PPP members
either absent or abstaining rather than voting against) to **demand the lifting of
martial law**. Under Article 77(5), the president is required to lift martial law
immediately upon receiving such a resolution.

**Yoon complied**: at approximately **4:30 AM on December 4**, he appeared on television
to announce the lifting of martial law, citing the Assembly's resolution and "the advice
of cabinet members." The declaration had lasted approximately **six hours**.

### Immediate Political Fallout

The rapid constitutional resolution—unprecedented in Korean or comparative
democratic history—demonstrated the robustness of the Assembly's check on the
executive, but the declaration itself produced an immediate political crisis:

- **PPP leader Han Dong-hoon** (한동훈), who had been Yoon's close political ally
  and justice minister, publicly condemned the martial law declaration within hours,
  demanding Yoon's resignation and warning of political catastrophe for the party.
- Mass protests erupted in central Seoul on the night of December 3–4 and swelled over
  subsequent days, drawing hundreds of thousands of demonstrators to Gwanghwamun Square.
- **International condemnation** was swift: the US State Department expressed "serious
  concern," the European Union called the declaration "alarming," and Japan and other
  allies urged restraint and adherence to constitutional processes.

## Impeachment Proceedings

### First Impeachment Vote (December 7, 2024)

The Democratic Party introduced an impeachment motion on December 4. The first vote,
held on December 7, failed: the PPP leadership imposed a boycott, and with fewer than
the required two-thirds of members (200 of 300) present, the vote could not reach a
quorum and was declared void without a valid tally.

Han Dong-hoon's public call for Yoon to resign and the PPP's internal fracture
deepened over the following week as additional details emerged about the military
deployment plan. A **warrant for the arrest of Defence Minister Kim Yong-hyun** was
issued on December 6; Kim attempted suicide in custody on December 11 and was
hospitalised.

### Second Impeachment Vote (December 14, 2024)

On **December 14, 2024**, a second impeachment vote succeeded. With PPP members
split and a significant group of **12 PPP defectors** voting in favour (bringing the
total to **204 votes in favour**, well above the 200 threshold), the National Assembly
voted to impeach Yoon Suk-yeol:

- **204** in favour (all 192 DP-coalition members plus 12 PPP defectors)
- **85** against (remaining PPP members)
- **3** abstentions
- **8** blank votes

Under Article 65 of the Constitution and the Constitutional Court Act, Yoon's
presidential powers were **suspended immediately** upon the National Assembly's
impeachment resolution. **Prime Minister Han Duck-soo** (한덕수) assumed the
acting presidential role on December 14.

## Constitutional Court Proceedings and Ruling

### Hearings (December 2024–April 2025)

The Constitutional Court (헌법재판소), seated in Seoul with a complement of nine
justices (one seat was vacant at the time of impeachment), accepted the case and
conducted a five-month review. The court held public hearings, reviewed evidence including
classified military communications and testimony from Defence Minister Kim Yong-hyun
(who had been arrested), and heard arguments from Yoon's legal team and the Assembly's
prosecution managers (*소추위원*).

Yoon's legal team argued:
- The martial law declaration was an exercise of a constitutional presidential prerogative.
- The legislative stalemate and "anti-state" opposition activities constituted a genuine
  national emergency.
- The Assembly's swift lifting of martial law demonstrated that constitutional checks
  had functioned normally, making the question of impeachment moot or disproportionate.

The Assembly's managers argued:
- Article 77 requires an actual military emergency, not political disagreement.
- Deploying special forces to the National Assembly constituted an assault on the
  legislative branch.
- The deployment to the Election Commission represented an attempt to subvert the
  electoral system.
- The attempt to arrest sitting judges and dissolve opposition parties constituted sedition.

### Ruling (April 2025)

On **April 4, 2025**, the Constitutional Court issued a **unanimous 8–0 decision**
upholding Yoon's impeachment and removing him permanently from office. All eight
sitting justices concurred in the majority reasoning; none wrote a dissent. The court held:

1. **No constitutional basis for martial law**: Article 77 requires a state of war,
   armed conflict, or equivalent national crisis. Legislative opposition and investigative
   pressure on the executive do not constitute such a basis.

2. **Assault on the legislative branch**: The deployment of armed special-forces soldiers
   to the National Assembly building, with the intent to prevent members from convening,
   constituted a direct attack on the core institution of representative democracy.

3. **Threat to electoral integrity**: The deployment to the National Election Commission
   buildings under the pretext of investigating election-system fraud had no legal basis
   and constituted an abuse of emergency powers.

4. **Violation of fundamental democratic principles**: The attempt to arrest sitting
   judges and dissolve opposition political parties—leaked from the martial law plan
   documents—demonstrated that the declaration was targeted at political opponents
   rather than genuine national security threats.

The court's ruling stated that the actions "fundamentally undermined the constitutional
order" and that impeachment was the proportionate constitutional remedy. The decision
was issued at 11:00 AM on April 4, 2025; Yoon's removal from office took effect
immediately.

## Arrest and Criminal Prosecution

**Yoon's arrest** became one of the most contested legal proceedings in South Korean
history:

- The **Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials** (고위공직자범죄수사처,
  *Gongsucho*) led the initial investigation, issuing an arrest warrant on December 31,
  2024.
- Yoon initially refused to comply, citing immunity concerns and the legitimacy of the
  investigation body. His Presidential Security Service (대통령경호처) physically blocked
  investigators from serving the warrant on January 3, 2025.
- On **January 15, 2025**, investigators from the CIO, supported by police and after
  a prolonged standoff, successfully arrested Yoon—the first arrest of a sitting or
  former president in South Korean history under non-immunity circumstances. He was
  held in pre-trial detention.
- Criminal charges included **insurrection** (*내란죄*, punishable by life imprisonment
  or death under the Criminal Code in the most aggravated cases), **abuse of power**,
  and violations of the Martial Law Act.
- The insurrection trial, assigned to the Seoul Central District Court, was expected
  to extend through 2026–2027 given the volume of evidence, witnesses, and legal
  motions.

## Snap Presidential Election (June 2025)

Following Yoon's removal, the Constitution required a presidential election within
**60 days**. The **June 3, 2025 snap presidential election** was contested primarily
between:

- **Lee Jae-myung** (이재명, Democratic Party), the candidate who had narrowly lost to
  Yoon in March 2022, now running on a platform of democratic restoration,
  economic equity, and resumption of inter-Korean engagement.
- **Kim Moon-soo** (김문수, People Power Party), former governor of Gyeonggi Province
  and a labour-activist-turned-conservative politician.
- Several minor-party candidates.

**Lee Jae-myung won** with approximately **49.4%** of the vote, defeating Kim Moon-soo
(41.2%) and minor candidates. Lee was inaugurated as South Korea's twenty-first president
on June 4, 2025, ending the PPP's hold on the executive and installing the first
Democratic Party president since Moon Jae-in (2017–2022).

## Constitutional and Historical Significance

### Democratic Resilience

The crisis is regarded by constitutional scholars as a significant test and partial
validation of South Korea's democratic institutions. Unlike the 1961 coup (which
dissolved the Second Republic), the 1972 Yushin system (which dismantled the
constitutional order), and the 1980 coup-within-a-coup (which triggered the
[Gwangju Uprising](gwangju-uprising.md)), the 2024 martial law episode was contained
within six hours by a freely-functioning legislature. The Assembly's defiance—with
members climbing fences under military guard to reach the chamber—became an iconic
image of constitutional democracy in action.

### The Military's Changed Role

A critical factor in the crisis's rapid resolution was the conduct of the military.
Unlike 1961 or 1980, special-forces commanders on the ground at the National Assembly
reportedly declined to use force against legislators, and the acting Joint Chiefs of
Staff chief expressed reservations about the deployment. By dawn on December 4, military
commanders had signalled to the president that they would not sustain the deployment
against the Assembly's resolution. The **military's effective subordination to civilian
and constitutional authority**—a transformation worked over three decades since 1987—
was identified by analysts as the decisive variable separating 2024 from previous
authoritarian episodes.

### Precedents for Emergency Power

The crisis initiated a legislative debate over amending or clarifying **Article 77** to
define more precisely the conditions justifying martial law. Proposed reforms include:
- Requiring National Security Council (NSC) approval or a cabinet supermajority before
  a declaration takes effect.
- Explicitly excluding domestic political conflict from the scope of Article 77.
- Establishing mandatory judicial review within 24 hours of any declaration.

### Partisan Polarisation Deepens

The crisis both reflected and accelerated South Korean partisan polarisation. Conservative
supporters interpreted Yoon's prosecution as political retribution and the Constitutional
Court's ruling as weaponised by the left. Progressive supporters viewed the episode as
evidence that South Korea's democratic institutions required strengthening against
future executive overreach. Public trust in the presidency, the PPP, and to a lesser
degree the Constitutional Court was affected in contrasting directions depending on
political affiliation.

### International Dimensions

The crisis disrupted South Korea's diplomatic posture at a critical moment:
- Allies were alarmed by the declaration's instability signal; the US reaffirmed its
  commitment to the alliance but pressed for swift constitutional resolution.
- The acting government's ability to manage North Korean provocation and engage in
  ongoing trade and alliance negotiations was constrained by domestic uncertainty.
- Lee Jae-myung's election victory raised questions about the future of the hawkish
  Yoon-era stance on North Korea, Japan relations, and defence cooperation—questions
  addressed in his inaugural speech with calls for "pragmatic diplomacy."
