# Korean Independence Movement

The **Korean Independence Movement** (한국 독립운동) refers to the organized anti-colonial struggle against Japanese imperial rule and foreign domination from the 1876 opening of Korea to foreign trade through the 1945 liberation. Spanning nearly 70 years, the movement encompassed diplomatic resistance, armed rebellion, religious awakening, intellectual mobilization, and mass demonstrations. It unified Koreans across social classes, regions, and ideologies in a common cause of national sovereignty and self-determination, laying the moral and organizational foundations for the Korean republic and shaping modern Korean identity. August 15, the day of Japan's 1945 surrender, is commemorated annually in South Korea as **Gwangbokjeol** (광복절, "Day of Restored Light"), and March 1 is a national holiday honoring the 1919 uprising.

## Early Resistance (1876–1905)

### The Treaty Port Era and Righteous Army

Korea's forced opening to international trade and diplomatic relations (beginning with the 1876 **Treaty of Ganghwa** with Japan) exposed the Joseon kingdom to Western imperialism and Japanese expansion. The shock of foreign contact and technological disparity destabilized Joseon governance and triggered both conservative resistance and modernization reforms. In 1882, the **Imo Incident** (a military mutiny by soldiers resentful of Japanese-trained troops) and subsequent Japanese intervention heightened tensions and drew Chinese forces back onto the peninsula to counter Japanese influence.

The earliest organized resistance emerged from the Joseon elite and gentry. The **Righteous Army** (의병, uibyeong) was a grassroots military resistance movement led primarily by yangban (aristocratic) intellectuals and former officials who rejected foreign encroachment. Beginning in the 1880s and intensifying after the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)—which humiliated China and exposed Japanese military superiority—the Righteous Army mounted guerrilla campaigns against both Japanese forces and the Korean government (increasingly dominated by Japanese influence). A major wave of Righteous Army activity erupted after the 1905 Eulsa Protectorate Treaty (see below), and an estimated 70,000 Righteous Army fighters participated in the 1907–1908 peak of resistance. Japanese counter-insurgency operations suppressed the movement at the cost of approximately 17,000 Korean guerrilla deaths between 1907 and 1910.

### An Jung-geun and the Harbin Assassination (1909)

The most dramatic act of individual resistance in the pre-colonial period was the assassination of **Ito Hirobumi** (伊藤博文) by **An Jung-geun** (안중근) on October 26, 1909, at Harbin Station in Manchuria. Ito, Japan's first Prime Minister and chief architect of Korea's subjugation under the 1905 Protectorate Treaty, was shot three times as he arrived by train. An Jung-geun, a 31-year-old Catholic intellectual and former Righteous Army commander, declared the killing a patriotic act and insisted on trial before a formal court. He was convicted and executed on March 26, 1910, at Lüshun Prison. His final writings, composed in prison, articulated a vision of pan-Asian peace based on cooperation rather than Japanese imperial domination. An Jung-geun is venerated as a patriotic martyr in both South Korea (where he appears on commemorative currency) and China, where a memorial hall opened at Harbin Station in 2014.

The assassination paradoxically accelerated Japanese annexation, as Japanese authorities used it as justification to finalize direct colonial rule. Korea was formally annexed on August 22, 1910.

### The Donghak Peasant Revolt (1894)

Preceding the Righteous Army in scale and popular mobilization was the **Donghak Peasant Revolt** (동학 농민 운동) of 1894. Donghak ("Eastern Learning") was a syncretic Korean religion blending Neo-Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism, and indigenous shamanism, founded in 1860 by Choe Je-u (최제우). Donghak teachings emphasized social equality, anti-foreign sentiment, and spiritual renewal under the motto "In-nae-cheon" (인내천, "humans are heaven"). Choe Je-u was executed by the Joseon government in 1864 for spreading "dangerous doctrines."

In 1894, Donghak followers—many from peasant and merchant classes—rose under the leadership of **Jeon Bong-jun** (전봉준, known as the "Mung Bean General" for his short stature) against heavy taxation, yangban exploitation, and Japanese commercial encroachment. The rebels captured Jeonju, Joseon's southwestern administrative center, and forced the government to negotiate reform agreements. When Joseon requested Chinese military assistance, Japan intervened with its own forces, triggering the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895). After Japan's victory, remaining Donghak forces launched a second uprising in autumn 1894 and were decimated by combined Japanese and Korean government forces. Jeon Bong-jun was captured and executed in 1895.

Though militarily defeated, the Donghak movement survived as a nationalist and religious force, reorganizing as **Cheondogyo** (천도교, "Religion of the Heavenly Way") in 1905. Its legacy directly influenced the 1919 independence uprising, where Cheondogyo supplied 15 of the 33 signatories of the Declaration of Independence.

## The Japanese Protectorate and the March 1st Movement (1905–1919)

### Protectorate and Direct Rule

Following Japan's victory in the 1904–1905 Russo-Japanese War (fought partly on Korean soil), Japan solidified control of the peninsula. The 1905 **Eulsa Protectorate Treaty** (을사조약, also called the Japan–Korea Treaty of 1905) placed Korea under Japanese protectorate status, stripping it of diplomatic autonomy and installing a Japanese Resident-General. The treaty was signed under duress—some accounts hold that Emperor Gojong's seal was affixed by force—and was widely condemned by Koreans as illegitimate. Senior minister Min Yeong-hwan and others committed suicide in protest. Anti-Japanese demonstrations erupted, and the Righteous Army's second major wave began.

In 1907, the Japanese forced Emperor Gojong to abdicate after he dispatched a secret delegation to the **Hague Peace Conference** attempting to appeal Korea's case to international opinion. His son Sunjong became a powerless figurehead, and the Korean military was formally dissolved. On August 22, 1910, Japan formally annexed Korea through the **Japan–Korea Annexation Treaty** (한일병합조약), making the peninsula a direct Japanese colony for 35 years.

The Japanese Colonial Period (1910–1919, "military rule" phase) saw systematic economic extraction, suppression of Korean-language education and media, confiscation of land through cadastral surveys (dispossessing smallholder farmers), and cultural assimilation policies. Public gatherings and political associations were prohibited.

### The March 1st Movement (1919)

The **March 1st Movement** (3·1 운동, also called the Samil Independence Movement) was a nationwide mass uprising for Korean independence, triggered by a convergence of factors: the death of former Emperor Gojong on January 21, 1919 (poisoning was widely suspected, fueling rage), the influence of U.S. President Woodrow Wilson's "Fourteen Points" self-determination principle announced at the 1919 Paris Peace Conference, and the networks built by Christian and Cheondogyo organizations.

A group of **33 signatories** (민족 대표 33인), representing the leadership of Korean religious communities, drafted and read a Declaration of Independence (기미 독립선언서). The 33 included 16 Christian leaders (mostly Protestant), 15 Cheondogyo leaders, and 2 Buddhist leaders. The declaration was written in part by Choe Nam-seon (최남선), one of Korea's most prominent intellectuals. The signatories deliberately turned themselves in to Japanese authorities after the reading, intending a peaceful demonstration.

On March 1, 1919, the declaration was read publicly at **Tapgol Park** (탑골공원, formerly Pagoda Park) in central Seoul, and simultaneously or shortly thereafter in cities and towns across Korea. Mass demonstrations erupted throughout the peninsula. The movement lasted approximately three months, with demonstrations recorded in cities, market towns, and rural villages from Pyongyang to Busan to **Jeju Island** (where local demonstrations occurred).

**Scale**: Estimates of participants range from 1 to 2 million, out of a total Korean population of approximately 17 million. Demonstrations occurred in every province.

**Yu Gwan-sun** (유관순), a 16-year-old student at Ewha Hakdang (women's school), organized demonstrations in her home county of Cheonan after returning from Seoul when Japanese authorities closed schools. She was arrested, tortured, and died in Seodaemun Prison on September 28, 1920, at age 17, becoming the movement's most iconic martyr. She was posthumously awarded South Korea's highest civilian honor in 2019 on the 100th anniversary of the uprising.

**Japanese response**: The colonial government deployed military and police to suppress the demonstrations with extreme violence. Bayonet charges, mass arrests, and destruction of villages followed. The most notorious incident was the **Jeam-ri Massacre** (화성 제암리 학살사건, April 15, 1919) in Suwon, where Japanese troops locked villagers in a church and burned them alive, killing 29. The total death toll was estimated at over 7,500 by contemporary independence sources; modern scholarship places verified deaths at approximately 1,600–2,000, with tens of thousands wounded and approximately 46,000 arrested.

**Aftermath**: The March 1st Movement forced Japan to moderate its colonial approach. The incoming Governor-General Saito Makoto implemented the **"Cultural Policy"** (문화정치, 1919–1931), permitting limited Korean-language newspapers, easing some assembly restrictions, and ending the practice of Japanese police and teachers wearing military swords. However, the reforms were largely cosmetic; land confiscation and economic exploitation continued, and surveillance intensified through expanded police networks. Domestically, the movement demonstrated Korean national will to the world and created organizational networks that sustained the independence movement for decades.

## Provisional Government and Organized Resistance (1919–1932)

Following the March 1st Movement, Korean nationalists established the **Korean Provisional Government** (대한민국 임시정부) in Shanghai, China, on April 11, 1919. The Provisional Government adopted a republican constitution modeled partly on the U.S. Constitution, the first in Korean history, and claimed to represent the Korean people before international opinion.

**Key figures** in the Provisional Government included:
- **Syngman Rhee** (이승만) — an American-educated Ph.D. (Princeton, 1910) and Christian nationalist who served as the first President; based primarily in Hawaii and Washington, he ran the Korean Commission lobbying effort in the United States
- **Kim Ku** (김구) — a nationalist activist who led the Korean Patriotic Corps (한인애국단), which organized direct-action operations against Japanese officials; he is considered the most iconic leader of the exile movement
- **Yi Dong-nyeong** (이동녕) — a veteran independence activist who served as President of the Provisional Government four times in succession during the 1920s–1930s, providing continuity
- **Yi Dong-hwi** (이동휘) — a Righteous Army veteran who became a communist and served as the Provisional Government's first Prime Minister before leaving to found the Korean Socialist Party in Soviet Russia

The Provisional Government maintained an underground communication network with Korea, published independence-movement newspapers (including *The Independent*, 독립신문), and sought recognition from the Paris Peace Conference—without success, as the Allied powers prioritized Japanese cooperation over Korean self-determination.

### Armed Resistance in Manchuria

In Manchuria, Korean independence fighters established military units and conducted guerrilla operations against Japanese forces. By 1920, three major independent armies operated in Manchurian territories: the **Korean Independence Army** (독립군), the **National Army** (국민군), and various affiliated units. The October 1920 **Battle of Qingshanli** (청산리 전투) was the largest Korean military victory against Japanese forces during the colonial period, as Korean commander **Kim Jwa-jin** (김좌진) and approximately 1,600 fighters defeated Japanese forces of 18,000–25,000 across multiple engagements over six days, inflicting approximately 1,200 Japanese casualties at the cost of around 130 Korean dead.

Japan retaliated with the **Gando (Jiandao) massacre** of 1920, in which Japanese forces killed an estimated 2,000–3,500 Korean civilians and fighters in Manchuria's Korean settlements. The massacre dispersed resistance forces further north.

**Kim Il-sung** (김일성) conducted guerrilla operations against Japanese forces in Manchuria during the 1930s as part of the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army (a Comintern-affiliated force). Though his wartime exploits were mythologized extensively by North Korean propaganda, his activities in Manchuria in the 1930s are historically documented. He and his forces eventually retreated into Soviet territory in 1941, where they trained with the Red Army until Korea's liberation.

### The Korean Patriotic Corps and the 1932 Shanghai Attack

Kim Ku reorganized armed resistance through the **Korean Patriotic Corps** (한인애국단), conducting targeted assassinations of Japanese officials. The most famous operation was conducted by **Yun Bong-gil** (윤봉길), a 24-year-old Korean nationalist operative, on April 29, 1932, at **Hongkou Park** (虹口公園) in Shanghai during a Japanese military celebration of the Emperor's birthday and Japan's capture of Shanghai. Yun threw a water-bottle bomb into the dignitaries' platform, killing General Yoshinori Shirakawa and the Japanese minister to China, and wounding several others including Mamoru Shigemitsu (future Japanese Foreign Minister). Yun was captured and executed by firing squad on December 19, 1932.

Chiang Kai-shek, impressed by the attack, granted the Korean Provisional Government Chinese Nationalist government support: "What a hundred thousand Chinese soldiers could not do, a single Korean did." The attack accelerated Nationalist Chinese backing of the Korean resistance and led eventually to the formation of the Korean Liberation Army.

## Cultural and Intellectual Resistance (1920s–1940s)

As military resistance was suppressed, Korean independence activism shifted toward cultural, intellectual, and economic resistance:

### Cultural Preservation

Korean intellectuals, writers, and artists emphasized **cultural nationalism**—preserving Korean language, literature, history, and art against Japanese assimilation policies. **Ju Si-gyeong** (주시경), the father of modern Korean linguistics, had developed systematic rules for Korean grammar before his death in 1914; his disciples continued promoting Hangul (Korean script) education and standardization through the **Society for the Study of the Korean Language** (조선어학회). In 1942, Japanese authorities arrested 33 members of the Society on fabricated treason charges (the **Joseon Language Society incident**, 조선어학회 사건), suppressing the most organized effort to preserve the language.

Modern Korean literature emerged as a vehicle of nationalist expression. The poet **Kim So-wol** (김소월), writing in the 1920s, articulated themes of longing, loss, and Korean beauty in pure Hangul; his 1925 poem "Azaleas" (진달래꽃) is among the most-read works in Korean literary history. Historians and public intellectuals including **Sin Chae-ho** (신채호) produced foundational works of Korean nationalist history, framing Korean civilization as independent and distinct from Chinese influence.

### Religious Movements

Korean Christian churches became centers of nationalist sentiment. **Protestant Christianity**, introduced to Korea in the 1880s, expanded rapidly and became associated with modernization, education, and democratic values, creating a large constituency aligned with independence. Korean Catholic communities, though less politically organized, also contributed martyrs to the independence cause. The **Cheondogyo** organization (reformed from Donghak) maintained pan-national networks and moral authority.

### Economic Resistance

The **Buy Korean Movement** (국산품 애용 운동) encouraged Koreans to purchase domestically produced goods over Japanese imports. More significantly, Korean entrepreneurs and nationalist businesspeople established Korean-owned enterprises in banking, textiles, and food production—including the 1919 founding of the **Gyeongseong Textile Company** (경성방직, predecessor to modern Kyungbang)—as expressions of economic nationalism.

## Korean Liberation Army (1940–1945)

On September 17, 1940, the Korean Provisional Government formally established the **Korean Liberation Army** (광복군, Gwangbokgun) in Chongqing, China, under the command of **Ji Cheong-cheon** (지청천), with **Yi Beom-seok** (이범석) as a senior field commander. The Korean Liberation Army worked with the Chinese Nationalist government (GMD) and later coordinated with the U.S. Office of Strategic Services (OSS, precursor to the CIA) for joint operations. The OSS was training Korean agents for infiltration into the Japanese-occupied peninsula when Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945.

## Late Colonial Resistance (1937–1945)

Following Japan's full-scale invasion of China in 1937, the Japanese colonial government intensified control over Korea. Authorities implemented:
- **Sōshi-kaimei** (創氏改名, 1939–1940): Mandatory adoption of Japanese names, enforced by cutting off access to rations, schools, and civil records for those who refused
- **Comfort women system**: Forced sexual slavery of Korean women (estimated 50,000–200,000 total from occupied territories) to serve Japanese military "comfort stations"
- **Forced labor mobilization**: An estimated 700,000 Koreans were conscripted or deceived into wartime industrial labor in Japan and elsewhere under brutal conditions
- **Military conscription**: Began formally in 1944, with an estimated 200,000+ Koreans conscripted into Imperial Japanese military service by 1945

Korean resistance, though weakened by systematic repression, continued through clandestine networks, student organizations, and overseas communities. The Manchurian diaspora (estimated 1.7 million Koreans in Manchuria by 1940) and the Hawaiian Korean community (organized through the **Korean National Association**, 대한인국민회) maintained fundraising, advocacy, and communication networks.

## Liberation and Legacy (1945)

On August 15, 1945, Japan surrendered to the Allied powers following the atomic bombings of Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9), ending World War II and 35 years of Japanese colonial rule. Korean independence activists throughout the world celebrated, but the victory was immediately complicated by Cold War division.

The United States and Soviet Union had informally agreed to divide Korea at the 38th parallel as a temporary administrative division in August 1945. This division, formalized at the December 1945 Moscow Conference, solidified into two separate states: the **Republic of Korea** (South Korea) established August 15, 1948, led initially by Syngman Rhee; and the **Democratic People's Republic of Korea** (North Korea) established September 9, 1948, led by Kim Il-sung. The Korean Provisional Government's authority was not recognized by either the United States or the Soviet Union, marginalizing the most internationally organized Korean independence leadership.

The liberation set the stage for the Korean War (1950–1953), in which competing visions of the independent Korean state produced devastating armed conflict that killed an estimated 2.5 million Koreans and entrenched permanent division. The pattern also produced lasting trauma in places like **Jeju Island**, where the April 3 Incident of 1948 grew directly from the post-liberation political chaos and the Cold War overlay on Korean independence movement factions.

## Contested Legacies

The Korean Independence Movement's diverse participants—monarchists, republicans, communists, Christians, businesspeople, workers—each claimed ownership of the movement's legacy. Post-war Korean historians and politicians have debated which figures and strategies best represented the "true" independence movement, often along Cold War and North-South lines:

- **South Korean nationalist historiography** has traditionally emphasized Christian, republican, and capitalist participants (Syngman Rhee, Kim Ku), while minimizing communist figures. The **Independence Hall of Korea** (독립기념관) in Cheonan, opened in 1987, was constructed as a national monument to this narrative.
- **North Korean historiography** attributes liberation primarily to Kim Il-sung's Manchurian guerrilla struggle and Soviet military power, reframing the independence movement as a proto-communist revolutionary struggle.
- **Contemporary Korean scholarship**, freed from Cold War constraints after South Korea's 1987 democratization, increasingly recognizes the movement's ideological pluralism, the contributions of women (particularly haenyeo who participated in local resistance on Jeju and coastal regions), workers, and religious leaders, and the complexity of collaboration versus resistance under Japanese rule.

The independence movement's moral authority remains central to Korean political discourse. Candidates across the political spectrum invoke its legacy—**Moon Jae-in's** 2017 campaign included a visit to the March 1st Memorial and the Jeju 4·3 Peace Park to claim continuity with historical justice movements. The pattern of who counts as a "true" independence fighter remains contested, particularly regarding figures like Yi Gwang-su who began as independence advocates and later collaborated with Japanese authorities.
