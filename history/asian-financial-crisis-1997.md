# The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and Korean Economic Restructuring

**Category**: History  
**Key figures**: Kim Young-sam, Kim Dae-jung, Soros Fund Management, Bank of Korea

## Summary

The **1997 Asian Financial Crisis** hit South Korea with extraordinary severity, transforming the Korean economy in ways that reverberated into the 2020s. Beginning in July 1997 with the Thai baht's collapse, the crisis spread through Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines before engulfing South Korea in November. The immediate shock was the precipitous devaluation of the Korean won (from ~900 to over 1,900 per USD by end-1997), a spike in foreign debt service costs, and the exhaustion of Korea's foreign-exchange reserves—forcing the government to seek a $57 billion International Monetary Fund (IMF) bailout on November 21, 1997. The crisis exposed decades of crony capitalism, unsustainable chaebol expansion, currency mismanagement, and the weakness of Korean financial regulations. Under IMF conditionality, Korea underwent severe restructuring: forced chaebol bankruptcies (especially Daewoo), labor-market deregulation, mass layoffs and casualization, the rapid rise of temporary and non-regular employment, and a shift toward market-oriented corporate governance. The restructuring imposed enormous social costs—unemployment more than tripled (from 2.6% in 1997 to roughly 7% in 1998, peaking near 8.7% in early 1999), wages stagnated, household debt surged, and the social safety net proved inadequate. Yet by 2001, Korea had repaid the IMF ahead of schedule and begun a recovery buoyed by semiconductor and export demand, positioning the crisis as a painful but ultimately temporary shock from which Korea recovered faster than peers. The legacy of the crisis persists in Korean anxiety about external vulnerability, corporate restructuring as a political tool, labor-market dualism, and the embeddedness of IMF conditionality in Korean policy consciousness.

## Origins and the Crisis Unfolds

The roots of the 1997 crisis lay in Korea's rapid, debt-fueled growth model of the 1980s–1990s. The Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan developmental-state framework had relied on directed credit, chaebol-led industrialization, and heavy investment in semiconductors, automobiles, and petrochemicals. By the early 1990s, however, the model showed signs of exhaustion: chaebol debt-to-equity ratios had soared above 300–400% (versus an OECD norm of roughly 100%), many conglomerates were diversifying into unrelated sectors with deteriorating returns on equity, and the banking system was loaded with non-performing loans to inefficient chaebol subsidiaries. Korea's 30 largest chaebol carried collective debts of approximately $80 billion by 1997.

Kim Young-sam's government (1993–1998) pursued some financial deregulation and capital-account liberalization—including Korea's 1996 admission to the OECD, which was celebrated as a mark of developed-nation status but also accelerated the inflow of volatile short-term capital. His administration's "segyehwa" (세계화, globalization) agenda pushed Korean firms to expand internationally and borrow in foreign currency, increasing external vulnerability. By early 1997, the cracks were widening: Hanbo Steel collapsed in January 1997, becoming the first major chaebol failure in a decade; Sammi Steel followed in March; Jinro Group, Korea's largest spirits company, filed for debt workouts in April. By mid-1997 eight smaller chaebol had effectively failed, exposing the fragility of the banking system. Meanwhile, external vulnerabilities mounted: Korea's current-account deficit widened to 4.4% of GDP in 1996, foreign debt accumulated rapidly (total external debt reached approximately $120 billion, with 60% in short-term instruments), and the Bank of Korea's foreign-exchange reserves—officially around $30 billion—were almost fully committed to covering earlier obligations and forward contracts.

The triggering event came in July 1997 when Thailand's baht collapsed after the Thai central bank's foreign reserves proved insufficient to defend the currency; capital flows reversed abruptly across Asia. By September, Indonesia's rupiah and the Malaysian ringgit were in free fall. Korean financial authorities initially declared the country immune to "Thai-style" contagion—Korea was, after all, the world's eleventh-largest economy and a new OECD member. But by October the won faced intense selling pressure; investors recognized that Korean banks had borrowed short-term abroad to fund long-term domestic loans, an archetypal maturity mismatch. The Bank of Korea burned through $20 billion in foreign reserves in an ultimately futile defense; by early November, usable reserves had fallen to roughly $6 billion against short-term debt obligations of over $60 billion. On November 21, 1997, the government was forced to approach the IMF.

## The IMF Bailout and Restructuring Program

On December 3, 1997, Korea signed a standby agreement with the IMF for a $57 billion package—the largest in IMF history to that date, comprising $21 billion from the IMF itself, $10 billion from the World Bank, $4 billion from the Asian Development Bank, and bilateral contributions from the United States, Japan, and European nations. The won's collapse was immediate and severe: from approximately 900 won per USD at the start of 1997, the currency fell to over 1,900 won per USD by late December 1997, a depreciation of more than 50%. The crisis was publicly framed as a temporary liquidity problem, but the IMF's conditions demanded deep structural reform across the economy.

The key components of the IMF conditionality included: (1) **macroeconomic austerity**—interest rates were raised sharply (the overnight call rate peaked above 30% in late 1997) to stabilize the currency and curb the current-account deficit; (2) **financial-sector restructuring**—14 of Korea's 30 merchant banks were immediately suspended and later closed; commercial banks underwent forced mergers and recapitalization, with the government injecting approximately 64 trillion won (~$50 billion) in public funds into the financial sector between 1997 and 2002; (3) **mandatory chaebol restructuring**—the government demanded the "Big Deals" (빅딜), forced sector swaps among the top five chaebol to rationalize industrial capacity, and imposed debt-to-equity ratio ceilings of 200% on the top 30 chaebol; (4) **labor-market liberalization**—the February 1998 revision of the Labor Standards Act legalized layoffs for "managerial reasons" (경영상 이유에 의한 해고), a watershed reversal of the norm of near-permanent employment that had defined Korean labor relations; (5) **corporate-governance reform**—foreign investor shareholding limits were raised from 26% to 50% in December 1997 and to 100% for most sectors by 1998, boards were required to include outside directors, and financial disclosure standards were tightened; and (6) **capital-account liberalization** to allow unimpeded foreign investment and capital repatriation.

### Social Costs and the Gold Collection Campaign

The restructuring process was extraordinarily painful. **Unemployment** exploded from 2.6% in 1997 to 8.7% in early 1999—over 1.7 million workers lost their jobs within a year. Real wages fell 7% in 1998. Korea's GDP contracted by 5.5% in 1998, ending a multi-decade streak of uninterrupted growth that had averaged 8–10% annually. Homelessness and poverty surged visibly in Seoul and other cities; the term "IMF era" (IMF 시대) entered Korean vernacular as a shorthand for economic catastrophe.

One remarkable popular response was the **Gold Collection Campaign** (금 모으기 운동, January–March 1998), in which ordinary Koreans voluntarily donated gold jewelry, wedding rings, and family heirlooms to help replenish the nation's foreign-exchange reserves. Organized through KBS (Korean Broadcasting System) and civic groups, the campaign collected approximately 226 tonnes of gold—valued at roughly $2.2 billion—from some 3.5 million participants, making it one of the most extraordinary episodes of collective national sacrifice in modern economic history. The campaign was both practically significant and symbolically important, expressing a national determination to overcome the crisis through collective effort rather than external rescue.

**Daewoo Group**, once the world's second-largest conglomerate by assets and a symbol of Korean industrial ambition, collapsed spectacularly in July 1999 with debts of approximately $80 billion—one of the largest corporate bankruptcies in world history. Founder Kim Woo-choong fled Korea, was later convicted of fraud and embezzlement (sentenced in 2006 to ten years, pardoned in 2008), and the group's assets were parceled to competitors: Daewoo Motors to General Motors (2002), Daewoo Electronics eventually to various buyers, Daewoo Heavy Industries restructured as Doosan Infracore. **Hyundai Motors** underwent forced debt restructuring and laid off over 10,000 workers; the sit-in strikes at Hyundai's Ulsan plant in December 1998—involving some 8,000 workers—were among the most dramatic labor actions of the crisis period. Banking consolidation reduced the number of domestic banks from 33 in 1997 to 17 by 2002; Citigroup acquired KorAm Bank, and Korea Exchange Bank was acquired by Lone Star Funds at controversial prices. **Household debt surged** as Koreans borrowed to smooth consumption during unemployment, setting a structural pattern of elevated household leverage that persisted into the 2010s and 2020s. The social safety net—already meager by OECD standards—proved wholly inadequate; welfare expenditures increased from 3.3% of GDP (1996) to 4.8% (1999) but remained among the lowest in the OECD.

## Recovery and Structural Transformation

Remarkably, Korea's recovery was swift. GDP rebounded to +10.7% growth in 1999—among the strongest in the world that year. By August 2001, Korea had repaid the entire IMF loan ahead of the scheduled 2004 deadline, a moment celebrated with public ceremony. The recovery was propelled by several converging factors: (1) the won's devaluation from 900 to ~1,100 per USD in the recovery period (from a trough of ~1,900) made Korean exports—automobiles, electronics, semiconductors, ships—suddenly highly price-competitive in global markets; (2) the global technology and semiconductor upcycle of 1999–2000 benefited Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix (then Hyundai Electronics) enormously, as DRAM prices recovered sharply; (3) structural reforms genuinely improved productivity and profitability in surviving firms; and (4) government credit-card stimulus—the Kim Dae-jung government actively encouraged consumer credit expansion in 1999–2002 to rebuild domestic demand, though this later generated the 2003–2004 credit-card crisis (카드 대란) when millions of consumers defaulted.

However, the restructuring wrought permanent changes to Korean capitalism:

**Chaebol governance** became more professionalized in form—outside directors, audit committees, and consolidated financial statements were mandated—but family control persisted and in some respects deepened. The collapse of weaker chaebol (Daewoo, Ssangyong, Halla) concentrated economic power in the surviving titans: Samsung Electronics grew to represent over 20% of Korea's total exports by the mid-2010s.

**Labor markets** became durably dualized. The legalization of mass layoffs normalized employment terminations that had previously been socially and legally impermissible, and the rise of "non-regular" (비정규직) workers—temporary, contract, daily, and dispatched employees without job security, benefits, or seniority—accelerated dramatically. By 2005, non-regular workers constituted approximately 37% of Korea's workforce, one of the highest rates in the OECD; this share remained roughly stable through the 2020s, creating a two-tier labor force that is among the most significant sources of contemporary Korean inequality.

**Foreign ownership** of Korean firms increased dramatically. The elimination of foreign shareholding limits and fire-sale acquisitions by foreign private equity and institutional investors meant that by 2004, foreigners owned approximately 42% of KOSPI (Korea Stock Exchange)-listed shares by value, compared to roughly 12% in 1996. Samsung Electronics was majority-owned by foreign investors for much of the 2000s–2010s, exposing it to shareholder pressure for dividends and share buybacks that constrained long-term R&D investment.

**Household debt** remained structurally elevated throughout subsequent decades. The ratio of household debt to GDP rose from roughly 45% in 1997 to over 100% by the 2010s—and to nearly 110% by 2023, one of the highest in the world—making Korean households chronically vulnerable to interest-rate shocks, housing-market downturns, and income disruptions.

## Political Consequences and Legacy

Politically, the crisis ended Kim Young-sam's presidency under a cloud and enabled Kim Dae-jung's victory in the December 1997 election—the first transfer of power to an opposition party. The crisis became the crucible of Kim Dae-jung's presidency (1998–2003); his "IMF Emergency" framing of the recovery period and his administration's continuation of IMF-mandated reforms gave the crisis an extended political half-life. The chaebol prosecutions and the Daewoo bankruptcy became markers of a new, more market-oriented Korea, though the reality remained one of managed capitalism and state-directed restructuring. The crisis also amplified Korea's awareness of external vulnerability and dependence on foreign capital flows, informing subsequent policy decisions (including the accumulation of massive foreign-exchange reserves that would have prevented another 1997-style crisis, and the expansion of foreign-exchange swaps with the US Federal Reserve). By the 2010s–2020s, the 1997 crisis had receded from immediate memory among younger Koreans, yet its institutional legacy—the dualized labor market, elevated household debt, the power of foreign shareholders over chaebol strategy, and the precedent of massive social adjustment in the name of external stability—remained deeply embedded in Korean capitalism and society.
