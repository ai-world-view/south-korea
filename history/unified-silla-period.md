# Unified Silla Period

**Category**: History  
**Key figures**: Kim Yu-shin, King Munmu of Silla, the Buddhist thinkers Wonhyo and Uisang, the monk Jajang, and the maritime trader Jang Bogo

## Summary

Unified Silla (통일신라, 668–935 CE) marks the period in which the Silla kingdom, after defeating the Baekje and Goguryeo kingdoms and their Tang dynasty allies, established the first political unification of the Korean peninsula and sustained it for nearly 300 years. This era, initiated by the military campaigns of General Kim Yu-shin and King Munmu of Silla (r. 661–681), transformed Silla from a regional power into the dominant East Asian player in its sphere and saw an extraordinary flowering of Buddhist civilization, artistic achievement, and administrative sophistication.

Unified Silla is conventionally divided into three periods: Early Unified Silla (668–780s), often called the "golden age," marked by military consolidation and Buddhist vitality; the Central period (780s–822), characterized by aristocratic reforms and cultural refinement; and the Late period (822–935), during which court factionalism, aristocratic fragmentation, and peasant unrest precipitated the kingdom's decline and the emergence of the Later Three Kingdoms (892–935) before Goryeo's reunification in 936. The era witnessed the development of a sophisticated administrative system based on Confucian principles (imported from Tang China), the maturation of Korean Buddhist art and philosophy, the emergence of seonbae-hubae (senior-junior) Confucian networking hierarchies, and the codification of Korean cultural identity distinct from Chinese models.

## Early Unified Silla (668–780s): The Golden Age

### Military Unification and Consolidation

General Kim Yu-shin (김유신, 595–673), a Hwarang-trained commander of aristocratic Silla lineage, orchestrated the decisive campaigns that unified the peninsula. The sequence involved:

- **660 CE**: Destruction of Baekje by Tang forces and Silla armies led by Kim Yu-shin; the Baekje royal family and court were taken to China, though some survivors fled to Yamato Japan. A Baekje restoration movement, backed by Yamato Japan, was crushed at the **Battle of Baekgang** (白江口之戰, 663 CE) at the mouth of today's Geum River, where Tang-Silla naval forces annihilated a combined Baekje loyalist–Yamato fleet of roughly 400 ships.
- **668 CE**: Fall of Goguryeo, Silla's north-eastern rival, after internal court purges weakened its leadership and a coordinated Tang-Silla campaign overran Pyongyang. Tang established the Andong Protectorate General (安東都護府) to administer the region, signalling its intention to control the whole peninsula rather than withdraw.
- **670–676 CE**: The **Silla-Tang Wars**, in which Silla resisted Tang's bid to absorb the peninsula, allied with Goguryeo and Baekje remnant forces, and conducted a sustained land-and-sea campaign to expel Tang garrisons south of the Taedong River. The decisive naval engagement was the **Battle of Gibeol-po** (기벌포, 676 CE), fought near today's Seosan in South Chungcheong Province, where Silla forces under Admiral Sijeuk destroyed a Tang fleet in twenty-two consecutive engagements over several days. Tang subsequently withdrew all forces north of the Taedong River and confined its Korean administration to Liaodong. This expulsion—not the 668 fall of Goguryeo—is celebrated in Korean historiography as the true moment of political independence.

Following these victories, King Munmu (r. 661–681) and his successors implemented administrative reforms, consolidated Silla's control over former Baekje and Goguryeo territories south of the Taedong, and began the process of "Silla-fication"—adapting non-Silla populations to Silla administrative and cultural norms. Munmu's dying wish, recorded in his epitaph, was to be cremated and his ashes scattered in the East Sea so he could become a sea dragon protecting the kingdom; a marine reef off Gyeongju's coast (Daewangam, 大王岩) is traditionally identified as his burial site.

### Administrative Reforms and the Bone-Rank System

Unified Silla retained and systematized the **bone-rank system** (골품제, golpumje), a hereditary aristocratic hierarchy that had evolved in earlier Silla. The system consisted of:

- **Sacred Bone (성골)**: Reserved exclusively for the royal family (extinct after 654 CE).
- **True Bone (진골)**: The highest-ranking nobility, from which most officials and generals were drawn.
- **Remaining ranks (육두품 onwards)**: Lower nobility and commoners, with advancement theoretically based on merit and examination, though birth and patronage remained critical.

This system created a stable aristocratic order but also concentrated power in the True Bone aristocracy, sowing tensions that would plague the late Unified Silla period. Officials were recruited through a limited civil-service examination system (modeled on Tang China), though the bone-rank system prevented commoners from reaching the highest ranks.

King Munmu and his successors also established a centralized provincial administrative structure, replacing older chiefdom-based rule with a Tang-influenced system of nine provinces (九州, gu-ju), subordinate prefectures and counties, and five secondary capitals (五小京, o-sogyeong). A capital bureaucracy oversaw regional governors, tax collection, and military garrisons.

### Buddhist Florescence

Buddhism, already a major religious and cultural force in Silla, reached its zenith in the unified period. The royal court patronized temple construction, scriptural translation, and the training of monks. Major achievements included:

- **Bulguksa Temple** (불국사, construction begun 751 under King Gyeongdeok and completed c. 774) — A masterpiece of Buddhist architecture conceived as an earthly representation of the Buddha Land (Pure Land), and associated with the nearby **Seokguram Grotto** (석굴암, completed c. 774), an artificial stone-domed cave enshrining a serene granite Buddha surrounded by carved relief panels. (The *Tripitaka Koreana*, by contrast, is a later Goryeo-era woodblock canon housed at Haeinsa, not a Silla work.)
- **Printing and Textual Transmission**: Silla became a major center for Buddhist scripture copying and an early experimenter with xylographic (woodblock) printing, following Tang models. The kingdom's monks trained in China and brought back the latest Buddhist interpretations and texts.
- **Buddhist Scholarship**: The period produced Korea's most celebrated Buddhist thinker, **Wonhyo** (원효, 617–686 CE), a self-taught layman who synthesized competing strands of Mahayana Buddhism into the "One Mind" (일심, Ilsim) philosophy. Wonhyo deliberately violated clerical rules—fathering a son (the poet Seol Ch'ong) with a widowed princess—to demonstrate that Buddhist enlightenment was available to laypeople, not only monks. His collected writings influenced Buddhist schools in China and Japan. His contemporary **Uisang** (의상, 625–702 CE) studied under the Chinese Huayan master Zhiyan and founded the Korean Hwaeom (Avatamsaka) school; Uisang's geometric diagram summarizing Huayan philosophy, the Dharmadhātu-diagram (法界圖), is still used in Korean temples. Monk Jajang (자장, 590–658 CE) promoted the Vinaya (monastic discipline) tradition and secured Chinese Tripitaka texts for Korea.
- **Emille Bell**: Completed in 771 CE under King Hyegong, the **Emille Bell** (에밀레종, also called the Divine Bell of the Great King Seongdeok, 성덕대왕신종) is the largest surviving bronze bell in Korea, weighing approximately 18.9 tonnes and standing 3.75 metres tall. Its name derives from a legend that a child was sacrificed in its casting to achieve its haunting resonance. The bell is now housed in the Gyeongju National Museum.
- **Pagodas and Sculpture**: The era saw construction of multi-tiered stone pagodas (탑, tap) and Buddhist statuary of increasing sophistication, blending Silla and Tang aesthetic traditions. The twin pagodas at Bulguksa—**Dabotap** (多寶塔) and **Seokgatap** (釋迦塔)—exemplify this synthesis: Dabotap is ornate and asymmetrical; Seokgatap is austere and geometric. Seokgatap was found during a 1966 accidental examination to contain the world's oldest surviving woodblock-printed text, the *Dharani Sutra* (무구정광대다라니경, c. 751 CE).

The integration of Buddhism with Confucian statecraft created a unique Korean Buddhist tradition that would persist through Goryeo and into early Joseon.

## Central Unified Silla (780s–822): Aristocratic Refinement

### Aristocratic Culture and the Hwarang

The Hwarang (화랑, "Flower Knights"), an elite warrior-aristocrat organization that had originated in Three Kingdoms–era Silla, continued to serve as a training ground for military and civil leaders. Hwarang ideology synthesized Confucian virtue ethics, Buddhist philosophy, and martial discipline, creating a distinctive Korean aristocratic culture.

Members of the True Bone aristocracy often passed through the Hwarang ranks before assuming official positions. The organization reinforced seonbae-hubae (senior-junior) hierarchical networks that would characterize Korean bureaucracy and society into the modern era.

### Literary and Artistic Achievement

The Central period saw the development of a distinctive Silla literary culture:

- **Hyangga** (향가): A Korean poetic form recorded in the native language using Chinese characters to represent Korean sounds (the idu/hyangchal script). Twenty-five hyangga poems survive, collected in the 13th-century *Samguk Yusa* (三國遺事, "Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms") compiled by the Buddhist monk Iryeon. The *Samguk Yusa* remains the primary source for Silla mythology, folk culture, and Buddhist legend, alongside the earlier, more court-focused *Samguk Sagi* (1145). Hyangga poems range from Buddhist devotional hymns ("Song of the Comet," attributed to the monk Yungcheon, allegedly composed to avert a foreign attack) to aristocratic laments ("Requiem for the Dead Sister") to simple courtly songs. The poem "Song of Ch'ŏyong" (處容歌), attributed to a son of the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, is one of the earliest examples of shamanistic lyric in the Korean literary tradition.
- **Seol Ch'ong and Idu Literature**: Seol Ch'ong (설총, fl. 7th–8th century), son of the monk Wonhyo, systematized the **idu** writing system, in which Classical Chinese characters were repurposed to represent Korean grammatical particles and inflections. This was a precursor to the later *hangul* alphabet (invented 1443) and is the earliest formalized attempt to render Korean in writing.
- **Calligraphy and Painting**: Silla artists and monks developed distinctive styles of Chinese-influenced calligraphy and Buddhist-themed painting, which influenced Japanese and subsequent Korean traditions. Silla artisans were active in Japan's Nara court; the *Shōsō-in* imperial treasure house in Nara still holds Silla-manufactured silk textiles, mirrors, and musical instruments from the 8th century.

### Economic Stabilization

Unified Silla's early centuries saw agricultural development, the consolidation of the tax system (based on grain/tax-in-kind and corvée labor), and the growth of trading networks. The capital Gyeongju became one of the largest and most cosmopolitan cities in East Asia, with foreign merchants (Chinese, Arab, Indian) establishing trading communities.

## Late Unified Silla (822–935): Decline and Fragmentation

### Court Factionalism and the Bone-Rank Crisis

In the late 9th century, Unified Silla's political unity fractured under pressure from several sources:

- **Aristocratic Factionalism**: Competition among True Bone families for control of the throne and bureaucracy led to coups, counter-coups, and the virtual subordination of weak kings to oligarchic councils.
- **Bone-Rank Rigidity**: The system prevented commoners from advancement, creating resentment among the emerging merchant and military classes. Unable to rise through official channels, talented non-aristocrats turned to rebellion or private military service.
- **Peasant Unrest**: Heavy taxation, forced labor obligations (especially for military projects), and periodic famines sparked uprisings, particularly in the 9th century.

### The Later Three Kingdoms (892–935)

By the late 9th century, Unified Silla's authority had eroded. Three successor states emerged:

- **Silla** (reduced): Controlled only the southeastern portion of the peninsula around Gyeongju; internally fractious and weakened.
- **Later Baekje** (892–934): Founded by Gyeon Hwon, a warlord, this state controlled the southwestern peninsula and rivaled Silla for resources and prestige.
- **Later Goguryeo / Goryeo** (901–918): Founded by Gung Ye and later reorganized by Wang Geon, this northern kingdom eventually unified the peninsula in 936 under the Goryeo dynasty.

This period of fragmentation echoed the Three Kingdoms era and is sometimes called the "Turbulent Era" or the "Age of Warlords," though many institutions and cultural practices persisted across the succession.

### Final Collapse and Transition to Goryeo

Unified Silla's last rulers faced military defeat and internal collapse. (An earlier king, Gyeongae, had been killed in 927 when Gyeon Hwon's Later Baekje forces sacked Gyeongju.) In 935, the final king, **Gyeongsun** (경순왕), voluntarily surrendered the throne to Wang Geon of Goryeo, formally ending Unified Silla. Wang Geon established the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392, formally unified 936), which absorbed Silla's territory, administrative structures, and much of its cultural and Buddhist legacy. The transition, though a dynastic change, maintained significant continuity in institutions and ideology.

## Culture and Religion

### Buddhism as State Religion

Buddhism achieved its fullest integration into statecraft during Unified Silla. The royal court sponsored temple construction, supported the monastic order (sangha), and employed Buddhist philosophy to legitimize the political system. Monks served as advisors to the throne and maintained the kingdom's intellectual centers.

### Art and Architecture

Silla artists developed distinctive styles in:

- **Stone Sculpture**: Buddha statues, bodhisattva figures, and guardian lions (신사자, shinsaja) showed refinement and Korean aesthetic innovation.
- **Ceramic Arts**: The gradual development of Korean celadon (cheongja, 청자) began during this period, though it would reach full maturity in the Goryeo era.
- **Architecture**: Temples, pagodas, and secular buildings combined Silla and Tang influences, creating a unique Korean aesthetic.

### Language and Writing

While official documents were written in Classical Chinese (an elite medium), Koreans developed the **Idu system** (이두), a phonetic script combining Chinese characters to represent Korean sounds, and **Hyangga** poetry, which represented early Korean literary culture distinct from pure Chinese forms.

## Economy and Trade

Unified Silla's economy rested on agriculture (rice, barley, millet), with taxation based on land tenure and grain surplus. The state granted officials income rights over designated villages through the **nogeup** (녹읍) stipend-village system — briefly abolished in 689 in favor of salary-grain grants (관료전, gwallyojeon) but restored in 757 — as a primary mechanism of surplus extraction from peasant farmers.

Gyeongju at its peak (8th–9th centuries) is estimated to have reached a population of approximately 1 million, making it one of the largest cities in the world at that time, comparable to Chang'an (Tang's capital), Constantinople, and Baghdad. The capital's streets were planned on a grid modeled on Chang'an's layout; it included a West Market and East Market with stalls for silk, ceramics, and exotic imports.

International trade flourished through several channels:
- **Tang China**: Regular diplomatic embassies (사신, sasin) exchanged silk, celadon, ginseng, gold, and books. Korean students (留學生, ryuhaksaeng) studying at Tang's imperial academy numbered in the hundreds during the 8th century.
- **Arab Caliphate**: A 9th-century Arabic geographical account by Ibn Khurdādhbih's *Book of Roads and Kingdoms* (c. 846–885 CE) mentions Korea (Silla) as a wealthy, gold-abundant land where Arab merchants settled permanently because conditions were so favorable. This constitutes the earliest Arabic documentation of Korea.
- **Japan**: Silla maintained trade with the Nara and early Heian courts, exporting Buddhist texts, metalwork, and textiles; Japanese nobles collected Silla goods as luxury items.
- **Balhae (Palhae, 渤海, 698–926 CE)**: The successor state to Goguryeo north of the Taedong River maintained periodic trade contacts with Silla despite political rivalry; Balhae monks and scholars also engaged Korean Buddhist networks.

Korea's first major maritime trader, **Jang Bogo** (장보고, died c. 846 CE), established a trading empire based at the Cheonghaejin (청해진) naval garrison on Wando Island (off the South Jeolla coast, adjacent to Gyeongbuk's maritime sphere). At its height, Jang controlled much of the East Asian maritime trade triangle (Silla–Tang–Japan) and commanded a private fleet estimated at 10,000 sailors. His assassination in 846 CE by Silla court factions illustrates the tension between mercantile wealth and aristocratic power that characterized the Late Unified Silla period.

## Decline and Legacy

Unified Silla's decline resulted from internal aristocratic competition, peasant rebellion, and the rise of powerful warlords who no longer acknowledged royal authority. The transition to Goryeo in 936 represented both political discontinuity (dynastic succession) and cultural continuity (preservation of Buddhist institutions, administrative systems, and aristocratic ideology).

The Unified Silla period is celebrated in Korean historiography as an age of cultural flowering, political stability (for nearly 300 years), and the formation of a distinctive Korean Buddhist and aristocratic civilization. The era's artistic and architectural achievements—particularly Bulguksa and Seokguram—remain iconic symbols of Korean cultural heritage. The period's administrative and social structures, particularly the bone-rank system and Hwarang ideology, influenced subsequent Korean dynasties and contributed to the development of Korean civilization.
