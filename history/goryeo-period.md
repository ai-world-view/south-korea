# Goryeo Period: Korean Kingdom and Medieval Civilization

The Goryeo dynasty (高麗, 918–1392) was a medieval Korean kingdom that unified the Korean peninsula following the turbulent Later Three Kingdoms period and preceded the Joseon dynasty. Spanning nearly five centuries, Goryeo became one of East Asia's most sophisticated polities, distinguished by its Buddhist civilization, celadon ceramics, innovations in printing technology, and resilient diplomacy against successive northern empires. The English name "Korea" itself derives from "Goryeo," preserving the medieval kingdom's identity into modernity.

## Origins and the Later Three Kingdoms Context

The Goryeo dynasty emerged from the political fragmentation that followed the decline of Unified Silla (668–935). After Silla's central authority weakened in the late 9th century, regional strongmen reasserted control over peripheral territories. Three rival states contested the peninsula:

- **Later Baekje** (후백제, 900–936): Founded by Gyeon Hwon in the Jeolla region, claiming succession from the ancient Baekje kingdom.
- **Later Goguryeo** (후고구려, 901–918): Founded by the monk Gung Ye in the central and northern regions, later renamed Majin and then Taebong. Gung Ye grew increasingly tyrannical, which ultimately provoked his overthrow.
- **Later Silla**: The rump of Unified Silla, retaining the southeastern peninsula, though politically enfeebled.

Wang Geon (877–943), a capable military commander who served under Gung Ye, led a coup against the unstable ruler in 918 and established the Goryeo kingdom with his capital at Kaesong (Songdo, in present-day North Korea). Wang Geon pursued a deliberate policy of reconciliation with rival elites: he married into multiple regional aristocratic families (ultimately taking 29 queens and consorts), cultivated Buddhist institutions, and issued the "Ten Injunctions" (훈요십조) to guide his successors. By 936 he had defeated Later Baekje and received the voluntary submission of the last Silla king, Gyeongsun, completing unification.

Wang Geon adopted the name "Goryeo" for his kingdom, explicitly invoking Goguryeo's ancient northern heritage and territorial ambition—a claim that had ideological implications for future relations with Manchuria.

## Political Structure and Key Reigns

### Gwangjong and Centralization (r. 949–975)

Gwangjong (광종) undertook the most significant early reforms aimed at strengthening royal power against the entrenched aristocracy. His measures included:

- **The slave liberation edict (956)**: Many aristocrats had expanded their landholdings and private armies by illegally enslaving free people. Gwangjong's edict freed those wrongly enslaved, reducing aristocratic economic power.
- **Gwageo examination system (958)**: Modeled on Tang and Song Chinese practice, the civil-service examination (과거, gwageo) allowed talent-based recruitment into the bureaucracy, theoretically opening government to non-hereditary candidates. In practice it was monopolized by the educated elite, but it created a meritocratic ideal that shaped Korean governance for nearly a millennium.
- **Official robes by rank**: Gwangjong standardized court rank insignia, symbolically asserting the king's authority over aristocratic social hierarchy.

### Seongjong and Neo-Confucian Governance (r. 981–997)

Seongjong (성종) consolidated Goryeo's administrative structure. Influenced by Confucian adviser Choe Seungno, he established twelve administrative circuits (도), organized local government, and built a central bureaucracy modeled on Tang China's Three Departments and Six Ministries system. He also founded the Goryeo equivalent of the National Academy (Gukhagam, later Goryeo Seonggyungwan), training Confucian scholars for government service.

### Military Dictatorship Period (1170–1270)

In 1170, military officers led by Jeong Jungbu staged a coup, massacring civilian officials and inaugurating nearly a century of military rule. The military dictators installed and deposed kings at will; real power lay with the supreme military commander. The Choe clan (Choe Chungheon and descendants) dominated this junta from 1196 to 1258. This era of military rule coincided with the Mongol invasions, creating a particularly fraught political environment in which the military rulers resisted Mongol demands while ordinary Koreans bore the burden of invasion and occupation.

## Buddhism and Cultural Florescence

Buddhism was the dominant religion, cultural patron, and ideological foundation throughout the Goryeo period. The state invested heavily in Buddhist institutions, and leading monasteries accumulated vast landholdings, tax exemptions, and populations of serfs and slaves.

### Buddhist Schools and Synthesis

Goryeo Buddhism developed two major competing traditions:

- **Gyo (교)**: Scholastic or textual Buddhism, emphasizing the study of sutras and doctrinal systems.
- **Seon (선, Zen)**: Meditative Buddhism emphasizing direct insight and personal realization, introduced earlier but flourishing in Goryeo.

The monk **Uicheon** (義天, 1055–1101), a prince of the royal family, attempted to synthesize these schools through the Cheontae (Tiantai) tradition and compiled the Goryeo Tripitaka supplement (Sokjanggyeong), a massive collection of Buddhist commentaries from across East Asia. A generation later, **Jinul** (知訥, 1158–1210) founded the Jogye Order (조계종), which became the dominant school of Korean Buddhism, synthesizing Seon and Gyo traditions into a distinctly Korean synthesis that persists today as the primary form of Korean Buddhism.

### The Tripitaka Koreana

The Tripitaka Koreana (팔만대장경, "Eighty Thousand Great Repository of Teachings") is Goryeo's most celebrated technological and cultural achievement. In fact, two editions were compiled:

- **First Tripitaka (1011–1087)**: Commissioned after the Khitan invasion of 1010 as a prayer for divine protection. This edition, comprising approximately 6,000 volumes on 80,000 blocks, was destroyed when the Mongols burned the Goryeo capital in 1232.
- **Second Tripitaka (1236–1251)**: Recompiled during Mongol occupation as a renewed prayer for divine intervention. Carved onto 81,258 wooden blocks (each approximately 70 cm × 24 cm × 2.8 cm), the second edition has survived intact and is now housed at Haeinsa temple in South Gyeongsang Province, where it has been preserved for over 750 years. UNESCO inscribed it on the Memory of the World Register in 2007.

The text contains 52,330,152 characters and represents one of the most complete and accurate versions of the Chinese Buddhist canon. The organizational feat of compiling, proofreading, and inscribing this corpus across a 15-year period under the pressures of Mongol invasion is remarkable.

### Metal Movable Type

Goryeo developed metal movable type printing, predating Johannes Gutenberg's European invention (c. 1440s) by at least a century. The world's oldest extant book printed with metal movable type is the **Jikji** (직지심체요절, "Anthology of Great Buddhist Priests' Zen Teachings"), printed in 1377 at Heungdeoksa temple in Cheongju using movable metal type. The Jikji was inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2001. A copy is held at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris.

While the precise origins of Korean metal type remain debated, the technology was in use by at least the early 13th century. The 1234 printing of the "Sangjeong Gogeum Yemun" (상정고금예문) is referenced in contemporary records as using movable bronze type, though no copies survive.

## Ceramics and Material Culture

### Celadon Ceramics

Goryeo achieved its highest international renown for **celadon ceramics** (청자, cheongja)—high-fired stoneware with translucent blue-green glazes and elegant forms. The tradition developed from Chinese celadon techniques imported during the 10th century, but Korean potters rapidly developed distinctive innovations:

- **Sanggam inlay technique**: A uniquely Korean innovation (perfected by the 12th century) involving incising designs into the clay body, filling the incisions with black and white slips, and then glazing and firing. This produced intricate decorative patterns—cranes, clouds, chrysanthemums, willows—that could not be achieved by glazing alone.
- **Glaze color**: Goryeo kilns at Gangjin (South Jeolla Province) and Buan achieved the coveted "kingfisher jade" (비색, biseok) color that Chinese connoisseurs of the Song dynasty praised as among the finest in Asia.

Production peaked between the 10th and 12th centuries; quality declined after the Mongol invasions disrupted the ceramic centers and artisan populations. Today Goryeo celadon pieces are among the most prized items in major museum collections worldwide, including the National Museum of Korea, the Metropolitan Museum of Art (New York), and the British Museum.

### Other Arts

- **Metalwork**: Goryeo bronze incense burners, bronze mirrors, and gilt-bronze Buddhist implements show sophisticated metallurgical skill. Gold and silver inlay techniques were applied to lacquerware and iron vessels.
- **Buddhist painting**: Goryeo Buddhist paintings (불화, bulhwa) achieved remarkable sophistication. Hanging silk scroll paintings depicting Amitabha Buddha, Bodhisattvas, and guardian figures feature fine brushwork, gold pigments, and complex iconographic programs. Major collections are held in Japan (where many were transported) and the National Museum of Korea.
- **Silk textiles**: Goryeo textiles featuring woven patterns of birds, flowers, and auspicious symbols reached high standards and were traded internationally.

## Intellectual Life and the Gwageo Tradition

### Neo-Confucianism's Arrival

Neo-Confucian philosophy, which Zhu Xi (朱熹, 1130–1200) systematized during the Song dynasty in China, reached Goryeo in the late 13th century through scholars returning from Yuan China. The scholar **An Hyang** (1243–1306) is credited with introducing Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucianism to Goryeo after encountering it on missions to the Yuan court; the reform-minded scholars he inspired revitalized the national academy—renamed the **Seonggyungwan** (성균관) in 1298—as a center of Confucian learning.

Neo-Confucianism challenged Buddhism's dominance by offering an alternative philosophical and ethical framework for governance: one that emphasized filial piety, hierarchical social relationships, meritocratic bureaucracy, and secular governance rather than ritual piety and monastic institutions. By the late Goryeo period, a faction of Neo-Confucian reformers—including **Jeong Dojeon** (鄭道傳, 1342–1398)—would become architects of the Joseon dynasty.

### Gwageo and Education

The gwageo examination comprised multiple stages: a preliminary examination, a regional examination, and a final court examination. Success entitled candidates to government positions. The curriculum required mastery of the Confucian classics (Four Books and Five Classics), composition of poetry in classical Chinese, and knowledge of history and statecraft. In practice, wealthy aristocratic families dominated because the educational preparation required was expensive and time-consuming.

## Foreign Relations and the Mongol Crisis

### Khitan Invasions (993–1019)

Goryeo's first major external challenge came from the Khitan Liao dynasty (916–1125), which invaded three times between 993 and 1019:

1. **First invasion (993)**: General **Seo Hui** negotiated brilliantly, convincing the Khitan commander Xiao Sunning to withdraw in exchange for Goryeo's promise to sever ties with Song China and establish tributary relations with Liao. Goryeo gained the territory between the Chongchon River and the Yalu River.
2. **Second invasion (1010)**: After Goryeo delayed fulfilling diplomatic obligations, Liao Emperor Shengzong personally led a 400,000-man army. The Goryeo capital Kaesong was temporarily occupied and burned, but Goryeo forces eventually repelled the invasion.
3. **Third invasion (1018–1019)**: A Liao force of 100,000 men under Xiao Bai invaded; General **Gang Gam-chan** won a decisive victory at the **Battle of Gwiju** (귀주대첩, 1019), destroying the Liao army. This battle is one of the great Korean military victories and established Goryeo's defensive credibility.

Following these conflicts, Goryeo maintained tributary relations with Liao while resuming unofficial trade relations with Song China—a dual diplomacy that preserved the kingdom's strategic flexibility.

### Jurchen Jin and Diplomatic Balance

When the Jurchen Jin dynasty (1115–1234) overthrew Liao, Goryeo adjusted its diplomatic posture, accepting Jin as the dominant northern power. The Jurchens demanded similar tributary recognition. Goryeo navigated this complex environment by maintaining formal tributary relations with successive northern empires while preserving internal autonomy.

### Mongol Invasions (1231–1259)

The Mongol invasions were the most devastating external challenge Goryeo faced. Ögedei Khan—Genghis (Chinggis) Khan's son and successor—launched the first invasion in 1231, and later Möngke Khan's forces continued the campaign. In total, the Mongols invaded Goryeo **six times** between 1231 and 1259.

The military dictatorship under Choe Woo (최우) pursued a strategy of resistance: in 1232 the court relocated to **Ganghwa Island** (강화도) in the Yellow Sea, where the Mongol cavalry—ineffective at amphibious operations—could not follow. For 28 years the court remained on the island while the Mongol armies ravaged the mainland. The destruction was immense: cities burned, peasant populations massacred or enslaved, agricultural infrastructure ruined. Contemporary Korean accounts record over 200,000 captives taken to Mongolia. The first Tripitaka was destroyed.

In 1259, King Gojong finally submitted to Mongol authority, ending formal military resistance. His successor **Wonjong** (r. 1259–1274) returned the court to Kaesong and formalized tributary relations with the Mongol Yuan dynasty.

### The Goryeo-Yuan Period (1273–1356)

Goryeo became a **semi-autonomous tributary state** of Yuan (the Mongol empire in China). This subordination took multiple forms:

- **Royal marriages**: Yuan emperors demanded that Goryeo crown princes marry Mongol princesses before ascending to the throne. Kings added Mongolian names and wore Mongolian dress at the Yuan court. For approximately 80 years (1260s–1350s) every Goryeo king had a Mongol mother.
- **Military service**: Goryeo provided troops, ships, and logistical support for Yuan's **two attempted invasions of Japan** (1274 and 1281). Both invasions failed—the second fleet of approximately 4,400 ships and 140,000 men was largely destroyed by typhoons (the "divine wind," kamikaze, 神風) that the Japanese credited with saving their homeland.
- **Tribute demands**: Regular tribute in grain, cloth, gold, silver, and human beings (falconers, skilled artisans, and women for the Yuan court) burdened the Goryeo economy.
- **The "Jeongdong Branch Secretariat"**: Yuan established a supervisory office in Goryeo that functioned as a direct administrative arm of Yuan authority.

Despite subordination, Korean aristocrats who served at the Yuan court gained cosmopolitan exposure to Mongolian, Chinese, and Central Asian cultures, influencing later Goryeo art, fashion, and intellectual life.

### Restoration and Late Goryeo Revival (1356–1388)

When the Red Turban rebellions (홍건적) weakened Yuan's control of China in the 1350s, **King Gongmin** (r. 1351–1374) seized the opportunity. In 1356 he expelled the Jeongdong Branch Secretariat, executed pro-Mongol aristocrats, and attempted to recover lost northern territories. General **Yi Ja-chun** and his son **Yi Seong-gye** (later the founder of Joseon) served in these northern campaigns.

Gongmin also attempted social reforms under the Buddhist monk **Shin Don** (신돈), including land reform that redistributed illegally seized estates and freed improperly enslaved people. These reforms provoked fierce aristocratic resistance; Gongmin was assassinated in 1374, and Shin Don had already been executed in 1371.

## Society, Economy, and Daily Life

### Social Hierarchy

Goryeo society was organized in a hierarchical structure that would crystallize further in the Joseon period:

- **Royal family** (왕족): The Wang clan and close relatives.
- **Aristocracy** (貴族, gwijok): Families that dominated government through the gwageo and hereditary privilege. The key distinction in late Goryeo between military (무반, muban) and civil (문반, munban) lineages would evolve into the Joseon yangban (兩班) class.
- **Jungin** (중인): Middle class of lower officials, technical specialists (physicians, translators, accountants), and local administrators.
- **Yangin** (양인): Commoners—free peasants, artisans, and merchants who paid taxes and performed military service.
- **Cheonmin** (천민) / Nobi (노비): The unfree underclass, including state and private slaves. The nobi system was extensive: temples, aristocratic families, and the state all owned slaves who performed agricultural and household labor. The proportion of slaves in Goryeo's population is debated but may have reached 10–30% in some periods.

### Agricultural Economy

Agriculture, primarily wet-rice cultivation supplemented by barley, millet, and other grains, formed the economic base. Aristocratic families and Buddhist temples controlled vast landholdings worked by serfs, slaves, and tenant farmers. The **Jeonsi System** (전시과) allocated land revenue rights (not ownership) to government officials as salary; this system underwent several revisions as aristocratic families subverted it to accumulate hereditary landed wealth.

### Maritime Trade

Kaesong's port of **Byeokrando** (벽란도) on the Yesong River served as a major international trading hub. Arab merchants (referred to in Korean records as "Daeseong" people) traded in Goryeo—a likely source of Western awareness of Korea's existence and possibly a channel through which the name "Goryeo" reached the Islamic world and eventually Europe as "Corea." Goryeo exported celadon, ginseng, paper, silk, and pine nuts; it imported copper coins, silk fabrics, books, and luxury goods from Song China and the Islamic world.

### Buddhism's Economic Role

Buddhist monasteries functioned as significant economic actors: they operated **sa-changsaek** (사창색) lending institutions, charged interest on grain and commodity loans, and accumulated land through donations, purchases, and foreclosure. This institutional wealth funded temple construction, artwork, and printing, but also drew Neo-Confucian criticism for diverting resources from productive agriculture and governance.

## Language and Script

During Goryeo, **Classical Chinese** (한문, hanmun) dominated official writing, literature, and scholarly discourse. The educated elite composed poetry, histories, and government documents in classical Chinese. However, Korean vernacular expression increasingly appeared in certain contexts:

- **Hyangga** (향가): A form of Korean vernacular poetry (written using a phonetic system of Chinese characters to represent Korean sounds) carried over from the Silla period. Eleven Goryeo hyangga survive, collected in the *Gyunyeo Jeon* (균여전, 967).
- **Goryeo Gayo** (고려 가요): Vernacular songs in the Korean language, including the celebrated "Cheongsan Byeolgok" (청산별곡) and "Gasi-ri" (가시리), which express longing, nature imagery, and emotional themes. These were transmitted orally and written down only in the later Joseon period.

The invention of **Hangul** by King Sejong of Joseon would not occur until 1443, but Goryeo's growing vernacular literary tradition demonstrates Korean's vitality as a spoken and creative language beneath the classical Chinese veneer.

## Waegu Pirates and Coastal Insecurity

In the 13th–14th centuries, **Waegu** (왜구, Japanese sea rovers/pirates) repeatedly raided Korean coastal areas, attacking fishing villages, grain shipments, and coastal towns. The raids intensified after 1350, driven partly by political instability in Japan during the Nanboku-cho civil war. By the 1370s, Waegu raids reached inland, threatening Goryeo's tax grain shipments and coastal prefectures.

Goryeo generals, including **Choe Yeong** and **Yi Seong-gye**, launched punitive expeditions against the raiders' base islands. Yi Seong-gye's victorious campaigns against the Waegu significantly enhanced his military prestige and political standing—contributing to his ability to stage the 1388 coup.

## Decline and Transition to Joseon

### Internal Fragmentation

Late Goryeo was characterized by intense factional conflict, land concentration, and institutional dysfunction:

- **Land accumulation**: Powerful families had seized enormous amounts of public land, reducing government revenues and impoverishing peasants. Tax evasion was endemic.
- **Buddhist excess**: Critics condemned the monasteries' wealth, the monks' political influence, and Buddhist ceremonies' expense as draining national resources. Neo-Confucian reformers like **Jeong Mong-ju** (鄭夢周, 1337–1392) and **Jeong Dojeon** debated whether Buddhism or Confucianism should guide the state—a debate with profound political stakes.
- **Succession instability**: Assassination, deposition, and disputed successions plagued the late Goryeo court. Kings U and Chang (who may not have been genuine members of the Wang royal line) were deposed in 1389.

### The 1388 Crisis and Joseon's Founding

In 1388, King U ordered General Choe Yeong and General Yi Seong-gye to attack Ming China's northern territory (Liaodong), which Ming had recently claimed. Yi Seong-gye, judging the campaign militarily unwinnable and politically disastrous, turned his army around at **Wihwa Island** (위화도) on the Yalu River and marched back to Kaesong—the famous **Wihwado Hoegun** (위화도 회군, "Retreat from Wihwa Island"). This coup effectively ended Goryeo rule.

Yi Seong-gye spent the next four years consolidating power, conducting land reform (redistributing illegally concentrated estates through the **gwajeon system** of 1390), and eliminating rivals, including the great loyalist Jeong Mong-ju (assassinated at Seonjuk Bridge in 1392). In **July 1392**, Yi was elevated to king by a coalition of reform-minded Neo-Confucian officials, ending the Goryeo dynasty after 474 years. He established the **Joseon dynasty** with a new capital at Hanyang (present-day Seoul) and renamed the kingdom after the ancient Joseon.

## Legacy and Historical Memory

Goryeo's contributions shaped Korean civilization across multiple dimensions:

**Name and identity**: The English word "Korea" derives from "Goryeo" via Arabic and Persian intermediaries who encountered the kingdom through trade. "Koryo" remains a common alternative romanization used especially in North Korea and in historical scholarship.

**Printing technology**: The Tripitaka Koreana and Jikji represent landmark achievements in information technology. Their survival demonstrates both the sophistication of Goryeo's woodblock carvers and metalworkers and the historical accident that preserved Haeinsa's storage halls from fire and invasion for 750 years.

**Buddhism's endurance**: Despite Joseon's Neo-Confucian suppression of Buddhism (reducing monasteries' landholdings, excluding monks from the capital), Goryeo Buddhism's institutional structures, doctrinal traditions, and artistic heritage persisted. The Jogye Order remains the dominant form of Korean Buddhism today.

**Administrative precedent**: The gwageo system, civil bureaucracy, and provincial structure established during Goryeo were refined—not abolished—by Joseon. The same bureaucratic logic of examination-based meritocracy (in theory), provincial governance, and centralized royal authority formed the Joseon state's skeleton.

**Ceramics heritage**: Goryeo celadon was largely forgotten during the Joseon period (which favored white porcelain, or baekja) but rediscovered by collectors and scholars in the 19th–20th centuries. Today it is recognized internationally as one of the great traditions of ceramic art.

**Cultural synthesis model**: Goryeo demonstrated that Korean civilization could absorb Chinese administrative, philosophical, and artistic models while developing distinctively Korean expressions—in celadon glaze color, in Buddhist doctrinal synthesis, in vernacular song. This pattern of creative adaptation within an East Asian cultural framework defined Korean cultural history.
