# Gwangju Uprising (May 18 Incident)

**Category**: History
**Key figures**: Chun Doo-hwan, Roh Tae-woo, Kim Dae-jung, Park Chung-hee, General Chung Ho-yung, General Chung Seung-hwa

## Summary

The Gwangju Uprising (May 18–27, 1980), formally designated the **May 18th Democratic Uprising** (오월항쟁, *Owol Hangjaeng*) and also known as the **Gwangju Democratization Movement** (광주민주화운동), was a ten-day armed popular resistance in Gwangju, South Jeolla Province, that stands as the bloodiest confrontation between South Korean citizens and their own military government in the twentieth century. The uprising erupted in the immediate wake of Major General **Chun Doo-hwan**'s declaration of expanded martial law on May 17, 1980—the culmination of an internal coup that Chun had been consolidating since December 12, 1979, following the assassination of President **Park Chung-hee** on October 26, 1979.

### Political Context

Park Chung-hee's assassination by KCIA director Kim Jae-gyu created an immediate power vacuum in a state governed for eighteen years under a blend of developmental authoritarianism and heavy military influence. Civil society—universities, labor unions, opposition parties—briefly hoped that the vacuum would yield political liberalization. Between October 1979 and May 1980, a period sometimes called the "Seoul Spring," popular demonstrations demanding a new constitution, lifting of martial law, and free elections spread across the country. Kim Dae-jung (then a leading opposition politician and Gwangju-born dissident) and other political figures pushed for democratic transition.

Chun Doo-hwan, then commander of the Defense Security Command (DSC), moved decisively to foreclose this opening. On **December 12, 1979**, he orchestrated the "Coup Within a Coup" (*12/12 Coup*), arresting Army Chief of Staff General Chung Seung-hwa without presidential authorization and installing loyalists in key military positions. By spring 1980, Chun effectively controlled the military apparatus. When student protests intensified in May—with 50,000 students marching in Seoul on May 15—Chun declared sweeping martial law on **May 17** at midnight, closed universities, arrested Kim Dae-jung and other opposition leaders, and dissolved the National Assembly.

### Chronology of Events

**May 18** — Students at Chonnam National University in Gwangju gathered to protest the university closure ordered under martial law. At the university gate, they encountered paratroopers from the 7th Airborne Brigade, who used batons and rifle butts to disperse students. The students retreated into the city center; paratroopers followed, and violence escalated. Downtown, soldiers beat not only protesters but bystanders, shopkeepers, and passersby indiscriminately—acts that enraged the city's population beyond the initial student core.

**May 19–20** — Protests swelled as ordinary citizens—workers, merchants, women's groups—joined students in response to the military's brutality. The crowd at the Provincial Hall plaza and Geumnam Boulevard numbered in the tens of thousands. Paratroopers used bayonets and live fire against the crowd; early fatalities occurred on the afternoon of May 20. Thousands of citizens mobilized taxis, buses, and private vehicles in a convoy to protest the violence, a moment that became iconic in accounts of the uprising.

**May 21** — Military units on Geumnam Boulevard opened sustained live fire into the crowd, killing dozens and wounding hundreds. In response, citizens raided police armories and National Guard weapons depots in surrounding towns—including Naju, Hwasun, Yeongam, and Muan—arming themselves with M1 carbines, rifles, and other weapons. By evening, the army had withdrawn from central Gwangju and citizens controlled the city. The Citizens' Army (*Minjung Siwidae*) and a Citizens' Settlement Committee were formed to maintain order.

**May 22–25** — Gwangju was effectively under citizen control, a period sometimes called the "liberated zone." Citizens organized food distribution, medical aid for the wounded, and communication committees. Negotiations between the Citizens' Settlement Committee and military authorities repeatedly failed. The military cordon around the city prevented information from entering or leaving; national media broadcast only the government's characterization of the uprising as a communist-instigated riot led by Kim Dae-jung.

**May 26–27** — Early on May 27, South Korean Army units—the 20th Division, supported by tanks—conducted **Operation Fascinating Vacation** (*Hwaryeohan Hyuga Jakjeon*), a coordinated assault to retake the Provincial Hall. A smaller group of armed citizen fighters who had voted the night before to remain and defend the building were killed or captured. The last defenders of the Provincial Hall were overcome by approximately 5 a.m. on May 27. The uprising was suppressed.

### Casualties and Repression

Official Korean government figures at the time acknowledged only 144–191 deaths. Independent investigations by the Korean government (1995–1997 special committee), victim families, and human rights organizations have documented at least **165 confirmed deaths** from combat, with credible estimates placing total fatalities—including those who died from injuries, torture, or in detention—between **200 and 600**. More than 1,000 were wounded. At least 1,394 people were arrested; many were tortured during interrogation. Trials were swift and military; Kim Dae-jung, accused of instigating the uprising though he was arrested on May 17 before it began, was sentenced to death (later commuted to life imprisonment and then exile under American pressure).

### U.S. Role and International Dimensions

The United States' posture toward the Gwangju Uprising became a source of lasting controversy in Korean-American relations. South Korean forces participating in the crackdown were technically under the Combined Forces Command (CFC), a joint U.S.-Korean command structure. The U.S. approved the release of the 20th Division from CFC operational control for use in domestic operations. American officials in Washington were aware of Chun's plans and did not object. A U.S. diplomatic cable from May 22 acknowledged the killings but prioritized "stability" over confrontation with Chun's government. This silence—interpreted by many Koreans as tacit American endorsement of the crackdown—fueled deep anti-American sentiment among South Korean student and democratic movement activists throughout the 1980s, contributing to the rise of anti-U.S. nationalism in Korean civil society.

## Significance

### Catalyst for the Democracy Movement

The Gwangju Uprising did not end South Korea's authoritarian era, but it permanently altered its moral landscape. Chun Doo-hwan assumed formal control of the government in August 1980 and ruled until 1988, but the memory of Gwangju became the defining grievance of the entire decade's democratic movement. Every subsequent protest—the 1981 Busan labor movement, the 1984–1985 student demonstrations, and ultimately the **June Democratic Struggle of 1987**—was infused with the memory of Gwangju's dead. Photographs and eyewitness testimonies that circulated underground through *samizdat*-style pamphlets and illegal videos radicalized an entire generation of activists. Lee Han-yeol, a 21-year-old student killed at a 1987 protest after being struck by a tear gas canister, became the symbol of 1987 much as the Gwangju victims had been the symbol of 1980.

### Kim Dae-jung and Moral Authority

Kim Dae-jung's death sentence was commuted after international pressure, and he was eventually released and exiled to the United States. His direct association with Gwangju—despite his absence from the city during the uprising—became central to his political identity. When he won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2000, his acceptance referenced the struggle for democracy and human rights of which Gwangju was the decisive episode. His presidency (1998–2003) was the first peaceful transfer of power from one political party to another in South Korean history.

### Legal Accountability and Memorialization

In **1995**, the Korean National Assembly passed special laws enabling prosecution of those responsible for the December 12, 1979 coup and the May 1980 crackdown. Chun Doo-hwan was arrested in November 1995 and tried for treason and sedition; in 1996 he was initially sentenced to death (later commuted to life imprisonment, then pardoned in 1997 by President Kim Young-sam at the incoming Kim Dae-jung's request). Roh Tae-woo received a 22.5-year sentence, also later commuted and pardoned. The prosecutions were the first in Asia in which a sitting government successfully tried its own former military leaders for rebellion.

The **May 18th National Cemetery** (*Gungnip 5·18 Minju Myoji*) was dedicated in 1997 in Gwangju, and **May 18th** was recognized as a national memorial day. UNESCO inscribed the documentary heritage of the May 18th Democratic Uprising—including archival documents, photographs, film footage, and audio recordings—on its **Memory of the World Register in 2011**, recognizing the uprising's global significance as a human rights and democratization event.

### Cultural Legacy

Gwangju's memory has generated a substantial body of Korean literature, film, and visual art. Author **Han Kang**'s novel *Human Acts* (2014; English translation 2016) reconstructs the uprising and its aftermath through multiple perspectives, portraying the physical and psychological dimensions of state violence. Director **Im Sang-soo**'s *A Petal* (1996) and the 2007 film *May 18* (*화려한 휴가*, literally "Glorious Holiday") dramatized the events for mass audiences. The song "Arirang Variations" and other musical works have commemorated the uprising within Korean folk and protest music traditions. These cultural productions have extended the memory of Gwangju beyond political discourse into a broader shared national narrative.
