# Joseon Dynasty

**Category**: History  
**Key figures**: Yi Seong-gye (Taejo), Sejong the Great, Sejo, Gwanghae-gun, Jeongjo, Dasan Jeong Yak-yong  
**Period**: 1392–1910 (518 years)

## Summary

The Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) was Korea's longest-reigning kingdom, succeeding [Goryeo](../history/goryeo-period.md) and enduring for 518 years until Japanese colonization ended the monarchy in 1910. Founded by the military commander Yi Seong-gye following the Wihwado Retreat of 1388—when he turned his army back against the pro-Mongol court rather than attacking Ming China—Joseon established a centralized Confucian state grounded in Neo-Confucian ideology, which permeated governance, education, and social hierarchy in ways that persist in Korean culture today.

The dynasty's history divides broadly into three phases: an early period of institutional construction and cultural flourishing (1392–c. 1550); a middle period marked by factional strife, devastating foreign invasions, and partial recovery (c. 1550–1750); and a late period of intellectual renaissance, mounting external pressure, and eventual collapse under Japanese imperialism (1750–1910). Over its five centuries, Joseon produced the Korean alphabet Hangul (1443), a sophisticated civil service examination system, and significant advances in astronomy, cartography, and printing—making it one of the most culturally productive pre-modern states in East Asia.

## Foundation and Early Joseon (1392–c. 1450)

Yi Seong-gye, a Goryeo general who had risen to prominence fighting Japanese pirates (waegu) and Jurchen raiders along Korea's northern frontier, established the new dynasty in 1392 after deposing King Gongyang. He took the reign title Taejo and moved the capital from Kaesong to Hanyang (present-day [Seoul](../geography/seoul.md)) in 1394, constructing Gyeongbokgung Palace and the city walls that still partly define the old city. The new capital's feng shui location—enclosed by four mountains, centered on the Han River—was chosen with Confucian geomantic care.

The dynasty's ideological break from Goryeo was decisive. Where Goryeo had patronized [Buddhism](../culture-society/religion-and-buddhism.md) as the state religion, Joseon adopted Neo-Confucianism (Seongnihak) as its governing philosophy, following the model of China's Ming dynasty. Buddhist temples were stripped of land grants and monks excluded from Hanyang; Confucian academies (hyanggyo) replaced Buddhist monasteries as centers of learning. The leading early theorist was Jeong Do-jeon (1342–1398), who designed much of Joseon's institutional framework before being executed in the Strife of 1398 (the first of many succession crises).

### The Reign of Sejong the Great (1418–1450)

The fourth Joseon king, Sejong (r. 1418–1450), is widely regarded as the greatest ruler in Korean history. His reign saw an extraordinary concentration of royal initiative and scholarly achievement:

- **Hangul (1443)**: Sejong convened the Hall of Worthies (Jiphyeonjeon), a royal academy of scholars, and directed the creation of a new Korean phonetic writing system. Promulgated in 1446 as *Hunminjeongeum* ("The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People"), Hangul comprised 17 consonants and 11 vowels designed to represent Korean phonology with scientific precision. The script was explicitly egalitarian: its preface stated it was created so that even the humblest person could learn to write within days. Today Hangul remains one of the most phonetically accurate alphabets in use and is central to Korean national identity.
- **Scientific and technical advances**: Sejong established the Office of Astronomy and Meteorology, supported the invention of the rain gauge (cheugugi, 1441)—the world's first standardized precipitation instrument—and sponsored the production of the armillary sphere, sundials, and water clocks (jagyeongnu). Korea's printing technology, building on movable metal type developed during Goryeo, was refined further.
- **Music and ritual**: Sejong composed court music (aak) and formalized ritual music for state ceremonies, some of which survives in performance today.
- **Agriculture and medicine**: He commissioned the *Nongsa jikseol* (1429), a manual of Korean farming techniques based on local conditions rather than Chinese models, and the *Hyangyak jipseongbang* (1433), a medical encyclopedia drawing on indigenous Korean medicinal plants.

## Social Structure and the Gwageo Examination System

Joseon society was organized around a four-tiered hierarchy: yangban (scholar-officials), jungin (technical specialists: doctors, translators, accountants), sangmin (commoners: farmers, artisans, merchants), and cheonmin (the lowest class, including slaves). Slaves (nobi) could constitute up to one-third of the population in some periods and were legally property, though their status was more complex than chattel slavery in other societies.

The yangban class reproduced itself through the gwageo civil service examination system, which had Goryeo antecedents but reached its fullest development under Joseon. The examinations comprised three levels: the local (sogwa), regional (haegwa), and final (daegwa) tests. Passing the highest civil examination in the Confucian classics (mungwa) opened access to positions in the central bureaucracy, the Six Ministries (Yukjo), and the State Council (Uijeongbu). In theory the examination was open to any male commoner; in practice, the expense of years of study and the social networks required to advance confined success largely to hereditary yangban families.

This examination culture generated enormous investment in education and created a highly literate elite, but also produced an over-supply of degree-holders competing for limited positions and entrenched social stratification. The Neo-Confucian emphasis on the Classics shaped Korean intellectual life for centuries: the leading texts were the Four Books (Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, Analects, Mencius) and the Five Classics, supplemented by works of the Song Chinese philosopher Zhu Xi (1130–1200), whose interpretations defined orthodoxy.

## Factionalism and the Four Major Literati Purges (Sahwa)

The mid-Joseon period was scarred by factional conflicts among yangban factions at court, partly driven by ideological disputes over Neo-Confucian orthodoxy and partly by competition for royal patronage. Four major purges (sahwa) between 1498 and 1545 killed or exiled hundreds of scholars:

1. **Muo Sahwa (1498)**: King Yeonsangun (r. 1494–1506) executed scholars associated with Kim Jong-jik and the Sarim (country-based Neo-Confucian scholars) faction.
2. **Gapja Sahwa (1504)**: Yeonsangun, increasingly despotic, conducted another purge.
3. **Gimyo Sahwa (1519)**: The reformist scholar Jo Gwang-jo and his followers were purged under Jungjong's reign when conservatives persuaded the king he was a threat.
4. **Eulsa Sahwa (1545)**: A purge early in Myeongjong's reign in which the royal in-law faction of Yun Won-hyeong (the "Lesser Yun") destroyed its rivals (the "Greater Yun").

These purges weakened the reform-oriented Sarim scholars temporarily, but by the late 16th century they had consolidated their position and dominated the bureaucracy, where they split into rival factions—Easterners and Westerners—whose splits generated further sub-factions (Northerners, Southerners, Old Doctrine, Young Doctrine) that characterized court politics into the 18th century.

## The Imjin War (1592–1598): Japanese Invasion

The most catastrophic event in Joseon's middle period was the Japanese invasions led by Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Hideyoshi, having unified Japan, attempted to conquer Ming China using Korea as a land bridge. In the First Invasion (Imjin War, 1592–1596), approximately 160,000 Japanese troops under generals Konishi Yukinaga and Kato Kiyomasa overran Korea in weeks. Hanyang fell within three weeks; King Seonjo fled to the northern border.

Korea's military infrastructure had atrophied during a century of relative peace. However, resistance coalesced through two channels:

- **Naval genius of Yi Sun-sin**: Admiral Yi Sun-sin (1545–1598) used the turtle ship (geobukseon), an iron-plated warship, and superior naval tactics to defeat the Japanese fleet repeatedly at Okpo (1592), Hansando (1592), and Myeongnyang (1597). Yi's campaigns severed Japanese supply lines, making conquest impossible. He remains Korea's most celebrated military hero.
- **Righteous armies (uibyeong)**: Civilian militias organized by local yangban mounted guerrilla resistance across the peninsula.

Ming China intervened in 1593 with 40,000–50,000 troops, stabilizing the front. Negotiations failed, and a Second Invasion (Jeongyu War, 1597–1598) followed until Hideyoshi's death in 1598 ended the campaigns. The wars left much of the peninsula devastated: population estimates suggest Korea lost 1–2 million people to death, abduction (over 100,000 Koreans were taken to Japan as craftsmen and laborers), and disease; agricultural production collapsed; and the bureaucratic and military infrastructure required decades to rebuild.

## Manchu Invasions and Qing Suzerainty

The early 17th century brought further catastrophe. The rising Jurchen state (later renamed Manchu and then Qing China) invaded Korea twice: the Jeongmyo War (1627) and the Byeongja Horan (1636–1637). In the 1636 invasion, approximately 120,000 Qing troops crossed the frozen Yalu River in winter. King Injo fled to Namhan Mountain Fortress (Namhansanseong) where, besieged for 45 days in bitter cold without adequate provisions, he was forced to surrender in January 1637 by performing the ritual of three kneelings and nine prostrations (samgwe-gugodu) before the Qing emperor Hong Taiji at Samjeondo—a humiliation that burned into Korean collective memory.

Korea became a Qing tributary state, sending annual tribute missions to Beijing and providing troops for Qing campaigns. This subordination generated intense intellectual tension: Korean Confucian scholars, who had viewed Ming China as the civilizational center, were forced to accept that the Manchus—barbarians in the Neo-Confucian framework—had conquered China. Some Korean thinkers responded with a form of cultural nationalism, arguing that Korea had now become the true custodian of Confucian civilization (*Joseon as the center of the world*).

## Cultural and Scientific Achievements

Beyond Hangul and Sejong's innovations, Joseon produced a rich cultural legacy:

- **Korean Confucian philosophy**: The philosopher Yi Hwang (Toegye, 1501–1570) systematized Korean Neo-Confucianism in the *Ten Diagrams on Sage Learning*; his exchange with the younger scholar Gi Dae-seung sparked the Four-Seven Debate—a philosophically sophisticated dispute over the relationship between the Four Beginnings (moral sprouts) and the Seven Emotions—which Yi I (Yulgok, 1536–1584) later carried forward in his own correspondence with Seong Hon. These debates remained central to Korean philosophical education for centuries.
- **Ceramics**: Joseon white porcelain (baekja), characterized by restrained, pure forms in contrast to Goryeo's celadon, became the dynasty's signature artistic medium. Buncheong ware (powder-celadon), produced in the 15th–16th centuries, influenced Japanese tea ceremony aesthetics following the Imjin War.
- **Literature and poetry**: Sijo (three-line lyric poems) reached their classical form during Joseon; the novelist Kim Manjung (1637–1692) composed *Guwun mong* (A Dream of Nine Clouds), considered the first great Korean novel.
- **Historical records**: The Annals of the Joseon Dynasty (Joseon Wangjo Sillok), compiled reign by reign, constitute one of the most detailed continuous historical records in the world, covering every reign from Taejo to Cheoljong (1392–1863) across some 1,893 volumes.

## Late Joseon: Silhak and Encounter with the West

The 18th century brought both intellectual renaissance and mounting crisis. A new school of practical scholarship (Silhak, "practical learning") emerged as a critique of speculative Neo-Confucian orthodoxy. Silhak scholars such as Yu Hyeong-won (1622–1673), Yi Ik (1681–1763), and Dasan Jeong Yak-yong (1762–1836) called for institutional reforms: land redistribution, expansion of opportunity beyond the yangban class, and empirical inquiry into science, economics, and agriculture.

Jeong Yak-yong (pen name Dasan) was the most prolific Silhak thinker, composing over 500 works during his 18-year exile imposed after he converted to Catholicism—one of the early signs of Western religious and intellectual influence entering Korea through China. His works addressed statecraft and land reform (the *Gyeongse yupyo*, alongside his "village land" *yeojeon* proposal for collective farming), penal reform (the *Heumheum sinseo*), and local administration (the *Mongmin simseo*).

Catholic Christianity reached Korea via Joseon's tribute missions to Beijing in the late 18th century. Korean scholars initially embraced it as Western learning (seohak) before the state identified it as heretical. Four major persecutions (1801, 1839, 1846, 1866) executed hundreds of Korean Catholics and French missionaries; in 1866 alone, approximately 8,000 Korean Catholics were killed. These suppressions brought Korea into conflict with France, which launched a punitive expedition (1866, Byeongin Yangyo) that Korean defenders repulsed at Ganghwa Island.

The late 19th century was defined by the Heungseon Daewongun's regency (1863–1873) and efforts to resist foreign encroachment through isolation; followed by the Meiji Japan–dictated Ganghwa Treaty (1876), which forcibly opened Korea to trade; and the factional struggle between the pro-Chinese conservatives and pro-Japanese reformers that culminated in the Gabo Reforms (1894–1896) and the Eulmi Incident (1895, the assassination of Queen Min by Japanese agents). Korea was declared the Empire of Korea (Daehan Jeguk) in 1897, a short-lived attempt at sovereign assertion before the Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty of 1910 extinguished Joseon's existence. See [Japanese Colonial Period](../history/japanese-colonial-period.md).

## Significance

Joseon's five-century reign shaped nearly every dimension of Korean civilization. The dynasty's educational meritocracy, Confucian ethics, and administrative frameworks left deep imprints on Korean approaches to hierarchy, family structure, gender relations, and the social prestige of scholarship. Hangul's creation remains the most transformative single event in Korean linguistic history, enabling mass literacy and national literature. The dynasty's experience of devastating invasion, partial recovery, intellectual self-renewal, and eventual subordination to outside powers set the terms for Korea's modern rupture—partition, colonization, and the postcolonial nation-building that shaped contemporary South Korea.

Joseon's legacy is visible in contemporary Korean culture: in the Confucian reverence for education and examination success that drives Korea's educational intensity (see [Education and Universities](../culture-society/education-and-universities.md)), in the hierarchical formality of Korean social interactions, in the architectural heritage of Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung palaces, and in the Hangul script itself.
