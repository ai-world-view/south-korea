# Candlelight Revolution (2016–2017)

The **Candlelight Revolution** (촛불혁명), also called the **Candlelight Protests** or **November-to-March Impeachment Movement** (촛불집회), was a sustained mass protest movement spanning November 2016 through March 2017 that led to the impeachment and constitutional removal of South Korean President **Park Geun-hye**. The movement was named for the candlelit vigils held by millions of citizens in Seoul's downtown Gwanghwamun area and other cities, and represented the largest scale of civic mobilization in South Korea since the 1987 June Democratic Struggle. The movement's political outcome — the removal of a sitting president through mass civilian pressure, court impeachment, and Constitutional Court action — demonstrated the resilience of South Korea's democratic institutions and set a precedent for civic power that would be invoked again during the 2024 martial law crisis.

## Background: The Choi Soon-sil Scandal

### The Scandal Emerges

On **February 25, 2013**, Park Geun-hye was inaugurated as South Korea's 18th and first female president, running on a conservative platform emphasizing economic democratization and welfare expansion. From early in her term, a mysterious figure named **Choi Soon-sil** (최순실) attracted media attention as an unusual influence on presidential policy and personnel decisions. Choi — the daughter of **Choi Tae-min**, a shamanistic cult leader who had served as a spiritual mentor to Park after the assassination of her mother in 1974 — held no official government position yet reportedly accessed classified documents, participated in high-level meetings, and influenced cabinet appointments and policy direction. The relationship traced back decades: Park had known Choi's family since childhood, and investigators later concluded that Choi had exerted influence over Park for most of her adult political life. Over the years, journalists and opposition figures alleged that Choi used her proximity to the president for personal enrichment and wielded extraordinary influence over state affairs without democratic accountability.

The allegations crystallized in **October 2016** when the news outlet **JTBC** broadcast revelations from a leaked tablet computer allegedly used by Choi. The tablet contained:
- Draft versions of presidential speeches before they were made
- Screenshots of sensitive documents marked "top secret"
- Personal schedules showing Choi's attendance at presidential meetings
- Evidence of Choi's involvement in the selection of cabinet ministers
- Details of Choi's conversations about national and corporate policy

### The Scandal's Scale

Investigations revealed that Choi had leveraged her influence with President Park to extract massive donations from **Samsung**, **Hyundai Motor**, **SK Innovation**, and other major chaebols, ostensibly for cultural foundation and sports projects that Choi herself chaired. The foundations — **Mir Foundation** (미르재단) and **K-Sports Foundation** (케이스포츠재단), both established in 2015–2016 — became vehicles for funneling corporate money with implicit pressure from the presidential office, receiving approximately **77.4 billion won** (roughly $68 million USD) in total from 53 major corporations. **Samsung Group** alone transferred approximately **43 billion won** ($38 million) to Choi-linked entities, including funding for Choi's daughter's equestrian training and a luxury apartment in Germany — a payment that later became the centerpiece of the criminal trial of Samsung heir **Lee Jae-yong** (이재용), arrested in February 2017 on bribery charges. Choi's family members were placed in lucrative corporate positions at Samsung and other firms through presidential intervention. Furthermore, Choi's daughter, **Chung Yoo-ra** (정유라), was admitted to **Ewha Womans University** despite academic performance that would not normally qualify, receiving preferential treatment in attendance, thesis evaluation, and equestrian credits — admissions misconduct that directly implicated the university's president, who later resigned.

The scandal touched on multiple dimensions of Korean corporate and political life:
- **Chaebol-state nexus**: The ease with which the president could coerce donations from major corporations without transparent justification or legal process
- **Gender and power**: The unchecked influence of a woman with no formal position, which also fueled feminist critiques of male-dominated power structures that had enabled her exploitation
- **Education inequality**: The revelation that a university had compromised academic standards under presidential pressure
- **Abuse of presidential power**: The use of the intelligence and security apparatus to suppress information about the scandal and target whistleblowers

## The Candlelight Movement (November 2016 – March 2017)

### The Spontaneous Beginning

The first major public gathering took place on **October 29, 2016**, two weeks after the JTBC tablet revelations, drawing an estimated **20,000 people** to Gwanghwamun Square. Momentum built rapidly: **November 5** drew approximately **100,000**; **November 12** drew approximately **1 million** in Seoul alone, with additional hundreds of thousands in regional cities; **November 19** attracted another approximately **1 million**; and **November 26** reached an estimated **1.9 million nationwide**, as regional cities added their own simultaneous rallies.

The movement's largest single day was **December 3, 2016**, when an estimated **2.32 million people** gathered across South Korea — among the largest protests in the country's history and one of the largest proportional mobilizations (by percentage of national population) in democratic world history. Subsequent rallies on December 10, December 17, December 24, December 31, and into January and February 2017 maintained pressure as the Constitutional Court deliberated.

The first large-scale candlelit vigil in Gwanghwamun Square was organized through social media rather than established civil society organizations. Unlike prior Korean protest movements with coordinated leadership structures, the Candlelight Revolution was horizontally organized, with many participants independently arriving with candles, placards, and a simple demand: **President Park's resignation** (박근혜 하야).

The movement's scale, dignity, and restraint astonished observers. Participants included:
- **Elderly citizens** in their 60s, 70s, and 80s — a demographic rarely seen at protests in Korea — who spoke of their concern for their country's future
- **Families with young children**, with the vigil taking on an almost sacred, civic atmosphere
- **Students and workers**, echoing historic Korean social movements but also expanding beyond them
- **Professionals, artists, and public figures** who lent cultural and moral weight to the call for accountability

The tone was distinctly non-violent and civic: participants held candles (later supplemented with LED candles for safety), sang protest songs, chanted slogans of democratic accountability, and distributed information about the scandal and impeachment procedures. The movement became known for its orderliness — participants cleaned up after themselves, respected traffic laws and police barriers, and consciously distinguished themselves from earlier "radical" student and labor protests.

### Scale and Geography

By **December 2016**, the movement had swelled to what organizers and independent observers estimated at **1.5 to 2 million people** in a single day (December 3), making it one of the largest demonstrations in world history. Candlelit vigils spread to other South Korean cities: **Busan, Daegu, Daejeon, Incheon, Gwangju**, and smaller towns saw thousands gathering in solidarity. The movement's global reach extended to Korean diaspora communities in the United States, China, Japan, and Europe, where overseas Koreans held solidarity rallies.

The geographic reach underscored that the movement transcended Seoul's middle-class elite: it drew participants from conservative provinces like **Daegu** and **Busan** (the "TK" region that had traditionally supported the conservative party and Park's father, **Park Chung-hee**), indicating a shift in conservative voters' views of accountability and democratic governance.

### Civic Rituals and Cultural Impact

The Candlelight Revolution generated distinctive cultural symbols:
- **Candlelight as democratic ritual** — The candle, a centuries-old symbol of hope and prayer across cultures, was reappropriated as a symbol of peaceful democratic resistance in the face of corruption and abuse of power
- **Protest songs** — Musicians and singers composed and performed songs expressing demands for accountability, with "We Are Candlelight" (*우리는 촛불이다*) becoming an anthem
- **Handmade placards** — Citizens wrote personalized placards with messages ranging from humorous to devastating critiques of Park's administration
- **Intergenerational solidarity** — The presence of grandmothers, parents, and children side by side signaled a shared vision of democratic citizenship across generations

Intellectuals, artists, and commentators noted that the Candlelight Revolution represented a different kind of Korean protest politics than had dominated since the 1980s: rather than challenging capitalism or national security, it centered on **institutional integrity, presidential accountability, and the rule of law**. The movement's demands were fundamentally conservative in a procedural sense — not revolution in the radical sense, but a call to enforce constitutional and legal norms against a president who had, through Choi Soon-sil, violated those norms.

## Legal and Constitutional Processes

### Impeachment

Facing overwhelming public pressure, the **National Assembly** (국회) began impeachment proceedings in December 2016. On **December 9, 2016**, the Assembly **voted 234–56 to impeach** President Park Geun-hye, adopting an impeachment motion drawn up by opposition and some conservative legislators who had split with Park. The charges alleged:
- Abuse of presidential power in pressuring corporate donations for Choi Soon-sil's foundations
- Abuse of power in presidential personnel decisions made at Choi's direction
- Violation of the Constitution in allowing an unelected civilian to influence state affairs
- Conspiracy with Choi and others to defraud the state and corporations

Under the **1987 Constitution**, an impeachment by the National Assembly does not immediately remove a president; instead, it suspends the president's powers and refers the case to the **Constitutional Court** (헌법재판소), which has 180 days to decide whether to uphold or reject the impeachment.

### Constitutional Court Ruling

The Constitutional Court, composed of nine justices, heard oral arguments from Park's legal defense team and prosecutors representing the National Assembly throughout **January–March 2017**. The court deliberated amidst ongoing candlelit vigils outside its building and intense international scrutiny.

On **March 10, 2017**, the Constitutional Court **voted 8–0 to uphold the impeachment unanimously** and **remove Park Geun-hye from office** — the court was sitting with eight justices, one seat having fallen vacant when Chief Justice Park Han-chul's term ended in January 2017. **Acting Chief Justice Lee Jung-mi** (이정미) read the ruling, announcing that Park had "gravely violated" the Constitution and laws, abused her presidential powers, and allowed her administration to be captured by Choi Soon-sil's influence. The ruling noted that presidential power is not absolute and that the Constitution's integrity depends on the president's adherence to law and democratic norms. Park immediately lost all presidential immunities and authority.

## Consequences and Legacy

### The 2017 Presidential Election

Following Park's removal on March 10, 2017, Prime Minister **Hwang Kyo-ahn** (황교안) served as acting president. Under the Constitution, a snap presidential election was required within 60 days. The election was held on **May 9, 2017**, producing a decisive result: **Moon Jae-in** (문재인) of the liberal Democratic Party won with **41.1% of the vote** in a five-candidate field, defeating conservative candidates Ahn Cheol-soo (21.4%) and Hong Joon-pyo (24.0%). Moon, who had run against Park in 2012 and lost narrowly, was a former human rights lawyer and close ally of late President **Roh Moo-hyun**. Moon Jae-in's victory was widely interpreted as a direct mandate from the candlelight movement — an expression of voter desire for democratic reform and accountability following Park's removal.

Moon Jae-in's presidency (2017–2022) pursued progressive policies on labor, welfare, inter-Korean diplomacy, and healthcare. His administration oversaw the first **North Korea–United States summit** negotiations in 2018–2019, though the diplomatic effort ultimately stalled. He was also president during the COVID-19 pandemic and signed the special pardon of Park Geun-hye in December 2021. Moon was succeeded by conservative **Yoon Suk-yeol** (윤석열), who won the March 2022 presidential election by a margin of just 0.73 percentage points — the closest presidential contest in South Korean history.

### Park's Legal Aftermath

Following her removal, Park faced multiple criminal trials. In **April 2018**, she was convicted of **bribery, abuse of power, and coercion** in connection with forcing Samsung and other chaebol to donate to Choi's foundations, receiving a **24-year sentence** — later revised to **20 years** upon appeal. Her separate conviction for leaking classified information added further time. On **December 24, 2021**, President Moon Jae-in issued a **special pardon** of Park Geun-hye on grounds of national reconciliation and her deteriorating health; she was released from Seoul Detention Center, where she had been held, and most remaining sentences were commuted. The pardon was controversial: supporters cited humanitarian grounds; critics, including many candlelight movement participants, viewed it as a betrayal of the accountability principle the movement had stood for. Park, born in 1952, has largely withdrawn from public life and made no substantial political statements since her release.

**Choi Soon-sil** was separately tried and convicted of fraud, abuse of authority, and coercion, receiving an initial **20-year sentence** (later adjusted on appeal). **Lee Jae-yong**, Samsung's heir apparent, was convicted in 2021 in his retrial of bribery charges connected to the ~43 billion won Samsung channeled to Choi-linked foundations; he received a 2.5-year sentence but was released early in 2022 and was later pardoned by President Yoon Suk-yeol in 2022 on the grounds that Samsung's leadership was needed for national economic recovery — a decision that itself sparked controversy about chaebol-state relations and the limits of accountability.

### Democratic Resilience

The Candlelight Revolution's immediate political outcome — the removal of a president through legal and constitutional processes backed by civic mobilization — demonstrated that **South Korea's democratic institutions, despite decades of vulnerability to executive power, had sufficient resilience to enforce accountability**. The Constitutional Court, an institution created during the authoritarian era and long viewed with suspicion by progressives, vindicated its independence by ruling against a conservative president under pressure from both a progressive legislature and a massive movement.

The movement also revealed:
- **Broad civil-society capacity** for mobilization beyond traditional organized labor and student movements
- **Inter-generational solidarity** across age groups and, to some extent, across the regional divide (the traditional TK–Honam split)
- **The power of peaceful, non-violent protest** in a country with a history of violent and militarized protest suppression
- **Presidential vulnerability** to accountability when the courts, legislature, and civil society align

### Precedent for 2024

The Candlelight Revolution's model — mass civic mobilization combined with constitutional and legal procedure to remove a president — was recalled and partially replicated during the **December 2024 Martial Law Crisis** under President **Yoon Suk-yeol**. When Yoon declared martial law on December 3, 2024, citizens returned to Gwanghwamun and other squares with candles, invoking the Candlelight memory, and the National Assembly quickly voted to lift martial law, followed by impeachment and the Constitutional Court's removal of Yoon in April 2025. The institutional lessons of the Candlelight Revolution — that courts and legislatures can constrain presidential power, and that civic mobilization matters — proved durable.

### Feminist Resonances

The revelation of Choi Soon-sil's extraordinary influence without formal accountability also sparked **feminist reflection** on the gendered nature of power in Korea. Some feminists noted that Choi's abuse of her position was enabled by her proximity to a female president who was herself an exceptional figure; others argued that the Candlelight movement's prominence of women (both as participants and as victims of Choi-era university corruption) marked a shift in the composition and consciousness of Korean civil society. The movement's concern with institutional integrity and accountability later dovetailed with the late 2010s and 2020s feminist activism around #MeToo, molka (hidden camera) crimes, and the escape-the-corset movement.
