# Japanese Colonial Period (1910–1945)

The Japanese Colonial Period spans 1910 to 1945, when the Korean Peninsula was under Japanese imperial rule. This 35-year period transformed Korea's political, economic, and social structures, reshaped its cultural landscape, and left legacies that continue to shape Korean identity and East Asian relations in the 21st century.

## Prelude: The Road to Annexation (1876–1910)

Japan's colonization of Korea was preceded by decades of escalating intervention. The **Japan-Korea Treaty of 1876** (Ganghwa Treaty) forced Korea to open ports and grant Japan extraterritorial rights, ending Korea's exclusive tributary relationship with Qing China. Japan's decisive victory in the **First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)** removed Chinese influence over Korea, and the **Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905)** — ending with Japan's unexpected victory over Imperial Russia — left Japan the dominant power on the Korean Peninsula.

The **Eulsa Treaty of November 1905** (also called the Japan-Korea Protectorate Treaty), signed under military duress, stripped Korea of diplomatic independence and established a Japanese Residency-General in Seoul. Korean Emperor Gojong refused to sign and secretly appealed to foreign powers, including sending delegates to the 1907 Hague Peace Conference; Japan responded by forcing Gojong to abdicate in favor of his son Sunjong. By the **Japan-Korea Annexation Treaty of August 22, 1910**, Korea was formally incorporated into the Japanese Empire. The treaty's legality has been contested by Korean scholars and the South Korean government, arguing it was coerced and therefore void — a point Japan disputes.

## Military Rule and Initial Colonization (1910–1919)

The Japanese established the **Government-General of Korea** (조선총독부, Joseon Chongdokbu) as the supreme governing institution, headquartered initially in Seoul's Gyeongbokgung Palace grounds. The Governor-General, always a senior Japanese military officer, wielded combined executive, legislative, and judicial authority with no Korean participation.

**Governor-General Terauchi Masatake** (1910–1916) established the harshest phase of colonial rule, known as "Military Rule" (무단통치, mudan tongchi). Key measures included:
- Dissolution of the Korean military and confiscation of weapons
- Systematic land surveys (토지조사사업, toji josasaop, 1910–1918) that registered land under Japanese-administered rules; approximately 40% of Korea's cultivable land became state property or transferred to Japanese owners and companies through this process
- Abolition of Korean judicial institutions and replacement with Japanese courts
- Prohibition of Korean newspapers and political organizations
- Deployment of armed gendarmerie (헌병, heonbyeong) as a pervasive police force empowered to discipline Koreans for minor infractions

Japanese colonists received preferential treatment in commerce, government positions, and land acquisition. The **Oriental Development Company** (동양척식주식회사, founded 1908) became the primary vehicle for expropriating Korean land for Japanese settlers.

## The March 1st Independence Movement (1919)

Korean nationalism had not been extinguished by colonization. The death of former Emperor Gojong on January 21, 1919 — amid rumors (unproven) that he was poisoned by Japanese agents — ignited nationwide grief. Korean nationalist leaders, influenced by U.S. President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points and the principle of national self-determination articulated at the Paris Peace Conference, organized a mass non-violent protest.

On **March 1, 1919**, 33 Korean intellectuals and religious leaders (Christians, Buddhists, and Cheondoists) publicly read the **Declaration of Independence of Korea** (기미독립선언서) at Tapgol Park in Seoul. Demonstrations spread across the peninsula within days: an estimated **2 million Koreans** participated in protests in cities, towns, and villages over the following weeks.

Japanese authorities responded with police and military force. Official Japanese colonial records acknowledged 553 killed, approximately 12,000 arrested, and 1,400–1,500 wounded. Korean and modern historical estimates are substantially higher: the Korean Independence Movement historians' consensus estimates **7,509 killed, 15,961 wounded, and 46,303 arrested** across the March–May 1919 period. Mansei (만세, "Long Live Korea!") demonstrations occurred as far as Korean communities in Manchuria, Russia, and the United States.

The Korean Provisional Government was established in **Shanghai on April 11, 1919**, claiming sovereignty over the Korean nation. It operated in exile for 26 years, led by various presidents including Syngman Rhee (이승만) and Kim Gu (김구), maintaining diplomatic activities and coordinating some resistance efforts.

## Cultural Rule and Its Contradictions (1920–1930s)

The March 1 Movement shocked the Japanese colonial administration. Under new Governor-General Saito Makoto (1919–1927, 1929–1931), Japan shifted to a policy of "Cultural Rule" (문화통치, munhwa tongchi), presenting a more flexible approach while maintaining fundamental colonial control. Key changes included:
- Permission for Korean-language newspapers: **Dong-A Ilbo** (동아일보, 1920) and **Joseon Ilbo** (조선일보, 1920) began publication under censorship; both were suppressed for printing nationalist content on multiple occasions, and both were eventually ordered to cease publication in 1940
- Permission for some Korean cultural organizations (though political organizations remained banned)
- Shift from gendarmerie to civilian police, though the total number of police officers increased

The 1920s and 1930s were paradoxically a period of cultural flourishing within repression. Korean writers, poets, and intellectuals — including Yi Gwang-su (이광수), Kim Sowol (김소월), and Yun Dong-ju (윤동주) — produced major literary works. Modern Korean literature (신문학, sinmunhak) emerged, grappling with themes of colonial alienation and national identity. Korean intellectuals engaged with global modernist movements, Marxism, and nationalist philosophy.

**Economic integration deepened.** Japanese capital built railways: by 1945, Korea had approximately **6,500 km of railways**, connecting the peninsula's resources to Japanese ports. Korean rice production was directed to Japan: in 1930, approximately 45% of Korean rice output was exported to Japan, feeding Japan's industrial labor force while Korean consumption fell. Korean farmers increasingly became tenant farmers on land owned by Japanese companies or Korean landlords who collaborated with colonial administration.

## Wartime Assimilation (1937–1945)

As Japan launched full-scale war in China in **July 1937**, Korean policy hardened dramatically toward total assimilation. The ideology of naisen ittai (内鮮一體, "Japan and Korea as One Body") aimed to dissolve Korean identity into a Japanese imperial consciousness.

**Key assimilation measures:**

- **Language elimination**: From 1938, Korean-language instruction was eliminated from schools; Japanese became the sole medium of education. By 1942, the use of Korean language was banned in schools entirely. Korean-language newspapers were forced to cease publication in 1940.
- **Name change (創氏改名, Sōshi-kaimei)**: From **February 1940**, Koreans were pressured — under social, administrative, and employment coercion — to adopt Japanese-style family names and given names. By the August 1940 deadline, approximately 80% of Korean households had formally registered Japanese names, though the extent of genuine voluntary adoption versus coerced compliance remains debated.
- **Shinto shrine worship**: Koreans were compelled to participate in Shinto shrine ceremonies, a practice that created deep conflict with the Christian minority (approximately 2% of Korea's population) and Buddhist communities.
- **Labor mobilization**: From 1939, Koreans were mobilized for wartime labor under the National Mobilization Law. Hundreds of thousands were conscripted to work in Japanese mines, factories, and construction projects. An estimated **670,000 to 800,000** Koreans were brought to Japan as laborers during the war years, many under coercive conditions.
- **Military conscription**: Military service was extended to Koreans beginning in **1944** through a formal draft system. Approximately 240,000 Koreans served in the Japanese military by 1945; others were pressed into service as civilian military employees.

## "Comfort Women" (위안부, Wianbu)

Among the most painful legacies of colonial rule is the system of "comfort women" (위안부) — women recruited or coerced into sexual slavery to service Japanese military personnel across occupied territories. Korean women constituted a large proportion of comfort women. South Korean historians and the Korean Council for Justice and Remembrance estimate the total number at **50,000 to 200,000** women, with Korean women accounting for a substantial majority; the precise figures remain contested and subject to ongoing historical research.

Many women were deceived with promises of factory or nursing work; others were directly coerced or trafficked. Comfort stations (위안소, wianso) were operated across Japanese-occupied Asia from China to Southeast Asia. The conditions — sexual slavery under military supervision — constituted systematic violations of human rights.

The comfort women issue became a major diplomatic flashpoint in the post-war era. In **1991**, Kim Hak-sun (김학순) became the first Korean former comfort woman to testify publicly, breaking decades of silence. Her testimony triggered broader public acknowledgment and legal action. The **1993 Kono Statement** by Japanese Chief Cabinet Secretary Kono Yohei acknowledged Japanese military involvement and expressed apology, but subsequent Japanese government statements partially walked back its significance. A **2015 Japan-South Korea agreement** — concluding that the issue was "finally and irreversibly resolved" in exchange for a JPY 1 billion fund — was rejected by many survivors and the Moon Jae-in government, which dissolved the fund in 2019.

## Economic and Social Transformation

Colonial rule accelerated Korea's economic modernization under deeply exploitative conditions. Japanese colonial infrastructure investment was motivated by Japan's extraction requirements: railways transported minerals and agricultural goods to ports; ports were expanded to ship goods to Japan; factories processed Korean materials.

**Infrastructure built by 1945:**
- Approximately 6,500 km of railways
- Major hydroelectric dams in northern Korea (particularly the Supung Dam on the Yalu River, 1941, with 700 MW capacity — one of the largest in Asia at the time)
- Modern port facilities at Incheon, Busan, and Wonsan
- Telegraph and telephone networks
- Roads and urban infrastructure

Industrialization concentrated in northern Korea, where coal and hydroelectric resources supported steel, chemical, and arms industries. Companies like Nippon Chisso (now Chisso) built the Heungnam chemical complex in what is now North Korea. This northern industrial base partly explains the different economic trajectories of the two Koreas after 1945.

**Urbanization** increased under colonization: Seoul (then called Keijō/Gyeongseong) grew from approximately 200,000 in 1910 to 900,000 by 1940. Japanese settlers constituted approximately 13% of the city's population by the late colonial period.

## Korean Collaboration and Resistance

The colonial period created complex dynamics of collaboration (친일파, chinilpa, "pro-Japanese faction") and resistance. Korean elites and landlords who cooperated with Japanese rule often retained or expanded their wealth. A Korean middle class, educated in Japanese schools and fluent in Japanese, served in colonial administration and business. Some Koreans achieved prominence within the Japanese military.

This collaboration had lasting political consequences: after liberation, many chinilpa retained positions of influence in the First Republic under Syngman Rhee, largely evading accountability. A genuine reckoning with collaboration was constrained by Cold War politics, anti-communist priorities, and the political power of collaboration networks. The **Special Committee for Investigation of Anti-National Acts** (반민족행위특별조사위원회, Banmin Teukwi) established in 1948 to prosecute major collaborators was effectively shut down in 1949 under Rhee's pressure.

Resistance included:
- Armed guerrilla campaigns in Manchuria (the Korean Independence Army and Korean Volunteer Army)
- Communist partisan networks (including those associated with Kim Il-sung's activities in Manchuria from the late 1930s)
- Underground nationalist networks in the peninsula
- The Korean Provisional Government in Chongqing, China, which declared war on Japan in 1941 and maintained the Korean Liberation Army

## Liberation and Its Complications (1945)

Japan's August 1945 surrender to Allied forces ended colonial rule. Japanese military personnel and settlers — some 700,000 from the peninsula — were repatriated to Japan in 1945–1946. Koreans held in Japan faced complex choices about return or remaining.

Korea's liberation was not achieved through its own resistance alone but through Allied victory — a reality that shaped post-colonial political legitimacy. The peninsula was immediately divided at the **38th parallel** by the U.S. and Soviet Union, not as a permanent partition but as an administrative division for accepting Japanese surrender. This division solidified into the Cold War confrontation that produced the Korean War (1950–1953).

**Colonial legacies shaped post-liberation politics:**
- The split between nationalist, communist, and moderate political forces — incubated during the colonial period — immediately fractured Korean politics in 1945
- Land redistribution in the North (carried out by the Soviet-backed Kim Il-sung government in 1946) contrasted with the South's delayed, partial land reform (1950), both grappling with the colonial land-ownership distortions
- The developmental state model later pursued by [Park Chung-hee](../people/park-chung-hee.md) drew partly on colonial-era institutional frameworks and the industrial infrastructure the Japanese had built — a deeply controversial inheritance
- Disputes over colonial-era property, reparations, and historical accountability shaped Japan-Korea relations for decades

## Post-War Diplomacy and Unresolved Legacies

The **Treaty on Basic Relations between Japan and the Republic of Korea (1965)** normalized Japan-South Korea diplomatic relations. An accompanying Agreement on the Settlement of Problems Concerning Property and Claims provided USD 300 million in grants and USD 200 million in low-interest loans from Japan to South Korea in exchange for South Korea waiving further state claims against Japan. The treaty explicitly stated that "all problems" regarding property and claims from the colonial period were "settled completely and finally."

South Korea's government for decades interpreted this settlement as covering individual claims, restricting victims' ability to sue in Korean courts. However, in **2012** and **2018**, the South Korean Supreme Court ruled that the 1965 Agreement did not extinguish individual claims for unpaid wartime forced labor compensation, ordering Japanese companies (Nippon Steel, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries) to pay damages. Japan responded that the 2018 ruling violated the 1965 treaty and imposed trade restrictions on semiconductor-critical exports (fluorinated polyimides, resist materials, hydrogen fluoride) to South Korea in **July 2019** — an action that intersected with South Korea's semiconductor supply chain.

## Historical Memory and Contemporary Identity

The Japanese Colonial Period remains a defining reference point for Korean national identity. Key sites of memory include:
- **Seodaemun Prison Historical Hall** (서대문형무소역사관) in Seoul — the colonial-era prison where independence activists were held and executed, now a museum
- **March 1st Independence Movement Day** (삼일절, Samiljeol) — March 1, a national holiday in South Korea
- **Independence Hall of Korea** (독립기념관) in Cheonan, Chungcheong Province — a major museum complex dedicated to the independence movement

The "history wars" between Japan and Korea remain unresolved: disputes over Japanese textbook descriptions of colonialism, shrine visits by Japanese officials to Yasukuni (which memorializes war dead including convicted war criminals), and the comfort women issue periodically inflame bilateral relations. South Korean governments have varied in their emphasis on these issues relative to security (U.S.-Japan-Korea triangle) and economic pragmatism.

## Historical Significance

The Japanese Colonial Period fundamentally reshaped Korea. It accelerated modernization through infrastructure, urbanization, and industrial development — under exploitative, coercive conditions. It solidified Korean national consciousness through shared experience of resistance to cultural erasure. It created economic dependencies and land-ownership distortions that shaped post-liberation politics. Its traumatic legacy — forced labor, comfort women, cultural suppression — continues to inflect Korea's relationship with Japan and its own national identity.

[Park Chung-hee's](../people/park-chung-hee.md) developmental state model drew on colonial-era administrative and industrial frameworks; critics argued this inheritance entailed moral costs. South Korea's later emphasis on technological development and semiconductor leadership partly responded to the vulnerability of colonial-era technical dependency. The [democratization movement](democratization.md) of the 1980s also drew on the anti-colonial tradition of popular sovereignty against authoritarian rule.
