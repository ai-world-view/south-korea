# South Jeolla Province (Jeollanam)

**South Jeolla Province** (전라남도, *Jeolla Namdo* or *Jeollanam*) is the south-western
province of South Korea, bordered by North Jeolla Province to the north, Gwangju
Metropolitan City to the north-east, the Korea Strait to the south, and the Yellow Sea
(서해, *Seohae*, also called the West Sea) to the west. The province is the historical
heartland of the **Honam** (호남) region, characterised by agricultural abundance, a
deeply indented island-fringed coastline, and one of the most distinctive regional
identities in modern South Korea. The provincial government is headquartered in **Muan
(Namak Newtown)**, following the relocation of administrative offices from Gwangju
after that city's separation, with **Mokpo** remaining the largest conventional city
and the historical provincial capital.

## Geography and Climate

South Jeolla Province covers approximately **12,313 km²** and has a resident population
of roughly **1.78 million** (2024 estimate), making it one of the least densely populated
mainland provinces—a product of sustained out-migration toward Seoul and Gwangju.

The terrain is predominantly low-lying, rising gently toward the east where the **Sobaek
Range** and **Noryeong Range** mark the provincial boundary with North Jeolla. The
dominant hydrological features are the **Yeongsan River** (영산강, 136 km), which drains
the central plateau and empties into the Yellow Sea through the Yeongsan Estuary near
Mokpo, and the **Seomjin River** (섬진강, 212 km), shared with North Jeolla and South
Gyeongsang, which flows into the Korea Strait at Gwangyang. Both river systems support
extensive paddy agriculture and have been managed by major irrigation infrastructure
since the Joseon period.

### Coastline and Islands

South Jeolla possesses one of the most complex coastlines in the world. The **Dadohae**
(다도해, literally "many-island sea") encompasses an estimated **1,696 named islands**, of
which roughly 275 are inhabited, stretching from the western Sinan archipelago to the
eastern Yeosu peninsula. This total represents the majority of South Korea's island count.
The **Dadohae Maritime National Park** (다도해해상국립공원), established 1981, at 2,344 km²
is the largest of South Korea's national parks.

Key islands include:

- **Jindo** (진도, 363 km²): South Korea's third-largest island, connected to the
  mainland by the Jindo Bridge (1984). Jindo is home to the **Jindo dog** (진돗개), a
  natural monument and one of Korea's best-known native dog breeds, internationally
  registered by major kennel organisations. Each spring (usually late April to early May, timed to
  the lowest tides), the **"Jindo Miracle Sea Road"** (신비의 바닷길) phenomenon exposes
  a 2.8-km tidal causeway between Jindo and the islet of Modo, drawing hundreds of
  thousands of visitors to the annual Yeongdeung Festival (*Yeongdeungjewi*).
- **Wando** (완도): A major centre of seaweed aquaculture, connected by bridge to
  the mainland (1969). Wando County supplies approximately 60% of South Korea's
  farmed seaweed (*gim* and *miyeok*) output.
- **Sinan Archipelago** (신안군): A county of 1,025 islands and islets—the world's
  largest island-count municipality—known for salt production, migratory-bird
  stopover habitat, and the internationally celebrated **Sinan Purple Islands** (*Banwol*,
  반월도, and *Bakji*, 박지도, popularised from 2021), where buildings, roads, and bridges were
  painted purple to attract tourism.
- **Goheung Peninsula**: The site of the **Naro Space Center** (나로우주센터),
  South Korea's first domestic launch facility, opened 2009 and used for the
  NARO-1 (KSLV-1) rocket launches and the successful NURI (KSLV-2) launch in 2022.

The climate is **warm-temperate maritime**: mean annual temperature in Mokpo ~14°C,
annual precipitation ~1,200 mm on the mainland coast, rising to over 1,500 mm on
southern islands. The monsoon season (June–August) delivers the bulk of rainfall.
The province is in the primary typhoon track for storms traversing the Korea Strait.

## Major Cities and Urban Centers

**Mokpo** (목포시, population ~215,000): Once the province's leading port and
administrative hub, Mokpo developed as a Japanese treaty port after 1897 and served
as a significant colonial naval base. Today it is a ferry hub connecting the mainland
to dozens of Dadohae islands, and a growing tourism destination emphasising its
colonial-era architecture (*Mokpo Near-Modern Cultural Space*) and seafood markets.
Plans for a high-speed rail link (KTX extension from Gwangju) are in various stages
of development, intended to reduce Mokpo's geographic peripherality.

**Yeosu** (여수시, population ~270,000): An industrial port and tourism city on a
peninsula commanding the western Korea Strait. Yeosu hosted the **2012 World Expo**
(May–August 2012) on the theme "The Living Ocean and Coast" (*Living Ocean and Coast:
Diversity of Resources and Sustainable Activities*), attracting 8.2 million visitors
from 104 nations. The Expo legacy infrastructure—the Expo Ocean Park, GS Caltex Art
Hall, and Yeosu Cable Car—has supported sustained tourism growth. The **Yeosu
National Industrial Complex** (여수국가산업단지), one of Korea's largest petrochemical
clusters, hosts GS Caltex (refining) and LG Chem, Hanwha Solutions, and Lotte Chemical
(petrochemicals), producing fuels, polymers, and specialty chemicals.

**Suncheon** (순천시, population ~285,000): An inland and coastal city that has
distinguished itself through ecological leadership. **Suncheon Bay** (순천만), a vast
estuary and reedbed system designated a **Ramsar Wetland** in 2006, protects nesting
habitat for rare migratory birds including hooded cranes, black-faced spoonbills, and
red-crowned cranes. The **Suncheon Bay National Garden** (*Suncheon Gukga Jeongwon*,
opened 2013) is South Korea's first national garden, covering 112 hectares around the
bay. Suncheon's ecological park model has been cited internationally as a successful
case of conservation-led tourism development.

**Naju** (나주시, population ~120,000): Historically one of the most important cities
in the Honam region—a major centre of the Joseon provincial administration and the
origin of Korea's most celebrated pear variety (*Naju Pear*, 나주배). The **Bitgaram
Innovation City** (*Bitgaram Hyeoksin Dosi*), developed from 2012, relocated a cluster of
public institutions to Naju — anchored by the **Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO)**
and including the Korea Rural Community Corporation, the Korea Energy Agency, and other
energy and agriculture bodies — as part of the government's balanced-development policy of
dispersing public agencies from the Seoul capital region.

**Gwangju Metropolitan City** (광주광역시), historically South Jeolla's largest city
and economic centre, was administratively separated from the province in 1986. While
now a separate metropolitan unit, Gwangju remains culturally and economically
inseparable from the Honam regional identity and interacts intensely with Jeollanam's
economy and culture. (See the [Gwangju](gwangju.md) article.)

## Economy

### Agriculture and Food Production

South Jeolla is South Korea's premier agricultural province. The province accounts for:
- Approximately **20% of national rice output** from the Yeongsan and Seomjin river
  deltas, among the nation's most fertile paddy regions.
- Leading national shares in barley, garlic, ginger, sweet potato, and citrus
  (notably the Goheung *yuzu* and the Wando tangerine).
- The world-leading **seaweed aquaculture** sector: Wando and Jindo counties dominate
  national production of *gim* (roasted laver), *miyeok* (sea mustard), and *dasima*
  (kelp). Korean dried *gim* became a major export product in the 2010s–2020s, driven
  by the Hallyu-linked global appetite for Korean foods; by 2023, South Korea exported
  more than US$700 million of *gim* annually, with Jeollanam producers at the core.
- Salt (*cheonillyeom*, sun-evaporated sea salt) from the Sinan and Muan tidal flats
  is considered a premium product and is used in high-end kimchi and food production.

### Fisheries and Aquaculture

The Dadohae's exceptionally rich marine ecosystem, at the convergence of Yellow Sea
and Korea Strait currents, supports large-scale fishing for anchovy (*myeolchi*),
hairtail (*galchi*), mackerel, squid, abalone, and sea cucumber. **Mokpo** is the
largest fisheries port in the region. Jindo's strong tidal currents produce prized
*jindo sea cucumber* and abalone, commanding premium market prices.

### Petrochemicals and Industry

The **Yeosu National Industrial Complex**, covering approximately 45 km² and employing
more than 35,000 workers directly, is one of South Korea's three major petrochemical
clusters (alongside Ulsan and Daesan). GS Caltex's Yeosu refinery processes around
800,000 barrels per day of crude oil; LG Chem and Hanwha Solutions produce NCC
(naphtha cracking centre) products including ethylene, propylene, and downstream polymers.
The complex contributes approximately 30% of South Jeolla's regional GDP.

### Space and High Technology

The **Naro Space Center** in Goheung, operated by the Korea Aerospace Research Institute
(KARI), is the launching point for South Korea's domestic space programme. After two
failed KSLV-1 launches (2009, 2010) and one success (2013), the KSLV-2 (*Nuri*) rocket
achieved orbital success in June 2022, placing a 1.3-tonne satellite into orbit and
making South Korea the seventh nation to independently launch a payload above 1 tonne
into orbit. The Nuri-3 (with the civilian payload *KAIST Sat*) launched in 2023.

### Tourism

The 2012 Yeosu World Expo provided a significant infrastructure platform. Since then,
tourism has grown steadily: the Suncheon Bay Ecological Park, Dadohae island tours,
culinary tourism centred on Jeolla's renowned food culture, and the growing MICE
(meetings, incentives, conferences, exhibitions) sector at Yeosu have diversified the
regional economy. In 2023, Jeollanam hosted approximately 65 million domestic visitor
nights, ranking it among the top-five Korean provinces for tourism volume.

## History

### Three Kingdoms Period: Baekje

During the [Three Kingdoms Period](../history/three-kingdoms-period.md), South Jeolla
was the southern heartland of the **Baekje kingdom** (백제, 18 BCE–660 CE). Baekje's
southern territories, known as *Mahan* during the earlier confederacy period, were
characterised by advanced bronze and iron working, Buddhist art (Baekje transmitted
Buddhism and Chinese writing to Japan via the Baekje Buddhist missions of 538–552 CE),
and maritime commerce with the southern Chinese dynasties and Japan. Baekje's
cultural legacy persists in the Buddhist temples of Jeolla—particularly **Seonamsa**
(선암사) and **Songgwangsa** (송광사) in Suncheon, both founded during the Unified
Silla period but incorporating Baekje-era religious traditions.

### Goryeo and Joseon

During the [Goryeo Dynasty](../history/goryeo-period.md) (918–1392), Jeolla was an
important administrative and commercial region. **Gangjin** (강진), on the south
coast, was the leading centre of **Goryeo celadon** (*cheongja*) production in the
12th–14th centuries; the coastal kilns there produced the distinctive blue-green
celadon ceramics regarded as Goryeo's highest artistic achievement. The Gangjin
kiln complex is a UNESCO World Heritage candidate and a significant archaeological site.

During the [Joseon Dynasty](../history/joseon-dynasty.md) (1392–1910), the province
developed into the most agriculturally productive region of the kingdom. The Joseon
administration relied heavily on Jeolla's rice surplus and maritime trade to sustain
the royal household and the capital. The 1592–1598 Japanese invasions devastated the
province: Admiral Yi Sun-sin's decisive naval victory at the **Battle of Myeongnyang**
(명량대첩, October 26, 1597) was fought in the Myeongnyang Strait (울돌목) between
Jindo and the Haenam mainland, where Yi—with only 13 warships against a Japanese
fleet of approximately 333—exploited the strait's extreme tidal currents to sink 31
enemy vessels and rout the invasion fleet. The Battle of Myeongnyang is commemorated
as the turning point of the naval campaign and one of the most celebrated victories
in Korean history.

### Japanese Colonial Period

Under [Japanese colonial rule](../history/japanese-colonial-period.md) (1910–1945),
South Jeolla's agricultural surplus was systematically extracted for export to Japan.
The colonial land survey (1910–1918) transferred ownership of large portions of Jeolla's
farmland from Korean small-holders to Japanese landlords and the Oriental Development
Company (*Tōyō Takushoku Kabushiki Kaisha*). Rural tenant farmers, reduced to
sharecroppers, were among the most exploited in the colony. This exploitation fuelled
significant participation in the [Korean Independence Movement](../history/korean-independence-movement.md):
the March 1st Movement (1919) saw mass demonstrations in Gwangju and provincial towns,
and labour and tenant uprisings in the 1920s–1930s were centred in Jeolla.

### Modern Democratic Identity

Post-liberation, South Jeolla developed its distinctive **progressive political identity**
rooted in three interconnected historical experiences:

1. **Exploitation and relative underdevelopment**: The Honam region received
   proportionally less investment in state-directed industrialisation under Park Chung-hee's
   1960s–70s development strategy, which heavily favoured Gyeongnam and the Busan–Ulsan
   corridor. This structural disparity fuelled regional grievance and opposition politics.

2. **The 1980 Gwangju Uprising**: The [Gwangju Uprising](../history/gwangju-uprising.md)
   (May 18–27, 1980), though centred in Gwangju, sent shockwaves through the entire
   Honam region. The suppression of the pro-democracy uprising by paratroopers under
   General Chun Doo-hwan's command, in which at least 165–200 people were confirmed
   killed (with actual totals likely higher), created a deep regional wound and
   permanently identified the Honam region with democratic resistance.

3. **Kim Dae-jung's Political Base**: [Kim Dae-jung](../people/kim-dae-jung.md)
   (김대중, 1924–2009), Nobel Peace Prize laureate (2000) and South Korea's fifteenth
   president (1998–2003), was born on Hauido, an island in what is now Sinan County, South
   Jeolla. His repeated persecution by authoritarian governments — including his 1973
   abduction by the Korean Central Intelligence Agency in Tokyo (halted by international
   intervention) and a 1980 military death sentence — and his eventual presidency became a
   narrative of Honam-as-democracy-vanguard that remains potent in contemporary Korean politics.

## Culture and Identity

### Regional Identity

The **Honam–Yeongnam** (호남-영남) regional divide is the deepest cleavage in modern
South Korean electoral politics. Since the 1971 presidential election (in which Kim
Dae-jung challenged Park Chung-hee), South Jeolla has voted overwhelmingly for
progressive and opposition candidates in every national election. In the 2022 presidential
election, the Democratic Party candidate received more than 85% of the vote in Jeollanam.
In the 2024 National Assembly election, the DP and allied parties swept virtually every
seat in the province.

This alignment reflects genuine ideological differences (Honam has historically had
stronger labour-union traditions, more support for welfare expansion, and firmer opposition
to authoritarian legacies) alongside the regional-loyalty dimension.

### Cuisine

South Jeolla's food culture is widely regarded as the richest in South Korea—a
distinction rooted in the region's agricultural abundance, its coastal resources, and
centuries of refined court-inspired cooking traditions:

- **Jeonju Bibimbap** (전주비빔밥): Though Jeonju is in North Jeolla, the bibimbap
  tradition extends throughout Honam and is its most internationally recognised dish.
- **Namul and Banchan abundance**: Jeolla's farm and sea bounty supports an exceptional
  variety of seasoned vegetable side dishes; a traditional Jeolla meal can include 20–30
  *banchan*.
- **Kimchi excellence**: The region's role in preserving and innovating *kimjang*
  (kimchi-making) traditions contributed to UNESCO's 2013 inscription of Kimjang on its
  Intangible Cultural Heritage list.
- **Seafood**: Mokpo's **hongeo** (홍어, fermented skate ray, known for its intense
  ammonia aroma) is a Jeolla delicacy consumed at celebratory occasions; Yeosu's
  **dolsan gat-kimchi** (돌산갓김치, mustard-leaf kimchi) and grilled *galchi* (hairtail)
  are regional specialities.
- **Traditional grain spirits**: The *goryangju* and *makgeolli* traditions of the
  region are being revived as heritage beverages alongside the Korean craft spirits movement.

### Pansori

**Pansori** (판소리), the narrative singing tradition inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible
Cultural Heritage list in 2003, has deep Jeolla roots. The canonical five *pansori*
stories—*Chunhyangga*, *Simcheongga*, *Heungbuga*, *Jeokbyeokga*, and *Sugungga*—were
largely transmitted through the **Seopyeonje** (서편제) and **Dongpyeonje** (동편제) stylistic
schools, which originated and were preserved in South Jeolla villages. Director Im
Kwon-taek's 1993 film *Seopyeonje* (서편제), one of the first Korean films to exceed
one million admissions in Seoul, introduced pansori to a broader national and
international audience and sparked renewed interest in Jeolla musical traditions.

### Traditional Crafts

- **Najijang** (나전장, mother-of-pearl lacquerware): The Tongyeong area of South
  Gyeongsang and the Wando area of Jeollanam have parallel traditions of applying
  cut mother-of-pearl to lacquered furniture, a craft designated as National Intangible
  Cultural Heritage.
- **Goryeo Celadon revival**: Contemporary potters in Gangjin maintain and study
  the Goryeo celadon techniques, producing scholarly and artisan reproductions.
- **Hanji (traditional paper)**: The Jangseong and Gokseong areas maintain traditional
  Korean handmade paper (*hanji*) production, supporting calligraphy and conservation arts.

### Jindo Sea Road Festival

The annual **Yeongdeung Festival** (*Yeongdeungjewi*) at Hoedong village on Jindo,
held when lunar-calendar conditions align the spring king tides, draws 500,000–1,000,000
visitors to witness the 40-minute–1-hour tidal window when the 2.8-km causeway to Modo
Island is exposed. The Korean folk legend attributes the path's opening to the prayers
of an old woman (Grandma Bbyong, 뽕할머니) left on the island when her family fled from
tigers; the French ambassador Pierre Landy reportedly dubbed it the "Korean Moses Miracle"
(*miracle de Moïse*) in a 1975 article, giving the phenomenon its international name.

## Administrative Division

South Jeolla Province is subdivided into **5 cities** (*si*) and **17 counties** (*gun*):

**Cities (Si)**:
- Mokpo, Yeosu, Suncheon, Naju, Gwangyang

**Counties (Gun)**:
- Damyang, Gokseong, Gurye, Goheung, Boseong, Hwasun, Jangheung, Gangjin, Haenam,
  Yeongam, Muan, Hampyeong, Yeonggwang, Jangseong, Wando, Jindo, Sinan

(Note: Gwangju Metropolitan City was separated administratively from the province in
1986 and is an independent metropolitan unit. The province's government relocated
its administrative offices from Gwangju to the Muan-Namak area in 2005.)

## Challenges and Future Directions

**Demographic Decline**: South Jeolla is among the provinces experiencing the steepest
absolute population decline in South Korea. From a peak population of approximately
2.5 million in 1985, the resident population fell to under 1.8 million by 2024. Rural
counties such as Gurye, Gokseong, and Boseong have populations that are majority over
65 years old, threatening the viability of farming, local services, and municipal finance.

**Agricultural Restructuring**: South Korea's trade liberalisation (WTO obligations since
1995, US-Korea FTA since 2012, and similar agreements) has depressed prices for rice,
garlic, and other Jeolla staples. The government's *Hoam* (*Rural Community Development*)
programme promotes high-value organic farming, direct-to-consumer sales, and agritourism
to supplement farm income, but competitiveness challenges persist.

**Regional Inequality**: The Honam region's per-capita GDP consistently trails Gyeonggi,
Gyeongnam, and Seoul. The ongoing political salience of regional economic grievances is
reflected in the "balanced national development" policies of successive progressive
governments (Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, Moon Jae-in), which directed public-sector
relocation and infrastructure investment to Jeolla, but structural gaps remain wide.

**Offshore Wind and Renewable Energy**: The Yellow Sea's shallow continental shelf and
strong wind resource are making South Jeolla a target for offshore wind development.
The **Sinan Offshore Wind Complex**, planned at 8.2 GW (the world's largest single
offshore wind project if fully developed), has received government approval and is in
the permitting and contracting stages, potentially representing a transformative
industrial anchor for the province.

**Ecological Threats**: The Suncheon Bay reedbed is threatened by upstream agricultural
drainage, plastic pollution, and changing migratory patterns driven by East Asian
climate change. The Dadohae's marine ecosystem faces pressure from over-fishing, invasive
species (particularly the crown-of-thorns sea star and Japanese kelp bladder-wrack),
and rising sea temperatures. Government marine protected area (MPA) designations and
fishing moratoria are partially mitigating these pressures.
