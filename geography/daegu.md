# Daegu — Metropolitan City and Industrial Hub

**Category**: Geography
**Key figures**: Hong Joon-pyo (mayor 2022–present, former South Gyeongsang governor), Kwon Young-jin (mayor 2014–2022)

## Summary

Daegu (대구, Hangul; also romanized Taegu in older transliterations) is South Korea's fourth-largest city by population, having been the country's third-largest for much of the twentieth century before Incheon overtook it. The city proper held approximately 2.38 million residents as of the 2020 census, while the broader Daegu–Gyeongbuk region encompasses closer to 5 million. Administratively separated from North Gyeongsang Province (*Gyeongsangbuk-do*) in July 1981 as a **Directly Governed City** (직할시) and redesignated a **Metropolitan City** (광역시) in 1995, Daegu is self-governing at the provincial level. The city serves as the economic, cultural, and administrative center of the southeastern interior of the Korean peninsula, historically known as the Yeongnam region.

## Geography and Climate

Daegu sits in a broad river basin formed by the Geumho River (금호강), a tributary of the Nakdong River (낙동강), which it meets southwest of the city. Mountains ring the city on multiple sides: **Palgongsan** (팔공산, 1,192 m) rises to the north and is designated a provincial park encompassing Buddhist temples including Donghwasa (동화사), founded in 493 CE. **Biseulsan** (비슬산, 1,084 m) anchors the southern perimeter. This topographic enclosure creates a distinctive microclimate: Daegu regularly records South Korea's highest summer temperatures, with August averages around 26–28°C and frequent days exceeding 35°C. The city's extreme summer heat — earning nicknames like "Daegu Oven" (*대구 화로*) — has been exacerbated by urban heat island effects as the city's green space per capita remains low relative to Korean metropolitan norms.

Daegu is situated approximately 80 km north of Busan and 240 km southeast of Seoul, positioning it at the intersection of major transportation corridors. The **Gyeongbu Line** — Korea's central railway linking Seoul and Busan — passes through Daegu's **Dongdaegu Station**, which became a major **KTX high-speed rail** hub when high-speed service launched in April 2004.

## Historical Development

Human settlement in the Daegu basin dates to the Bronze Age, and the area was part of the Silla kingdom (57 BCE–935 CE). During the Goryeo (918–1392) and Joseon (1392–1897) periods, Daegu developed as a regional market town and administrative seat of Gyeongsang Province. The **Joseon government established Daegu as the provincial capital** of Gyeongsang Province in 1601, a status that gave the city administrative importance sustaining its commercial development over subsequent centuries.

During the late Joseon and early colonial periods, Daegu became one of Korea's most important trading and market centers. The **October 1, 1946 Daegu Uprising** (대구 10.1사건) — a major popular rebellion against food shortages and US Military Government policy — began in Daegu and spread across southeastern Korea before being violently suppressed. Hundreds were killed and thousands arrested in the aftermath, marking a defining early confrontation of the liberation period.

During the **Korean War** (1950–1953), Daegu served as a provisional capital and rear-area supply base during the desperate summer of 1950 when North Korean forces pushed South Korean and United Nations troops to the **Pusan Perimeter** — the defensive line holding the southeastern corner of the peninsula. Daegu, positioned inside the perimeter, functioned as a staging area for military operations while the front stabilized approximately 30–50 km to the north and west. The city's wartime role reinforced its strategic importance in southeastern Korea.

## Economy: Textile Heritage and Diversification

Daegu's economic identity through much of the 20th century was defined by **textiles**, particularly cotton fabric weaving and dyeing. The city earned the informal designation "Manchester of Korea" (*한국의 맨체스터*) for its dominance in Korea's textile manufacturing. At its peak in the 1970s–1980s, the textile and clothing sector accounted for a large majority of Daegu's industrial employment. The **Seobu Market** (서부시장) and **Chilseong Market** (칠성시장) formed cores of textile wholesale trade; the Chilseong market remains one of Korea's largest wholesale centers.

The textile sector's decline from the 1990s onward — driven by competition from lower-cost manufacturers in China and Southeast Asia — forced significant economic restructuring. Daegu has pursued diversification into:
- **Automotive components**: supply-chain manufacturers for the broader southeastern automotive industry centered on Ulsan (Hyundai Motor) and nearby plants
- **Machinery and metalworking**: through the **Seongso Industrial Complex** (성서공단) established in 1980, one of Daegu's largest industrial zones
- **Medical and bio-industry**: Daegu has positioned itself as a medical technology and bio-industry hub, with the **Daegu Technopolis** cluster including research institutes and medical device manufacturers
- **Fashion and design**: leveraging textile heritage into higher-value fashion design, with Daegu hosting the **Daegu Textile Fashion Cluster**

The **Gumi Industrial Complex** (구미국가산업단지), located 30 km north of Daegu in the city of Gumi, is one of South Korea's most important electronics manufacturing zones — home to Samsung SDI, LG Electronics manufacturing, and scores of component suppliers — and the broader Daegu region functions as a logistics and service center for Gumi's industrial workforce.

## The 2003 Daegu Subway Fire

On **February 18, 2003**, a catastrophic fire at **Jungang-ro Station** on Daegu Metro Line 1 killed **192 people** and injured 151 others. The fire was set by arsonist Kim Daihan, a 56-year-old stroke survivor who carried flammable liquid onto a train and ignited it. The fire spread rapidly through the station's poorly fire-proofed interiors, and delayed evacuation announcements and sealed station exits trapped passengers. The disaster became one of South Korea's worst peacetime mass-casualty events.

The Daegu subway fire triggered sweeping reforms to public safety standards across South Korea: mandatory non-flammable materials in subway cars, comprehensive evacuation drills, improved emergency communication systems, and reinforced station safety protocols. Subway operators nationwide underwent equipment replacement programs in the years following. The disaster also prompted broader reflection on emergency management capacity and the prioritization of construction cost over safety standards in public infrastructure.

## COVID-19 Outbreak (2020)

In February 2020, Daegu became the center of South Korea's first major domestic **COVID-19** outbreak, traced to a cluster among members of the **Shincheonji Church of Jesus** (신천지 예수교 증거장막성전), a religious organization. Within weeks, the Daegu-centered cluster had generated over 4,000 confirmed cases — the largest single outbreak outside China at that time. South Korea's rapid and transparent public health response (mass testing, contact tracing, quarantine) drew international attention as a model. Daegu's healthcare infrastructure was severely strained, and the city became the focus of an unprecedented national mobilization of medical resources, personnel, and public health coordination.

## Education

Daegu hosts several major universities:
- **Kyungpook National University** (경북대학교, KNU): founded 1946, one of South Korea's 10 national universities (*국립대학교*); the flagship research university of the southeastern region with approximately 30,000 students across campuses in Daegu and Sangju
- **Yeungnam University** (영남대학교): founded 1947, a major private comprehensive university in adjacent Gyeongsan city, with approximately 24,000 students
- **Keimyung University** (계명대학교): founded 1954 with Presbyterian missionary roots, approximately 18,000 students
- **Daegu University** (대구대학교): founded 1956, known for programs serving students with disabilities
- **Daegu Gyeongbuk Institute of Science & Technology** (DGIST): established 2004 as a national science and technology institute, focused on advanced research

The concentration of universities and their approximately 200,000 combined students constitutes a significant driver of Daegu's service economy.

## Culture and Traditional Medicine

**Yangnyeong Market** (약령시, Yangnyeong-si) is one of Korea's oldest and largest traditional herbal medicine markets, with origins tracing to a 1658 Joseon-era market established by royal decree. The market — concentrated along Gyeongchung-daero in Jung District — sells hundreds of dried herbs, medicinal roots, and traditional medicine preparations. The adjacent **Daegu Yangnyeong Oriental Medical Culture Center** offers museum exhibitions and educational programs. Traditional medicine remains a significant part of Daegu's cultural identity and tourism economy.

Major cultural institutions include the **Daegu Arts Center** (대구문화예술회관), the **Daegu Opera House** (대구오페라하우스, opened 2003, one of Korea's premier operatic venues), and the **Daegu National Museum** (국립대구박물관), which holds collections of artifacts from the Yeongnam region. The **Daegu Colorful Festival** (대구컬러풀페스티벌) is an annual summer street parade and cultural festival.

In sports, the **Samsung Lions** (삼성 라이온즈) baseball team, competing in Korea's KBO League, is based at **Daegu Samsung Lions Park** (rebuilt and reopened in 2016). The Lions are among Korea's most successful baseball franchises, winning multiple Korean Series championships. **Daegu FC** (*대구FC*), the city's professional football club founded in 2002, competes in the K League 1 top division.

Daegu hosted the **2011 IAAF World Championships in Athletics** (August 27 – September 4, 2011), the first World Athletics Championships held in Korea, at the **Daegu Stadium**. The event brought an estimated 40,000 international visitors and elevated the city's profile as an international sports venue.

## Political Identity

Daegu and the surrounding North Gyeongsang Province form the core of the **TK block** (*대경권*, Daegu-Gyeongbuk), historically one of South Korea's most reliably conservative political regions. The region's political identity has been shaped by its association with major conservative figures: Park Chung-hee (born in Gumi, North Gyeongsang), Roh Tae-woo (from the Daegu area), and Park Geun-hye (who represented a Daegu constituency). Conservative political parties — from the Democratic Republican Party through the Liberty Korea Party to its successors — have dominated Daegu and North Gyeongsang electoral politics for decades. The region's conservative loyalty has been so consistent that Korean political commentators describe Daegu and North Gyeongsang as "the conservative heartland."

This political identity stands in contrast to the southwestern Honam region (Gwangju, South and North Jeolla), which is the heartland of progressive politics in Korea — a regional polarization that has shaped Korean democratic politics since the 1971 presidential election.

## Significance

Daegu's regional importance lies in its role as economic and cultural center of the Yeongnam interior, one of South Korea's most distinct regional identities. The city's development arc — from Joseon-era trading center to colonial-period market town, wartime staging base, textile manufacturing capital, and post-industrial diversifying metropolitan — mirrors the broader trajectory of Korean modernization. The 2003 subway fire became a watershed for national public safety standards. Daegu's COVID-19 experience in 2020 demonstrated both the vulnerability of dense urban centers to infectious disease and the capacity of coordinated Korean public health response. The city's textile heritage represents an important phase of Korean economic development whose decline continues to shape local economic policy. Its political conservatism offers a lens into the regional cleavages that have defined Korean democratic competition.
