# Ulsan

**Ulsan** (울산), officially the **Metropolitan City of Ulsan** (울산광역시), is the smallest by population of South Korea's six metropolitan cities but its preeminent heavy-industrial center. Positioned on the southeastern coast with deep-water harbor access, Ulsan houses Korea's largest petrochemical complex, the world's largest automobile factory by floor area (Hyundai Motor Company's Ulsan plant), and the headquarters of Hyundai Heavy Industries—the world's leading shipbuilder by tonnage. Its industrial concentration made it the richest city per capita in Korea for much of the late twentieth century, and it remains the country's dominant manufacturing metropolis.

## Geography and Climate

Ulsan lies in the southeastern tip of the Korean Peninsula, approximately 70 kilometers northeast of Busan along the coast of the Korea Strait. The metropolitan area covers approximately 1,062 square kilometers—the largest land area of any Korean metropolitan city—encompassing coastal plains, river valleys, and inland forested mountains.

The **Taehwa River** (태화강) flows west to east through the city before entering Ulsan Bay, dividing the urban core between Dong-gu and Nam-gu districts. The river has been central to Ulsan's recent environmental transformation (see below). **Ulsan Bay** provides sheltered deep-water anchorage for industrial shipping, while nearby **Onsan Bay** to the south hosts petrochemical tanker terminals. Inland, the Yeongnam Alps rise to elevations exceeding 1,000 meters, providing watershed protection and recreational area.

Ulsan's climate is temperate maritime: winters are mild (January average ~2°C, rarely below −5°C) owing to the moderating influence of the Korea Strait; summers are warm and humid (August average ~26°C). Annual precipitation is approximately 1,200–1,500 millimeters, concentrated in the June–September monsoon season. Typhoons occasionally affect the region in late summer.

## Administrative Structure

Ulsan was designated a **Metropolitan City** (광역시) on July 15, 1997, separating from South Gyeongsang Province and gaining direct central-government administration—recognition of its industrial and economic significance. The city is divided into five administrative units:

- **Dong-gu** (동구, East District) — core industrial zone; Hyundai Heavy Industries shipyard
- **Nam-gu** (남구, South District) — commercial center; SK Energy refinery complex
- **Buk-gu** (북구, North District) — automotive manufacturing; Hyundai Motor Plant 2 and 3
- **Jung-gu** (중구, Central District) — historical core; civic administration
- **Ulju-gun** (울주군) — rural county encompassing mountains and agricultural land

## Early History

The area that is now Ulsan has been inhabited since the Bronze Age; petroglyphs at Bangudae Petroglyphs (반구대 암각화) in Ulju-gun, discovered in 1971 and dating to approximately 5,000–3,000 BCE, depict whales, deer, tigers, and boats—among the oldest whale-hunting records in the world. These petroglyphs are a UNESCO World Heritage nomination candidate.

During the Three Kingdoms period, the Ulsan region formed part of the Silla kingdom's southeastern core. In the Joseon dynasty it was a coastal administrative district (*dohobu*) known for fishing, salt production, and limited maritime commerce. It served as a minor naval base without significant industrial development.

The Japanese colonial period (1910–1945) brought limited industrial installations, primarily a small chemical plant and fishing-processing facilities. The decisive transformation came after the Korean War.

## Industrialization: The Heavy and Chemical Industry Drive

The modern industrial city was created in a single decade. Following military general Park Chung-hee's seizure of power in 1961, his developmental state identified Ulsan's deep harbor and coastal location as ideal for heavy industrialization. On February 3, 1962, the government declared Ulsan a **Special Industrial District** (*teugbyeol gongeopji*)—the first such designation in Korean history—and began channeling state investment, preferential financing, and directed foreign loans into the site.

The 1970s **Heavy and Chemical Industry (HCI) Drive** (중화학공업화 정책), announced in January 1973, massively accelerated Ulsan's development. Park identified six priority industries: steel, non-ferrous metals, machinery, shipbuilding, electronics, and chemicals. Ulsan became the nucleus for shipbuilding, petrochemicals, and automotive production. Land was expropriated, workers recruited from rural provinces, and entire industrial complexes built within years. By 1980, Ulsan had been transformed from a small coastal town of approximately 100,000 people (1960) into an industrial city of over 500,000.

## The Petrochemical Industry

### Ulsan Petrochemical Complex

The **Ulsan–Onsan Petrochemical Complex** is Asia's largest integrated refinery-and-chemicals zone by capacity. Established in the 1960s and massively expanded in the 1970s, it spans the Onsan industrial zone south of central Ulsan across approximately 70 square kilometers of reclaimed coastline. The complex processes crude oil imported from the Middle East and Southeast Asia into gasoline, diesel, naphtha, ethylene, propylene, and a vast array of downstream chemicals.

Key producers include:

- **SK Innovation / SK Energy** (formerly Korea Oil Corporation, then Yukong) — Korea's largest refiner, with crude distillation capacity of approximately 840,000 barrels per day across its Ulsan and Incheon facilities. SK Energy's Ulsan complex is the single largest refinery site in Korea, producing fuels, lubricants, and petrochemical feedstocks. The company also operates extensive cracking and aromatics plants at the site.

- **S-Oil** (에쓰-오일) — a joint venture originally between Saudi Aramco and Ssangyong Oil (later Hanjin), now majority-owned by Saudi Aramco, with refining capacity of approximately 669,000 barrels per day at its Onsan facility. S-Oil's integration of downstream chemicals (residue upgrading complex) represents a multi-billion-dollar investment in high-value petrochemicals.

- **LG Chem** — produces polyethylene, polypropylene, acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene (ABS) resin, and engineering plastics at the Ulsan complex for Korea's electronics and automotive industries.

- **Lotte Chemical** — produces ethylene oxide, polyols, and specialty chemicals.

The complex produces approximately 30% of Korea's total petrochemical output. Petrochemical exports from the Ulsan region—primarily to China, Southeast Asia, and the United States—have constituted a significant fraction of Korea's chemical trade balance.

### Industry Pressures

Since approximately 2015, the petrochemical sector has faced compounding pressures: Middle Eastern producers (Saudi Arabia, UAE) with structural feedstock-cost advantages; Chinese overcapacity adding competitive pricing pressure; tightening Korean environmental regulations; and the accelerating energy transition reducing long-term demand projections for fossil-fuel-based chemicals. Industry players have responded with investments in high-margin specialty chemicals, circular-economy plastics recycling, and hydrogen production.

## Automotive Manufacturing: Hyundai Motor Company

### The Ulsan Automotive Complex

**Hyundai Motor Company** (현대자동차) established its first manufacturing facility in Ulsan in 1967–1968, initially producing cars under license from Ford. The Ulsan complex has since grown into one of the world's largest single-site automotive manufacturing facilities, with five production plants occupying approximately 5 square kilometers in Buk-gu:

- **Plant 1** (1968) — produces Avante (Elantra), Grandeur (Azera), and commercial vehicles; repeatedly modernized. The original 1968 facility was retooled for global model production in the 1990s.
- **Plant 2** (1993) — produces Tucson SUV and Kona crossover for export markets.
- **Plant 3** (1999) — produces Sonata sedan and Porter commercial truck.
- **Plant 4** (2006) — dedicated export facility producing Genesis models and flagship sedans.
- **Plant 5** (2008) — produces Ioniq EV and hybrid models; now the primary electrification production hub.

The Ulsan plants collectively employ approximately 34,000 direct workers (as of 2024), with annual combined vehicle capacity of approximately 1.4 million units. In peak years (2015–2018), Hyundai's Ulsan complex produced over 1.5 million vehicles annually, supplying domestic and international markets including North America, Europe, and the Middle East.

### Electrification Investment

As Hyundai Group pivots to electric and hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles, Ulsan's facilities are undergoing transformation. The company announced in 2023 a ₩24 trillion (approximately $18 billion) investment in Ulsan through 2032 to build dedicated EV production lines, a battery assembly plant, and a hydrogen fuel-cell manufacturing hub. The Ioniq 5 and Ioniq 6 EVs are produced in Ulsan; the Ioniq 9 is scheduled for Ulsan production from 2025.

## Shipbuilding: Hyundai Heavy Industries

**Hyundai Heavy Industries** (현대중공업, HHI) is headquartered in Ulsan's Dong-gu district and operates the world's largest single-site shipyard by production capacity. Founded in 1972 by Hyundai Group founder Chung Ju-yung, who secured an order for two supertankers from Greek shipping magnate Livanos before the shipyard was even built, HHI became emblematic of Korean developmental-state industrialization.

HHI's Ulsan shipyard spans approximately 2.7 square kilometers of coastline with dry docks capable of constructing the largest vessels afloat: very large crude carriers (VLCCs), liquefied natural gas (LNG) carriers, container ships, and naval vessels. At its peak in the early 2010s, the yard launched a new vessel every four days. The yard has constructed over 2,600 vessels since 1972.

HHI reorganized as HD Hyundai in 2021 (creating a holding company structure), with the shipbuilding subsidiary renamed HD Hyundai Heavy Industries. Despite industry-wide downturns from 2015–2021, HHI/HD HHI consistently ranked among the top two global shipbuilders by order book. The recovery of LNG carrier demand from 2022 onward—driven by European energy diversification following Russia's Ukraine invasion—has fueled a new orderbook boom at the Ulsan yards.

## Labor History

Ulsan was a central site of Korea's democratization-era labor movement. The **Great Workers' Struggle of 1987** (노동자대투쟁) saw massive strikes erupt across Korea's industrial zones in July–September 1987, immediately following the June Democracy Movement that pressured President Chun Doo-hwan to accept direct presidential elections. In Ulsan, Hyundai Motor and Hyundai Heavy Industries workers launched simultaneous strikes demanding wage increases, union recognition, and improved working conditions. The strikes spread to virtually every Hyundai affiliate in Ulsan and to other manufacturers.

The 1987 struggles fundamentally altered Korea's labor landscape: independent democratic unions replaced government-controlled enterprise unions, wages rose sharply, and collective bargaining became institutionalized. Ulsan's union movement—particularly the **Hyundai Motor Union** and **Korean Metal Workers' Union** (금속노조) affiliates—has remained one of Korea's most influential and contentious, with periodic large-scale strikes (1989, 1995, 2003, 2010, 2019) shaping national labor policy.

## Environmental History: From Pollution to Restoration

Ulsan's rapid industrialization produced severe environmental degradation. By the 1980s, the city was known as one of Korea's most polluted urban areas:

- **Taehwa River** — industrial discharge from upstream chemical plants reduced the river to near-biological death by the 1990s. Fish populations collapsed; residents avoided the riverbanks due to chemical odor.
- **Air quality** — VOC and particulate emissions from petrochemical plants contributed to elevated respiratory disease rates and chronic odor complaints. Ulsan's PM10 and PM2.5 levels routinely exceeded Korean standards.
- **Soil and groundwater** — decades of petrochemical production created contamination plumes around the Onsan complex.

### The Taehwa River Restoration

Beginning in the early 2000s, Ulsan implemented a comprehensive watershed restoration program. Industrial discharges were regulated, wastewater treatment capacity expanded, and riverbank habitats replanted with native species. By 2012, the results were remarkable: **Eurasian otters** (천연기념물, Natural Monument No. 330), absent from the river for decades, returned naturally to the Taehwa River—used by the government as a flagship indicator of water quality recovery. By 2018, over 1,200 species of plants and animals had been documented in the Taehwa River ecological corridor, and the riverfront was converted into a 4.5-kilometer urban park.

The Taehwa River restoration is now recognized as one of Asia's most successful urban river rehabilitation projects and has been featured in international environmental policy discussions as a model for reconciling industrial cities with ecological recovery.

## Demographics

Ulsan's population trajectory reflects the arc of Korean industrialization:

| Year | Population |
|------|-----------|
| 1960 | ~100,000 |
| 1980 | ~551,000 |
| 1990 | ~683,000 |
| 2000 | ~1,012,000 |
| 2005 | ~1,050,000 |
| 2020 | ~1,143,000 |
| 2024 (est.) | ~1,105,000 |

The recent decline from a 2015 peak of approximately 1,166,000 reflects outmigration, particularly of younger workers, and industry restructuring. Male workers historically outnumber female in Ulsan's industrial workforce, but that ratio has shifted as the city diversifies.

The population is relatively homogeneous (over 97% ethnic Korean), though foreign workers—primarily from Vietnam, Nepal, and China—have increased in the manufacturing sector.

## Education and Research

- **UNIST** (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology, 울산과학기술원) — established 2009 as a government-funded research university modeled on KAIST and POSTECH. UNIST has rapidly achieved international rankings, particularly in energy science, materials, and environmental engineering. As of 2024, it ranks among the top 100 globally in several subject areas.
- **University of Ulsan** (울산대학교) — a comprehensive private university established 1970, affiliated with Hyundai Group; known for engineering and medical programs.
- **Ulsan College** and several vocational colleges serve the industrial workforce.

## Transportation

- **KTX Ulsan Station** — opened November 1, 2010, as a stop on the Gyeongbu High-Speed Line connecting Seoul (approximately 2 hours) and Busan (20 minutes). The station is located in Ulju-gun, approximately 15 kilometers from the city center, connected by shuttle bus and local rail.
- **Ulsan Port** — a dedicated industrial port handling petroleum tankers, automotive exports, and general cargo. Approximately 95 million tons of cargo passes through annually, ranking it among Korea's top five ports by tonnage. The automotive terminal exports approximately 400,000 Hyundai vehicles per year.
- **Expressways** — the Gyeongbu Expressway (Seoul–Busan) and Ulsan Expressway connect the city to the national road network. The Ulsan–Pohang Expressway provides a coastal route northward.
- **Air access** — Ulsan Airport (domestic flights) and Gimhae International Airport in Busan (80 km south) serve international access.

## Economic Outlook and Diversification

Ulsan's economy remains highly concentrated in two volatile sectors. Both face structural transitions:

- **Petrochemicals** — the global energy transition, Chinese overcapacity, and tightening regulations on fossil-fuel-derived plastics require diversification into high-performance specialty chemicals, biodegradable materials, and hydrogen production.
- **Automotive** — the shift to EVs requires retooling body and assembly plants, restructuring supply chains away from internal-combustion-engine components, and building battery expertise.

The Ulsan city government has launched a **Hydrogen Valley** initiative, leveraging existing industrial infrastructure to develop hydrogen production (from reforming and eventually electrolysis), distribution, and fuel-cell vehicle applications. Hyundai Motor's fuel-cell buses and trucks are supplied from Ulsan production lines.

The government also supports **smart manufacturing** investment—digitalization, automation, and advanced robotics integration in existing plants—to maintain competitiveness.
