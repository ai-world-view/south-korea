# Gyeongju

Gyeongju (경주), located in North Gyeongsang Province in the southeastern Korean peninsula, is the ancient capital of the kingdom of Silla (57 BCE–935 CE) and one of Asia's most historically significant archaeological and cultural centers. Known colloquially as "the museum without walls," Gyeongju is home to an extraordinary concentration of Buddhist temples, stone monuments, royal tumuli, and pre-modern architecture spanning nearly a millennium of Korean civilization. Today a UNESCO World Heritage Site and the largest repository of Unified Silla's material culture, Gyeongju represents the apex of Korean Three Kingdoms civilization and remains central to the national identity.

## Geography and Location

Gyeongju is situated in the Yeongnam region (영남), roughly 60 km south of Daegu and approximately 370 km southeast of Seoul. The city lies in a river valley formed by the Hyeongsan River (*Hyeongsan-gang*), which flows eastward to the Sea of Japan (East Sea). The surrounding landscape is characterized by low mountains, terraced agricultural valleys, and forested hills typical of southeastern Korea. The city covers approximately 1,315 square kilometers, with the historic core concentrated in its downtown district and extending outward to archaeological zones and temple complexes. As of the 2020 census, Gyeongju's population was approximately 257,000, down from a post-war peak as continued urbanization draws residents to Daegu, Busan, and Seoul.

Gyeongju sits on major transportation routes; the Gyeongbu Line (경부선) connects it via rail to Seoul and Busan, and the Gyeongbu Expressway (경부고속도로) passes nearby. Its position on the peninsula's southeast has historically made it a cultural crossroads where Chinese, Central Asian, and indigenous Korean influences converged.

## Early History and Silla Unification

Gyeongju emerged as the capital of the kingdom of Silla, one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea (alongside Goguryeo and Baekje), founded traditionally in 57 BCE according to the *Samguk Sagi* (삼국사기, *History of the Three Kingdoms*). The city was known in antiquity as Seorabeol (서라벌) and also as Geumseong (금성, "gold city"). Under Silla's rulers, it grew into a major urban center and political seat of power.

The turning point in Gyeongju's importance came with Silla's unification of the Korean peninsula in 668 CE under King Munmu (r. 661–681) and his successor Sinmun (r. 681–692), following decades of military campaigns. This marked the establishment of Unified Silla (668–935), a period of unprecedented political stability and cultural flourishing. Gyeongju became the center of one of medieval East Asia's most advanced civilizations, a Buddhist capital whose sophistication drew comparison with Tang China.

## Peak of Silla Civilization

During Unified Silla, Gyeongju was a thriving metropolis; historical sources such as the *Samguk Yusa* record on the order of 170,000 households at its height, and population estimates that approach a million make it one of the largest cities of its era. The city was laid out in an orderly grid pattern influenced by Chinese urban planning (particularly Chang'an, present-day Xi'an), with wide avenues, administrative districts, and distinct quarters for merchants, artisans, and the nobility.

Gyeongju was the center of Korean Buddhism during this period. The city hosted numerous Buddhist temples—at its peak, the area contained over 200 temples—and became a pilgrimage destination for Buddhists across East Asia. The city's intellectual and artistic culture reached extraordinary heights: poets composed works in Sino-Korean and native Korean poetic forms; scholars studied Confucian classics and Buddhist philosophy; and craftspeople produced some of the world's finest ceramics, metalwork, and stone sculpture. The elite *hwarang* (화랑, "flower youths"), a military and cultural organization of Silla aristocratic youth, trained in Gyeongju and became models of chivalry and learning.

## Archaeological Heritage and Material Culture

Gyeongju's historical significance today rests largely on its unparalleled archaeological record. The city preserves numerous classes of monuments:

**Royal Tumuli (Burial Mounds)**: Scattered throughout Gyeongju are dozens of large earthen tumuli that are the burial sites of Silla kings and queens. These mounds, ranging from a few meters to over 80 meters in diameter and in the largest cases more than 20 meters in height, are concentrated in several zones, particularly the *Daereung-won* (대릉원, "Great Tumuli Park") in the city center. The largest, such as Hwangnam-daechong (a twin-mound royal tomb) and Cheonma-chong (the "Heavenly Horse Tomb"), stand as striking landscape features. Unlike Chinese imperial tombs with surface structures, Silla tumuli are characterized by their simple mound form over a stone-mounded wooden chamber, a construction that sealed and preserved their grave goods. These sites have yielded invaluable archaeological data including pottery, metalwork, jewelry, weapons, and organic remains.

**Buddhist Temples and Pagodas**: Surviving Buddhist temples include the historically significant **Bulguk-sa** (불국사, "Temple of Buddha's Land"), begun in 751 CE under King Gyeongdeok and completed in 774, which exemplifies Silla Buddhist architecture with its multi-tiered stone pagodas, ornate wooden halls, and integration with the natural landscape. Nearby stands the **Seokguram** (석굴암, "Stone Grotto Hermitage"), an artificial stone cave shrine carved into a mountainside, housing a monumental seated stone Buddha sculpture. Both are UNESCO World Heritage Sites and masterpieces of East Asian Buddhist art. The wooded slopes of Namsan (남산), immediately south of the city, preserve scores of Buddhist rock carvings, pagodas, and hermitage sites in an open-air setting.

**Stone Sculpture and Monuments**: Gyeongju contains an exceptional collection of stone Buddhist and secular sculpture, including stone lanterns, steles, and Buddhist statuary. The most famous is the *Dabotap* (다보탑, "Many-Treasure Pagoda") and *Seoktap* (석탑, "Stone Pagoda") at Bulguk-sa. Stone seals, official monuments, and commemorative steles with Sino-Korean inscriptions document administrative and religious history.

**Cheomseongdae (첨성대) — The Star Observatory**: One of Gyeongju's most iconic surviving structures, Cheomseongdae is a 9.4-meter stone tower constructed circa **647 CE** under the reign of Queen Seondeok (r. 632–647). It is widely recognized as the oldest extant astronomical observatory in Asia. The cylindrical granite structure—composed of 362 granite blocks arranged in 27 courses—was likely used to observe the position of stars and planets and to calibrate the agricultural lunar calendar. The number of blocks corresponds symbolically to the number of days in a lunar year; the structure aligns with astronomical directions. Cheomseongdae stands in the city center and is designated National Treasure No. 31 of South Korea.

**Gold Crowns and Royal Jewellery**: Excavations of Gyeongju's tumuli have recovered an extraordinary collection of gold and gilt-bronze artifacts, particularly the famous Silla gold crowns (*geum-gwan*, 금관). The **Geumgwan-chong** (Gold Crown Tomb), excavated in 1921, yielded a spectacular gold crown featuring upright tree-branch and antler ornaments decorated with comma-shaped jade pendants (*gogok*, 곡옥) and hanging gold discs—motifs with parallels in Siberian and Central Asian nomadic traditions. Five gold crowns have been identified from Gyeongju tumuli, all now National Treasures. The crowns are among the most recognizable symbols of Korean antiquity and are displayed at the National Gyeongju Museum and the National Museum of Korea in Seoul.

**The Emille Bell (Seongdeok Daewang Sinjong, 성덕대왕신종)**: Completed in **771 CE** under King Hyegong and dedicated to King Seongdeok, this massive bronze bell weighs approximately 18.9 tonnes and stands 3.75 meters tall, making it the largest surviving bell in Korea and one of the finest examples of East Asian bell-casting. The bell is renowned for its exceptionally deep, resonant tone (produced by a unique internal acoustic structure) and for its elaborate bas-relief surface decorations depicting flying apsaras (heavenly beings) and floral motifs. A popular legend claims the bell's haunting tone sounds like a child calling *"eomille"* (mother), giving the bell its colloquial name. The bell is housed at the National Gyeongju Museum, where it is occasionally rung on ceremonial occasions.

**Pottery and Decorative Arts**: Silla pottery, particularly bowls, ritual vessels, and roof-tile ornaments, reveals sophisticated ceramic technology and aesthetic sensibility. Silla stoneware (*togi*, 토기)—characterized by grey-blue high-fired surfaces and distinctive foot-ring shapes—represents a unique ceramic tradition distinct from Chinese and Japanese wares. Metalwork including bronze bells, gilt-bronze Buddhas, and gold earrings demonstrates mastery of metallurgy. These artifacts populate museums and archaeological collections worldwide.

**Residential and Civic Remains**: Archaeological excavations have uncovered foundations of elite residences, administrative buildings, and workshops, revealing the material life and urban organization of Silla society.

## Decline and Later History

With Silla's collapse in 935 CE and the rise of the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), Gyeongju's importance as the political capital ended. However, the city remained a significant religious and cultural center, particularly as a pilgrimage site for Buddhism. During the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910), when Neo-Confucianism replaced Buddhism as the state ideology, Gyeongju's temples declined, though some were maintained as historical monuments.

The Japanese colonial period (1910–1945) saw increased archaeological interest in Gyeongju as both Japanese scholars and Korean intellectuals conducted excavations and documentation. Korean independence and the subsequent South Korean state led to systematic preservation efforts and the recognition of Gyeongju's national importance; the area around the city was designated Gyeongju National Park in 1968, the only such park in Korea centered on cultural rather than natural heritage. In 2000, Gyeongju's key monuments were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site under the name "Gyeongju Historic Areas."

## Contemporary Significance and Tourism

The UNESCO inscription in **December 2000** covered five zones: the Namsan Belt (an outdoor museum of Buddhist carvings and hermitages), the Wolseong Belt (the palace precinct), the Daereung-won (royal tumulus park), the Hwangnyong-sa Belt (site of the great 7th-century temple), and the Sanseong Belt (mountain fortress). The Bulguk-sa Temple and Seokguram Grotto had been inscribed separately as early as **1995**, making Gyeongju one of the most UNESCO-recognized sites in Korea.

Today, Gyeongju is South Korea's premier historical and archaeological tourist destination, attracting over 7 million visitors annually. The city's economy has largely shifted from agriculture to cultural heritage and tourism. The downtown core preserves traditional architecture, while the surrounding archaeological zones are carefully managed as heritage parks with museums, information centers, and reconstruction projects.

Major cultural institutions include the **National Gyeongju Museum** (*Gyeongju Kungnip Bangmul-gwan*), which houses one of Korea's most comprehensive collections of Silla artifacts and provides scholarly documentation of the period. Nearby is the **Gyeongju World Culture Expo** site and various smaller museums dedicated to specific aspects of Silla culture.

The city has developed heritage tourism infrastructure including walking trails connecting major monuments, heritage performance spaces showcasing traditional music and dance, and specialized museums. The annual **Gyeongju World Culture Expo** (held periodically) and various cultural festivals celebrate the city's historical and artistic traditions.

## Significance in Korean Identity and International Recognition

Gyeongju holds profound symbolic meaning in Korean national consciousness. The civilization that flourished there—Unified Silla—is considered the golden age of pre-modern Korean culture and the foundation of Korean civilization. The sophistication of Silla society, its Buddhist achievements, and its political stability under a unified Korean state provide a source of national pride and a touchstone for discussions of Korean cultural identity and continuity.

Internationally, Gyeongju and its monuments contribute to the global understanding of East Asian civilization. Silla's interactions with Tang China, Buddhism's transmission across Asia, and the technical achievements of medieval Korean artisans have attracted scholarly attention from archaeologists, art historians, and religious studies specialists worldwide.

The inscriptions on Gyeongju's monuments and artifacts, written in Middle Korean using Chinese characters, have been instrumental in reconstructing the history of the Korean language and the development of Korean writing systems—a legacy that connects Gyeongju to the later creation of Hangul.

## Modern Preservation Challenges

Balancing heritage protection with urban development presents ongoing difficulties. Strict construction regulations within the historic core (no buildings above three stories in designated zones, mandatory archaeological impact assessments before any ground disturbance) limit economic growth and have contributed to population decline. A 2016 5.8-magnitude earthquake—the strongest recorded in modern South Korea—struck close to Gyeongju, damaging tile roofs and stone structures and raising questions about the seismic vulnerability of ancient masonry monuments. The South Korean Cultural Heritage Administration subsequently commissioned structural assessments of all major stone monuments.

Tourism pressure also creates conservation tension: the millions of annual visitors erode paths near tumuli and increase humidity inside structures. Authorities have restricted direct access to the interiors of some tumuli and installed climate monitoring at Seokguram to protect the main Buddha sculpture, which had suffered from moisture damage in earlier decades.

[Chuseok](../culture-society/chuseok.md), Korea's autumn harvest holiday, brings a significant surge of domestic visitors to Gyeongju's Buddhist temples and ancestral sites, making the city one of the busiest heritage tourism destinations in the country during that period.
