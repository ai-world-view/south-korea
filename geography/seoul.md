# Seoul

**Seoul** (서울; officially the **Seoul Special Metropolitan City**) is the capital and largest city of South Korea, situated in the Han River basin in the northwestern part of the peninsula. It is the nation's political, economic, cultural, and educational center — the seat of government, the engine of the Korean economy, and the origin point of the Korean Wave (Hallyu) that has reshaped global popular culture. Within the city boundaries, Seoul's population stands at approximately 9.4 million (2024); the Seoul Capital Area — encompassing Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province — houses roughly 26 million people, about half the entire South Korean population.

## Geography and Physical Setting

Seoul occupies a natural basin formed by the Han River and enclosed by a ring of granite mountains: **Bugaksan** (342 m) to the north, **Naksan** (125 m) to the northeast, **Namsan** (262 m) to the south, and **Inwangsan** (338 m) to the northwest. The Hangang (Han River) bisects the city from east to west before emptying into the Yellow Sea near Incheon. At approximately 605 km², Seoul is compact relative to its population density of roughly 15,500 people per km², making it one of the world's most densely settled major cities.

The city spans both banks of the Han River, with the areas north of the river (Gangbuk) traditionally housing older districts, royal palaces, and government institutions, and the southern bank (Gangnam) developed primarily after the 1960s as a planned modern extension. Thirty-one bridges cross the Han River within Seoul, a figure that has grown steadily since the first permanent modern bridge — Hangang Railway Bridge — was completed in 1900.

Seoul lies at approximately 37.5° N latitude, comparable to Athens, Greece, and Washington, D.C. The climate is **humid continental** with four distinct seasons: winters are cold (January average −2 °C / 28 °F) and dry due to Siberian high-pressure systems; summers are hot and humid (August average 26 °C / 79 °F) with a monsoon season (jangma) from late June through mid-July; spring and autumn are mild and clear.

## Historical Development

### Pre-Joseon Settlements

Human habitation near the Han River delta dates back more than 2,000 years. The area was contested by the Three Kingdoms of Korea — Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla — with Baekje establishing its first capital, Wiryeseong, near present-day southeastern Seoul around the 1st century BCE. The region later passed between Silla and Goguryeo control and was known under the Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392) as **Namgyeong** (South Capital), one of the dynasty's four administrative capitals.

### Joseon Capital: Hanyang (1392–1910)

In 1392, General Yi Seonggye overthrew the Goryeo Dynasty and founded the **Joseon Dynasty**, establishing a new capital. After briefly considering several sites, he designated the Han River basin location as the permanent capital in **1394**, naming it **Hanyang**. Construction of the royal palace complex **Gyeongbokgung** began immediately and was completed in **1395**; it remained the primary royal residence for most of the Joseon period. The city was enclosed by a fortified wall approximately 18 km in circumference, punctuated by four great gates — the most celebrated of which, **Heunginjimun** (Dongdaemun, Great East Gate, 1396) and **Sungnyemun** (Namdaemun, Great South Gate, 1398), survive today as national treasures. The population of Hanyang grew steadily, reaching perhaps 200,000 by the mid-18th century.

The Joseon capital was laid out according to **confucian urban planning principles**: the palace at the north, a ceremonial boulevard running south, government offices on either side, and the royal shrines — Jongmyo (the royal ancestral shrine, 1395, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site) and Sajikdan (altar to earth and grain) — positioned to the east and west respectively. The Joseon name for the city changed to **Gyeongseong** under Japanese administration.

### Japanese Colonial Period (1910–1945)

Japan's annexation of Korea in **1910** renamed the capital **Keijō** (京城) and transferred administrative control to the **Government-General of Joseon**, which built its headquarters directly in front of Gyeongbokgung Palace, symbolically blocking it from public view. Colonial-era urban redevelopment introduced a Western-influenced grid street pattern in central areas, laid rail lines, and expanded the city's infrastructure under Japanese imperial priorities. The population grew to approximately **700,000** by 1940, reflecting in-migration driven by industrialization and the dismantling of the traditional agrarian economy.

Anti-Japanese sentiment was intense; Seoul was the focal point of the **March 1st Movement** of 1919, when peaceful demonstrations against Japanese rule began at Tapgol Park in the city center and spread nationwide, resulting in approximately 7,500 Korean deaths in Japanese crackdowns.

### Liberation, Division, and the Korean War (1945–1953)

Japan's surrender on **August 15, 1945** — celebrated in South Korea as **Gwangbokjeol** (Liberation Day) — ended 35 years of colonial rule. The city was renamed **Seoul** (a native Korean word meaning "capital") and became capital of the newly established Republic of Korea on **August 15, 1948**. President **Syngman Rhee** declared Seoul the seat of government, and the National Assembly met in the former colonial assembly building.

The [Korean War](../history/korean-war.md) brought catastrophic destruction. North Korean forces captured Seoul on **June 28, 1950** — just three days after their invasion began — and the city changed hands **four times** over the course of the war. UN forces recaptured it on **September 28, 1950** after the Incheon Landing; North Korean and Chinese forces retook it briefly in January 1951; UN forces secured it again in March 1951. By the armistice of July 1953, most of Seoul's buildings had been destroyed, its population had dropped from approximately 1.5 million to under 600,000, and its infrastructure lay in ruins.

### Reconstruction and the Economic Miracle (1953–1988)

Post-war reconstruction began immediately, financed partly by U.S. aid under the **Mutual Defense Treaty of 1953** and by the South Korean government's determination to rebuild. The 1960s brought rapid industrialization under President **Park Chung-hee**, who seized power in a military coup in 1961 and pursued export-led economic development. Seoul expanded dramatically: the southern bank of the Han River was opened for development in the late 1960s, expressways were constructed, and planned districts such as **Yeouido** (Seoul's financial island) and **Gangnam** were built from largely undeveloped land. Seoul's population surpassed **5 million** in 1970 and **8 million** in 1980.

The **1988 Seoul Summer Olympics** (September 17 – October 2, 1988) represented a global coming-out moment for the city and the nation. By then Seoul had become a modern metropolis: the Han River Expressway ran along both banks, the Seoul Metro had been operating since **1974**, and Korea's GDP had grown from under $100 per capita in 1953 to over $4,000. The Olympics were also a political landmark, taking place as South Korea transitioned to full democratic governance following the **June Democracy Movement** of 1987.

## Modern Seoul

### Economy

Seoul is the center of the South Korean economy, which had a nominal GDP of approximately **$1.7 trillion** in 2024, making it the world's 13th largest. Seoul alone accounts for roughly **21% of national GDP**. The city hosts the headquarters of South Korea's largest **chaebol** (family-owned conglomerates): **Samsung Electronics** (Seocho-gu), **Hyundai Motor** (Seoul and Gyeonggi), **LG** (Yeouido), **SK** (Jongno-gu), and **Lotte** (Jung-gu). The city's economy spans advanced manufacturing, semiconductors, shipbuilding services, petrochemicals, biotechnology, and digital services.

**Yeouido** — a flat island in the middle of the Han River — functions as Seoul's financial district, hosting the **Korea Exchange** (KRX), the **National Assembly**, and the headquarters of major banks and securities firms. The **Gangnam** district, particularly the **Teheran-ro** corridor named for Tehran, Iran (with which Seoul has a sister-city relationship), is the center of the technology and venture capital industry.

South Korea ranked **1st globally in internet speed** for multiple consecutive years; Seoul's broadband penetration and 5G deployment are among the world's highest.

### Infrastructure and Transportation

Seoul operates one of the world's largest urban rail systems. The **Seoul Metropolitan Subway** — including lines operated by Seoul Metro, Korail, and affiliated operators — encompasses **23 lines** and over **700 stations**, with approximately **8 million daily riders**. The first line opened on **August 15, 1974** (deliberately coinciding with Liberation Day). Free public Wi-Fi is available throughout the entire subway system.

**Incheon International Airport**, which opened in **March 2001** on reclaimed land west of Incheon, replaced the older Gimpo Airport as the primary international gateway. It has repeatedly ranked among the world's best airports and handles over 70 million passengers annually at its two terminals. A dedicated **Airport Railroad Express (AREX)** direct train connects the airport to Seoul Station in **43 minutes**. Gimpo Airport continues to serve domestic and short-haul international routes.

**KTX** (Korea Train Express) high-speed rail service, launched in **April 2004**, connects Seoul Station to Busan in approximately 2 hours 15 minutes, reducing a 400-km journey by roughly 80% in time. An extension to the Gyeongbu line and the Honam line (to Gwangju and Mokpo) followed in subsequent years.

The **Cheonggyecheon** stream — a 5.8-km urban waterway running through central Seoul — was buried under an elevated expressway in 1968 as part of rapid modernization. In **2003–2005**, Mayor Lee Myung-bak led a celebrated project to demolish the expressway and restore the stream as a public greenway, which now draws millions of visitors annually.

### Presidential Residence and Government

Historically, the **Cheong Wa Dae** (Blue House, literally "house with blue tiles") served as the official presidential residence from 1948 until **May 2022**, when President **Yoon Suk-yeol** relocated the presidential office to the Ministry of National Defense compound in **Yongsan**, opening the former Blue House grounds to the public for the first time in 74 years. The **National Assembly** meets on **Yeouido Island**. The **Constitutional Court** and the **Supreme Court** are located in Jongno and Seocho wards respectively. All 18 government ministries maintain their principal offices in Seoul, though the administrative capital functions of certain agencies were shifted to **Sejong City** — a purpose-built administrative capital — beginning in 2012.

## Culture and Society

### Korean Wave (Hallyu)

Seoul is the production and distribution center of the **Korean Wave (Hallyu)**, the global spread of South Korean popular culture. **K-pop** — characterized by meticulously produced music, synchronized choreography, and highly managed idol groups — is manufactured principally by the **Big Three** entertainment companies based in Seoul: **HYBE** (Gangnam, home of BTS), **SM Entertainment** (Jamsil), and **YG Entertainment** and **JYP Entertainment** (Mapo and Gangnam). The global commercial impact of Hallyu has been estimated at over **$10 billion annually** in export value, including music, film, television, beauty products, and food.

Seoul's **Hongdae** (Hongik University area) neighborhood functions as the birthplace of South Korean indie music and street performance culture, distinguishing itself from the more corporate entertainment zones of Gangnam. The area hosts hundreds of small live music venues, independent galleries, and creative studios.

### Heritage and Museums

The city's most significant historical sites include:

- **Gyeongbokgung Palace** (1395; rebuilt 1867 after destruction by Japanese forces in the Imjin War of 1592–1598): The largest of Seoul's five grand palaces; houses the **National Folk Museum** and the **National Palace Museum** within its grounds.
- **Changdeokgung Palace and Secret Garden** (1405): A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1997, renowned for its integration of architecture with natural landscape.
- **Jongmyo Shrine** (1395): UNESCO World Heritage Site and venue for the biennial Jongmyo Jerye (royal ancestral rites), the longest continuously performed Confucian rite in the world.
- **Bukchon Hanok Village**: A preserved neighborhood of traditional *hanok* tile-roofed houses in Jongno, located between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung palaces.
- **National Museum of Korea** (Yongsan, 2005): The largest museum in Korea and sixth-largest in the world by floor area, with over 220,000 artifacts.
- **N Seoul Tower** (Namsan Tower, 1980): A communications and observation tower atop Namsan mountain, visible from much of the city.
- **Lotte World Tower** (2017): At 555 m, the fifth-tallest building in the world at time of completion; the centerpiece of the Lotte World Mall complex in Songpa-gu.

### Cuisine

Seoul is a global culinary destination. Traditional Korean dishes — **bulgogi** (marinated grilled beef), **bibimbap** (mixed rice bowl), **doenjang jjigae** (fermented soybean paste stew), **galbi** (short ribs) — are served alongside the ubiquitous **kimchi**, a fermented vegetable staple with over 200 documented varieties. **Street food** remains integral to Seoul's food culture: *tteokbokki* (spicy rice cakes), *hotteok* (sweet pancakes), *gimbap* (rice rolls), and *sundae* (blood sausage) are sold from stalls throughout markets like **Gwangjang Market** (1905, the oldest indoor market in Korea) and **Namdaemun Market**.

The **Myeongdong** and **Insadong** areas are major concentrations of traditional tea houses and specialty food shops, while **Gangnam** and **Itaewon** host high-end international dining. Seoul ranked among the top 10 cities globally for Michelin-starred restaurants, with 32 starred establishments as of 2024.

## Notable Districts

- **Gangnam-gu** — The affluent southern district (average land prices the highest in South Korea) encompassing Teheran-ro, COEX convention center, and Apgujeong; immortalized globally by Psy's 2012 song "Gangnam Style."
- **Jung-gu** — The historic central ward; contains Namdaemun Gate, Myeongdong, City Hall, and Deoksugung Palace.
- **Jongno-gu** — The royal/cultural center; contains Gyeongbokgung Palace, Bukchon, Insadong, Cheong Wa Dae, and Jogyesa Temple (headquarters of Korean Buddhism's Jogye Order).
- **Mapo-gu** — A mixed residential and creative district on the western bank; includes the World Cup Stadium (built for the **2002 FIFA World Cup**) and Hongdae.
- **Yongsan-gu** — Site of the presidential office (2022–), Yongsan Electronics Market, and the main Itaewon international district; formerly site of the U.S. Army Garrison Yongsan (relocated to Camp Humphreys in Pyeongtaek by 2017).
- **Songpa-gu** — Eastern residential and commercial district; contains Olympic Park (built for the 1988 Games), Lotte World Tower, and Jamsil Baseball Stadium.
- **Eunpyeong-gu** — Northern outer district known for Bukhansan National Park, the only national park within a major city's limits in South Korea.

## Administration

Seoul is one of five **special-status cities** in South Korea, governed as a **Special Metropolitan City** (특별시) independent of any province. The city is divided into **25 autonomous wards (gu)** and approximately 423 legal-status *dong* (neighborhoods). The mayor is directly elected by Seoul citizens; notable mayors include Lee Myung-bak (2002–2006, later President), Oh Se-hoon (2006–2011, 2021–present), and Park Won-soon (2011–2020). The **Seoul Metropolitan Government** manages transportation, urban planning, water and sanitation, public health, and social services, with an annual budget exceeding **40 trillion KRW** (~$30 billion).

## Contemporary Significance

Seoul's transformation from a war-ravaged capital in 1953 to a global megacity is among the most dramatic urban histories of the 20th century. Within a single generation — aided by authoritarian-directed industrialization, a highly educated workforce, and deep cultural emphasis on collective achievement — the city developed the infrastructure, institutions, and cultural confidence to host the Olympics (1988), co-host the FIFA World Cup (2002), and become the center of a global pop-culture movement. Its trajectory illuminates both the extraordinary achievements and the social pressures — extreme housing costs, intense academic competition (the *suneung* college entrance exam), and high work hours — that characterize contemporary South Korean society.
